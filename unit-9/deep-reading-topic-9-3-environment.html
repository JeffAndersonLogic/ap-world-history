Topic 9.3  ·  Deep Reading  ·  AP World History: Modern

The Bill Nobody Was Sent

Every technology in the Topic 9.1 chapter made something cheaper, and some of them were cheap because a cost was left off the invoice. Smoke, runoff, drawn-down groundwater and carbon dioxide are all real costs paid by real people who were not party to the transaction that produced them. This chapter explains why an unbilled cost gets produced in excess, follows four kinds of degradation to the competition they set off, and then separates what the evidence about climate settles from what it does not.

01

A Cost Paid by Someone Who Was Not in the Room

1920 to 1990  ·  Pigou, Hardin, Ostrom

Environmental damage is not usually produced by people who want damage. It is produced because the damage is free to the person producing it and expensive to somebody else, and a cost that does not appear on an invoice does not appear in a decision. Everything in this chapter is a version of that sentence.

The mechanism

The economist Arthur Pigou gave the idea its name in 1920. An externality is a cost or benefit of a transaction that falls on someone outside it. A factory upstream sells cloth to a merchant; the ash and the dye go into the river; the people downstream were not part of the sale and get the ash anyway. The factory's costs are wages, fuel and materials, and the river is free, so the factory produces more smoke and more effluent than it would if the river had a price. Nobody has to be malicious for this to happen. The arithmetic does it.

Two properties make a resource especially vulnerable. It is non-excludable if you cannot keep others from using it, like the atmosphere or an ocean fishery, and it is rival if one person's use reduces what is left, like groundwater or fish. A resource that is both is a common-pool resource, and the incentives around it are brutal: your restraint benefits everyone and costs only you, while your extraction benefits only you and costs everyone.

That is the connection the success criteria ask for between degradation and competition. As a shared resource is drawn down, restraint becomes both more necessary and less rational for any individual user, because the water you leave in the aquifer is water your neighbor pumps. Competition intensifies precisely as the resource declines, which is the opposite of what a naive reading would predict, and it is why water disputes sharpen during droughts rather than resolving.

The argument about whether the commons always collapses

Garrett Hardin's 1968 essay in Science, "The Tragedy of the Commons", is the version most people meet. His picture is a shared pasture on which each herder gains the full value of an extra animal while the cost of overgrazing is spread among all of them, so each adds animals until the pasture is ruined. Hardin concluded that shared resources require either privatization or coercive state regulation.

Elinor Ostrom spent decades demonstrating that this is not what usually happens. Her 1990 book Governing the Commons assembled case studies of irrigation systems, fisheries, forests and pastures managed for centuries by their users through locally made rules: defined membership, monitoring by the users themselves, graduated penalties, and cheap ways to resolve disputes. She received the Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences in 2009, and her finding is that Hardin described an unmanaged commons, which is a different thing from a shared one.

Common mistake to avoid

Do not write that shared resources are always destroyed, and do not cite Hardin as though the question were settled. It is one of the genuinely live disputes in this field, and the evidence favors a middle position: commons that are small enough for users to monitor each other, with clear membership and enforceable local rules, frequently survive for centuries, while commons that are large, open to newcomers and impossible to monitor are the ones that collapse. That distinction is exactly why the atmosphere is the hardest case in this chapter. It has close to eight billion users, no membership boundary, no monitoring by the users, and no penalty anyone can impose.

Use this in your answer
Strongest tool, with its mechanism
The externality. The mechanism is that a producer decides how much to produce by comparing revenue against the costs on its own books, so any cost that lands on someone outside the transaction is invisible to the decision and the damaging activity is carried out in larger quantities than anyone would choose if the bill arrived. Regulation, taxation and property rights are all attempts to put the missing cost back onto the invoice.
Best evidence of exclusion or limit
Naming a cost as an externality does not settle who should pay it or how much it is worth, which is where the political argument in section 04 begins. Valuation of harms spread across centuries and continents is genuinely contested, and reasonable people reach different numbers.
Comparison hook
Against the enclosure of English common land in Topic 5.3: enclosure was Hardin's first solution applied three centuries early, converting a shared resource into private holdings, and it did raise agricultural output. It also ended the customary rights of the households that had depended on the common, which is the part the Topic 5.3 chapter tracks into the factories. Solving a commons problem by privatization transfers the resource as well as protecting it.

Terms to use precisely

Externality
A cost or benefit of a transaction falling on someone outside it, which is invisible to the decision that produces it and therefore overproduced.
Common-pool resource
A resource that is hard to exclude people from and depleted by use, such as an aquifer, a fishery or the atmosphere.
Tragedy of the commons
Hardin's 1968 argument that shared resources are inevitably overused; contested by Ostrom's evidence that managed commons often endure.
Ostrom's design principles
The features Ostrom found in long-lasting commons: defined membership, user monitoring, graduated sanctions and accessible dispute resolution.
Resource competition
Conflict over a declining shared resource, which intensifies as the resource shrinks because restraint by one user simply leaves more for another.
02

Forest, Soil, and the Water Under the Ground

c. 1930 to the 2020s  ·  Dust Bowl to the Nile dispute

Deforestation, desertification and freshwater depletion look like three problems and are largely one: a renewable resource used faster than it renews, with the shortfall paid by people downstream, downwind or later. The cases below are chosen because each one produced a documented political conflict, which is the second half of the success criterion.

Deforestation and the soil it holds

Tropical forest clearance accelerated sharply in the second half of the twentieth century, driven by cattle ranching, soy and oil palm cultivation, logging and road building, and concentrated in the Amazon basin, the Congo basin and Southeast Asia. The immediate causes are usually economic and legal at once: land is cleared because cleared land has an owner and a market value while standing forest often has neither, which is the externality of section 01 written into property law.

The effects run in three directions. Biodiversity falls, and tropical forests hold a disproportionate share of the world's species. Carbon stored in trees and soil is released, so deforestation is a source of greenhouse gases as well as a loss of the sink that absorbs them. And the people already living there, frequently indigenous communities with customary rather than documented tenure, lose the basis of their livelihood, which is why forest conflict in Brazil, Indonesia and the Congo has repeatedly become violent.

Desertification is the related process on drier land: the loss of soil productivity in arid and semi-arid regions through overgrazing, over-cultivation, deforestation and poorly managed irrigation, after which wind and water erosion remove the topsoil. The American Dust Bowl of the 1930s is the textbook case and a useful one, because it shows the mechanism working in a wealthy country: plowing grassland whose root systems had held the soil, followed by drought, produced dust storms that stripped farms across the southern Plains. The Sahel droughts of the late 1960s and 1970s and again in the 1980s did the same across a region with far less capacity to absorb it, killing hundreds of thousands of people and displacing millions, and prompting a United Nations convention on desertification in 1994.

Water, which is where the conflicts are

Freshwater is the resource where competition is most visible, because it is finite, essential, and it flows across borders. Three cases carry the range.

The Aral Sea is the clearest single image of environmental degradation in this unit. From the 1960s Soviet planners diverted the Amu Darya and Syr Darya rivers to irrigate cotton in Central Asia, and the inland sea they fed lost most of its volume over the following decades, with the eastern basin drying out entirely by the 2010s. The fishing industry ended, the exposed seabed produced salt and pesticide dust storms, and the local climate changed. This was not an accident or an unknown risk; planners expected the sea to shrink and judged the cotton worth it. It is a decision, and it should be taught as one.

Groundwater is the quieter version. Aquifers such as the High Plains system under the American Great Plains, the aquifers of the North China Plain, and those under the Indian Punjab have been pumped faster than they recharge since the mid-twentieth century, in each case using the electric or diesel pumps that the Topic 9.1 chapter's cheap energy made possible. Groundwater depletion is politically difficult precisely because it is invisible: nobody sees a falling water table, and each farmer's pump is individually rational.

Rivers that cross borders turn scarcity into diplomacy. The Colorado River in the United States was allocated among states under a compact of 1922 that assumed more water than the river reliably carries, and the federal government declared its first shortage on the system in 2021. On the Nile, Ethiopia began building the Grand Ethiopian Renaissance Dam in 2011 and began filling it from 2020, over objections from Egypt, which depends on the Nile for nearly all its water and had relied on colonial-era agreements that gave it a dominant share. As of the early 2020s the dispute had produced years of negotiation and sharp rhetoric without a comprehensive agreement.

Common mistake to avoid

Do not write that scarcity causes war. It is a popular claim and the evidence does not support it as a general rule. Systematic studies of transboundary rivers find that cooperation, in the form of treaties, joint commissions and data-sharing, is far more common than armed conflict, and that most water violence is local rather than between states. The defensible version is narrower and still worth writing: resource scarcity raises the political stakes of an existing relationship, so where states already distrust each other it hardens the dispute, and where institutions exist it usually produces a negotiation. Which of those happens depends on the politics, which is the point the Topic 9.8 chapter makes about institutions generally.

Use this in your answer
Strongest tool, with its mechanism
Renewable resources used past their renewal rate. The mechanism is that a forest, a soil or an aquifer replenishes at a fixed natural rate, and any extraction faster than that rate draws down a stock rather than harvesting a flow, which looks identical to sustainable use right up to the point the stock runs out. That delay is why the political response almost always arrives after the damage: nothing in the short run distinguishes the two.
Best evidence of exclusion or limit
The pace and the responsibility differ enormously by case. Aral Sea depletion was a deliberate central-planning decision, Amazon clearance is millions of separate decisions inside a legal regime that rewards clearing, and Punjab groundwater loss followed from a development program that succeeded at what it was designed to do.
Comparison hook
Against Mesopotamian salinization in Foundations 1: irrigation without adequate drainage concentrated salt in the soil and forced a shift from wheat to barley thousands of years ago. The mechanism is identical to the Aral basin. What changed is scale and speed, which is the honest way to say that the twentieth century is a difference of degree in this respect and not a difference in kind.

Terms to use precisely

Deforestation
Clearance of forest for ranching, cropland, plantation and timber, which releases stored carbon, reduces biodiversity and displaces communities holding customary tenure.
Desertification
Loss of soil productivity in arid and semi-arid land through overgrazing, over-cultivation and erosion, the subject of a UN convention from 1994.
Aquifer depletion
Groundwater pumped faster than it recharges, invisible to users and individually rational for each of them, which is what makes it hard to regulate.
Transboundary river
A river crossing state borders, where one state's use is another's loss, which produces treaties far more often than it produces war.
Aral Sea
The inland sea drained from the 1960s by Soviet irrigation diversions for cotton, a deliberate trade of an ecosystem for a crop.
03

Air, and How the Measurements Were Made

1856 to 1987  ·  Foote and Tyndall to the Montreal Protocol

Air pollution is the externality at its purest, because nobody can be excluded from an atmosphere and nobody can be billed for it. This section follows air from the local problem that was solved to the global problem that was not, and it spends most of its length on how we know, because the evidence is the part of this topic students are least equipped to defend.

Smoke you can see

In December 1952 a temperature inversion trapped coal smoke and sulfur over London for four days. Visibility fell to a few meters, transport stopped, and mortality rose sharply. Contemporary estimates put the excess deaths at around four thousand; later analyses examining the following months put the figure considerably higher, in the region of twelve thousand. The Great Smog produced Britain's Clean Air Act of 1956, which restricted smoke emissions and created smokeless zones, and the London fogs of the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries did not recur.

The lesson is that visible, local, attributable pollution is politically tractable. People could see it, it killed on a timescale of days, the source was obvious, and the cost of the fix fell in the same jurisdiction as the benefit. Every one of those conditions is missing for carbon dioxide, and that is why the two problems have such different histories.

The intermediate case is acid rain. Sulfur dioxide from power stations in Britain and central Europe fell as acidified precipitation on Scandinavian lakes and forests hundreds of kilometers away, which made it the first air pollution problem no state could solve inside its own borders at continental scale. Cross-border smoke disputes were older, and the United States and Canada had already arbitrated one over a smelter at Trail through the 1930s, but that was two governments and a single chimney; acid rain was dozens of governments and every power station they had. It produced a European convention on long-range transboundary air pollution in 1979, and in the United States the Clean Air Act amendments of 1990 created a market in sulfur dioxide permits that cut emissions faster and more cheaply than its designers had projected. That is worth remembering as evidence that pricing an externality can work, and as a reminder of how much easier it was: a few hundred large power stations, one pollutant, one country.

The ozone case, which is the control experiment

Mario Molina and F. Sherwood Rowland published in 1974 the finding that chlorofluorocarbons, chemically inert gases used in refrigeration and aerosols, would drift into the stratosphere and destroy ozone, which absorbs ultraviolet radiation. In 1985 researchers from the British Antarctic Survey reported a severe seasonal depletion over Antarctica, larger than any model had predicted. A framework convention followed the same year and the Montreal Protocol in 1987, which phased out the chemicals on a fixed schedule with financial assistance for developing countries. It has been ratified by every member state of the United Nations, and the ozone layer has been assessed as recovering.

Four features made it work, and naming them is the most efficient way to explain the difficulty of climate policy. The offending chemicals were made by a small number of firms in a small number of countries, so the number of parties who had to agree was small. Substitutes existed and were commercialized quickly. The harm, skin cancer and cataracts from ultraviolet exposure, was concrete, personal and near-term. And the cost of the fix was modest relative to the economies bearing it.

How we know about carbon dioxide

The physics is nineteenth century, which surprises most students and is worth stating plainly. Eunice Newton Foote reported in 1856 that a jar of carbon dioxide in sunlight warmed more than a jar of ordinary air. John Tyndall measured in 1859 that carbon dioxide and water vapor absorb infrared radiation while nitrogen and oxygen do not, which is the actual mechanism. Svante Arrhenius calculated in 1896 roughly how much the surface would warm if atmospheric carbon dioxide doubled, and arrived at about five degrees, which is high against the modern best estimate of around three but the right sign and the right order of magnitude from arithmetic done by hand. None of this was controversial, and none of it was framed as a warning; Arrhenius thought a warmer Sweden would be an improvement.

How we know: the Keeling curve and the ice cores

Charles David Keeling began continuous measurement of atmospheric carbon dioxide at Mauna Loa in Hawaii in 1958, deliberately far from cities and industry. The resulting record, the Keeling curve, shows an annual sawtooth as Northern Hemisphere vegetation grows and dies back, riding on a rising trend that has continued every year since. It is the single most reproducible measurement in this topic: the instruments are calibrated against known standards, and independent stations from Alaska to the South Pole record the same trend. Concentrations passed four hundred parts per million in the 2010s, against a pre-industrial level of roughly two hundred eighty. The longer record comes from ice cores drilled in Antarctica and Greenland, which trap air bubbles in dated annual layers and extend the record back hundreds of thousands of years, and the isotopic signature of the added carbon identifies it as coming from fossil sources rather than from volcanoes or the ocean. Two independent methods, one instrumental and one geological, agreeing on the same curve, is a much stronger form of evidence than either one alone.

The Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change was created in 1988 by two United Nations bodies. It runs no experiments of its own; it assesses the published literature and produces reports whose summaries are negotiated line by line with government delegations, which makes it a conservative body by construction, since every participating government including major fossil-fuel producers must accept the wording. In its sixth assessment, published from 2021, the panel's headline finding was that human influence on the warming of the atmosphere, ocean and land is unequivocal, and it assessed warming at roughly 1.1 degrees Celsius above the 1850 to 1900 baseline for the decade 2011 to 2020.

Use this in your answer
Strongest tool, with its mechanism
Attributability as the political variable. The mechanism is that a pollution problem gets solved when the harm is visible, soon, and traceable to a source inside the jurisdiction that would bear the cost of stopping it. London smog met all three and was fixed in four years; acid rain met two and took a treaty; carbon dioxide meets none, because it is invisible, its effects are delayed by decades, and it mixes globally so no state’s emissions are its own problem.
Best evidence of exclusion or limit
The features that made Montreal easy are not a template. A treaty that worked on a few dozen firms making refrigerant does not scale to an economy-wide input, and citing ozone as proof that climate agreements can work skips the four conditions that made the difference.
Comparison hook
Against Manchester in Topic 5.3 and Topic 5.9: the mechanism is unchanged from 1850 to 1952, coal smoke trapped over a city killing the people who breathe it, and so is the eventual response, regulation once the harm became undeniable and politically expensive. What changed by the twentieth century is the scale at which the same logic had to operate, and that is exactly where it stopped working.

Terms to use precisely

Great Smog
The London coal-smoke episode of December 1952, whose excess mortality prompted the Clean Air Act of 1956 and effectively ended the city's killing fogs.
Montreal Protocol
The 1987 treaty phasing out ozone-depleting chemicals, universally ratified and effective, and the clearest case of an environmental agreement that worked.
Keeling curve
The continuous record of atmospheric carbon dioxide begun at Mauna Loa in 1958, showing an annual cycle riding on a rising trend.
Ice core
A drilled cylinder of layered polar ice whose trapped air bubbles preserve a dated record of past atmospheric composition over hundreds of thousands of years.
Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change
The body created in 1988 to assess published climate research, whose report summaries are agreed line by line with member governments.
04

What Is Settled, What Is Uncertain, and What Is Political

1992 to 2015  ·  Rio to Paris

The key concept says greenhouse gases and pollutants "contributed to debates about the nature and causes of climate change", and the useful thing a student can do with that sentence is to separate three kinds of question that get argued as though they were one. They have different evidence, different experts and different answers.

Three questions, kept apart

Is the climate warming and are human greenhouse gas emissions the main cause of the recent warming? This is a question about physical evidence, and the assessed scientific literature converges on yes. The mechanism was measured in 1859, the concentrations have been recorded continuously since 1958, the isotopic signature identifies the added carbon as fossil, the warming trend appears in surface stations, ocean heat content, satellite records, sea level and ice extent, and the pattern of warming (the stratosphere cooling while the surface warms) matches what greenhouse forcing predicts and does not match what a brighter sun would produce. Every major national academy of sciences and the assessment reports of the intergovernmental panel state this position. Presenting it as an open scientific controversy misrepresents the state of the evidence.

How much will it warm, how fast, and what exactly will happen where? Genuinely uncertain, and scientists say so in print. The panel's sixth assessment gives a likely range rather than a number for how much warming a doubling of carbon dioxide produces, and the projections for particular regions, for the behavior of the great ice sheets, for tipping points and for extreme weather at local scale carry substantial and honestly stated uncertainty. Uncertainty here cuts both ways, which is a point worth making: it includes outcomes worse than the central estimate as well as milder ones.

What should be done, by whom, how fast, and at whose expense? Not a scientific question at all. It involves how much a future harm is worth against a present cost, how much risk a society should accept, which technologies to bet on, how the burden falls across countries and across income groups within them, and what governments should be permitted to require of people. Serious people disagree about all of it, and the disagreement is not evidence of scientific ignorance. It is the ordinary politics of distribution, and it is the part this chapter treats as a debate.

The equity argument, which is the real obstacle

Here is the argument that has stalled climate negotiations since they began, stated from both sides as its holders state it.

Lower-income and industrializing countries point out that the accumulated carbon dioxide in the atmosphere was put there overwhelmingly by the countries that industrialized first, that those countries became rich in the process, and that emissions per person remain far higher in wealthy countries than in poor ones. On that view, asking a country to forgo cheap energy before its population has electricity is asking it to pay for a problem it did not create. The common but differentiated responsibilities principle, written into the framework convention agreed at Rio in 1992, is that argument in treaty language.

Wealthier countries respond that current and future emissions are what determine future concentrations, that emissions growth after the 1990s came predominantly from industrializing economies, that China has been the largest annual emitter since the mid-2000s, and that an agreement binding only some countries cannot solve a problem defined by the global total, while also placing their own industries at a competitive disadvantage. Both arguments are coherent. Neither is refuted by the other, because they are appealing to different principles, historical responsibility against present arithmetic.

Kyoto and Paris: two designs for the same problem

The framework convention of 1992 committed states to the goal and left the mechanism open. The Kyoto Protocol, adopted in 1997 and in force from 2005, chose binding targets, and it applied them to industrialized countries only, in line with the differentiated-responsibility principle. The United States signed and did not ratify, Canada withdrew in 2011, and the countries with the fastest-growing emissions had no targets. Binding commitments turned out to be binding mainly on the states willing to be bound.

The Paris Agreement of 2015 inverted the design. Every party, not only the industrialized ones, submits its own nationally determined contribution, and there is no enforcement beyond publication, review and the reputational cost of missing your own target. The stated aim is to hold warming well below two degrees Celsius above pre-industrial levels and to pursue efforts to limit it to one and a half. The trade is explicit: universal participation was bought by giving up binding targets. Whether that trade was worth making is a live argument, and the honest position as of the mid-2020s is that pledged contributions in aggregate have consistently fallen short of the stated temperature goal, which is exactly what a voluntary regime would be expected to produce and is not, on its own, evidence that a binding regime would have done better, since Kyoto's binding version produced non-participation instead.

How we know: the internal documents are part of the record

Historians of this debate have a source type that is unusual for a recent controversy: the internal research and public communications of the companies involved. Naomi Oreskes and Erik Conway's Merchants of Doubt (2010) traced a strategy of emphasizing uncertainty, developed earlier around tobacco, into the climate debate, and later peer-reviewed analyses by Supran and Oreskes compared one major oil company's internal projections with its public statements and found the internal science broadly consistent with the mainstream and the public messaging considerably more doubtful. Handle this carefully and precisely. It is evidence about how public doubt was cultivated, which is a question about corporate communications and is separately documented. It is not an argument about the physics, which stands or falls on the measurements in section 03, and it is not a reason to treat every person who opposes a particular climate policy as acting in bad faith. The policy disagreement described above is real and would exist without any of this.

Use this in your answer
Strongest tool, with its mechanism
Separating an evidentiary question from a distributive one. The mechanism is that a policy dispute is far easier to argue in scientific language than in the language of who pays, so a conflict about cost gets conducted as a conflict about facts. Pulling the two apart lets you state accurately that the attribution question is settled in the assessed literature while the response question is genuinely open, and that combination is the strongest position available on this topic.
Best evidence of exclusion or limit
The separation is analytical, not absolute. How much warming will occur and what it will cost feed directly into what response is justified, so the uncertain science and the contested policy are connected even though they are not the same question.
Comparison hook
Against the ozone case in section 03: same institutional machinery, same United Nations sponsorship, opposite outcome. Montreal had few producers, ready substitutes, a personal near-term harm and a modest bill; climate has effectively every economy, no drop-in substitute for the whole energy system, a diffuse and delayed harm and an enormous bill. Holding those two side by side is the fastest way to explain why one treaty worked.

Terms to use precisely

Common but differentiated responsibilities
The 1992 framework convention principle that all states share the problem while those that industrialized earlier bear greater obligation.
Kyoto Protocol
The 1997 agreement, in force 2005, setting binding emissions targets for industrialized countries only; the United States did not ratify and Canada withdrew in 2011.
Paris Agreement
The 2015 agreement under which every party sets its own contribution, trading binding targets for near-universal participation.
Nationally determined contribution
A country's self-set emissions pledge under the Paris framework, reviewed and published but not enforced.
Manufactured doubt
The documented strategy of emphasizing scientific uncertainty to delay regulation, traced by historians from tobacco into the climate debate.

Building an Answer That Scores

Each card is a claim with its evidence and its mechanism. The fourth is the one to practice out loud, because it is the answer that requires you to hold two different kinds of claim in one paragraph without collapsing them into each other.

Mechanism

Degradation follows from costs that never reach the decision-maker

Pigou named the externality in 1920: a cost falling on someone outside a transaction is invisible to the people making it, so the damaging activity gets carried out in larger quantities than anyone would choose if the bill arrived. Forest is cleared because cleared land has an owner and a market value while standing forest often has neither. Groundwater under the High Plains, the North China Plain and the Punjab is pumped past its recharge rate because each farmer's pump is individually rational and the falling water table is nobody's. Soviet planners drained the Aral Sea from the 1960s because the cotton had a price and the sea did not. The same sentence explains all three, and it explains carbon dioxide too.

Competition

Scarcity hardens existing politics rather than starting wars

As a shared resource declines, restraint becomes more necessary and less rational for each individual user, because the water you leave is water your neighbor pumps, so competition intensifies exactly as the resource shrinks. But the popular claim that scarcity causes war is not supported: systematic study of transboundary rivers finds treaties, joint commissions and data-sharing far more common than armed conflict. The Colorado was over-allocated by a 1922 compact and produced a federal shortage declaration in 2021 rather than violence, and the Nile dispute over the Ethiopian dam begun in 2011 produced years of hard negotiation. The defensible claim is that scarcity raises the stakes of an existing relationship, and whether that yields a treaty or a confrontation depends on the institutions, which is Topic 9.8.

Evidence

The greenhouse mechanism is Victorian physics with a modern measurement record

Foote reported the warming effect of carbon dioxide in sunlight in 1856; Tyndall measured infrared absorption by carbon dioxide and water vapor in 1859; Arrhenius calculated the warming from a doubling in 1896 and got about five degrees, above the modern best estimate of around three but the right sign and order of magnitude. Keeling's continuous record at Mauna Loa from 1958 shows the concentration rising every year, passing four hundred parts per million in the 2010s against a pre-industrial figure near two hundred eighty, and polar ice cores extend the record back hundreds of thousands of years while the isotopic signature identifies the added carbon as fossil in origin. Two independent methods agreeing, one instrumental and one geological, is the strongest form this kind of evidence takes.

Argument

Kyoto and Paris made opposite trades, and the ozone treaty shows why both were hard

The Montreal Protocol of 1987 worked because a handful of firms made the chemicals, substitutes existed, the harm was personal and near-term, and the bill was modest; it is universally ratified and the ozone layer has been assessed as recovering. Climate has none of those features. Kyoto in 1997 chose binding targets and applied them only to industrialized countries, and the United States did not ratify while Canada withdrew in 2011, so the binding version bound the willing. Paris in 2015 chose universal participation with self-set contributions and no enforcement, and pledges in aggregate have fallen short of the stated goal of holding warming well below two degrees. The equity argument underneath both is genuine on both sides: accumulated emissions came overwhelmingly from the early industrializers, and future concentrations depend on present emissions wherever they occur.
