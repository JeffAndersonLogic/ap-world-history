A Standard You Can Be Held To

In 1948 the United Nations General Assembly adopted a declaration of human rights that bound nobody to anything. Governments signed it partly because it cost them nothing. Within a generation their own citizens were quoting it back at them, and that is the mechanism this chapter is built on: a publicly accepted standard is a lever, because the distance between what a state says and what it does can be measured, documented and published.

01

How an Unenforceable Document Acquired Force

1948 to 1993  ·  Paris to Vienna

The Universal Declaration of Human Rights created no court, no penalty and no obligation. Understanding how a document like that changes anything is the most transferable idea in this topic, and it explains far more than the document itself.

What was adopted, and by whom

The General Assembly adopted the Universal Declaration of Human Rights in Paris on 10 December 1948: forty-eight states in favor, none against, and eight abstentions. Those abstentions are worth naming because they show what the argument was about. The Soviet Union and its allies abstained, objecting that the declaration protected civil and political rights while saying too little about economic and social security and interfering in domestic affairs. South Africa abstained because its government had begun building apartheid that year and could not accept an equality clause. Saudi Arabia abstained over provisions on marriage and on changing religion.

The drafting was chaired by Eleanor Roosevelt, and the drafting committee is the answer to the most common objection to the document. It included René Cassin of France, Charles Malik of Lebanon, Peng Chun Chang of China and John Humphrey of Canada, and the debates drew on Confucian, Islamic and Latin American as well as European traditions. Latin American states, whose own constitutions already contained social rights, pushed hard for the economic and social articles. The declaration is not simply a Western document exported, and a student who can say why has answered an argument they will meet.

Because the declaration bound nobody, the binding instruments came later and in two pieces. The International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights and the International Covenant on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights were both adopted in 1966 and entered into force in 1976. The split is the Cold War in treaty form: the Western bloc emphasized speech, assembly, due process and elections, while the Soviet bloc emphasized work, housing, health and education. Splitting them into two covenants was the compromise that let both be adopted, and it left the question of whether rights are a single package unresolved.

The mechanism: a standard nobody can enforce is still a lever

Here is how a document with no teeth changes behavior. First, it converts a political demand into a claim about a rule the government has already accepted, which is a much stronger rhetorical position: the citizen is not asking for a favor, they are asking for compliance. Second, it makes a state's conduct comparable, so that publicity about a violation is embarrassing in a way that a general accusation is not. Third, it gives outside organizations a fixed thing to monitor.

How we know: Helsinki is the controlled experiment

In 1975 thirty-five states including the Soviet Union signed the Helsinki Final Act, a broad European security agreement whose third section committed signatories to respect human rights. Soviet negotiators accepted it because they wanted the rest of the agreement, which recognized postwar borders, and because the human rights section was not enforceable. What followed is the clearest evidence in this chapter that a standard is a lever. Citizens' groups formed across the Eastern bloc to monitor their own governments' compliance with an agreement those governments had signed and published: Helsinki monitoring groups in the Soviet Union from 1976 and Charter 77 in Czechoslovakia in 1977. Western monitoring organizations formed in parallel, including Helsinki Watch in 1978, which later became Human Rights Watch. Their members were arrested and imprisoned, so the mechanism should not be romanticized. But dissidents were now demanding compliance with a document their own state had signed, which is a very different political position from opposition, and historians of the period widely regard it as a factor in the erosion of Eastern bloc legitimacy through the 1980s.

The other institution the mechanism produced is the human rights organization. Amnesty International began in 1961 with a newspaper article by the lawyer Peter Benenson about prisoners jailed for their opinions, and built a method out of documentation and letter-writing that assumed publicity was itself a sanction. It received the Nobel Peace Prize in 1977. The method's limits are as instructive as its successes: it works best against governments that care about their international standing and worst against those that do not.

The universality argument, which is not settled

The World Conference on Human Rights held at Vienna in 1993 reaffirmed that human rights are universal, and it did so because that claim had been challenged. In the run-up, a group of Asian states meeting in Bangkok issued a declaration emphasizing national sovereignty, non-interference and the importance of regional and cultural particularities. Leaders including Singapore's Lee Kuan Yew and Malaysia's Mahathir Mohamad argued publicly that what was presented as universal reflected a Western emphasis on the individual against the community, and that development and social order were legitimate priorities.

The counter-argument was made by others in the same region, notably by dissidents and by the South Korean politician Kim Dae-jung, who argued that Asian traditions contained their own grounds for limiting rulers and that "Asian values" was a convenient doctrine for governments that did not want to be criticized. Both positions have serious advocates. Present it as the live dispute it is, note that the loudest defenders of cultural exception were frequently governments rather than the populations concerned, and note also that the objection to selective enforcement, that powerful states are rarely held to the standard they apply to others, is a criticism the record supports.

Use this in your answer
Strongest tool, with its mechanism
A published standard as a lever. The mechanism is that once a government has publicly accepted a norm, criticism stops being a demand for change and becomes a demand for consistency, which is far harder to dismiss, and it gives citizens and outside monitors a fixed benchmark to document against. That is why the unenforceable Helsinki commitments of 1975 produced monitoring groups inside the Eastern bloc within two years.
Best evidence of exclusion or limit
The lever needs a fulcrum. It works on states that value international standing, trade access or domestic legitimacy, and it works poorly on states indifferent to all three, which is why the same documents produced results in some places and nothing in others.
Comparison hook
Against the Mandate of Heaven in Topic 1.1: both are standards a ruler publicly accepts that can then be used against them, since a claim to rule justly is a claim that can be shown to have failed. The difference is who does the showing. Heaven's verdict was read from floods and rebellions after the fact; a human rights standard is monitored continuously by organizations that publish.

Terms to use precisely

Universal Declaration of Human Rights
The non-binding declaration adopted by the General Assembly in December 1948 with forty-eight votes in favor and eight abstentions.
Two covenants
The binding treaties of 1966, in force 1976, splitting civil and political rights from economic, social and cultural rights along Cold War lines.
Helsinki Final Act
The 1975 European security agreement whose unenforceable human rights section gave Eastern bloc citizens a published standard to hold their governments to.
Naming and shaming
The method of documenting a state's violations against a standard it has accepted and publicizing the gap, which works only where reputation carries a cost.
Universality debate
The dispute, sharpened around the 1993 Vienna conference, over whether human rights standards apply across cultures or reflect a particular tradition.

The Vote, the Classroom, and the Things That Moved Slowest

1893 to the 2020s  ·  New Zealand to the present

Women’s legal status changed more in the twentieth century than in the previous several combined, and the change was extremely uneven across three different domains: formal political rights moved first, education second, and the division of household work barely at all. Keeping the three apart is what makes an answer precise.

Suffrage, and why the sequence is instructive

New Zealand extended the parliamentary vote to women in 1893, the first self-governing country to do so; Finland followed in 1906 with the right to stand for election as well. The United States ratified the Nineteenth Amendment in 1920 and the United Kingdom equalized the franchise in 1928. France enfranchised women in 1944, and Switzerland only granted the vote in federal elections in 1971, with one canton holding out until a court decision in 1990. Saudi Arabia permitted women to vote in municipal elections in 2015.

That spread of over a century inside the group of wealthy democracies is the useful fact, because it refuses the story of a steady march. The pattern that does hold is that suffrage tended to arrive where women's organizations were already large and where a political crisis, most often a war that had put women into paid work and public roles, made the exclusion visibly awkward. Newly independent states after 1945 frequently enfranchised women in their founding constitutions, which meant the formal right arrived without the decades of campaigning it had taken elsewhere, and sometimes without the organizations that campaigning had built.

Education, which is the variable with the widest downstream effects

The expansion of schooling to girls is the change in this topic with the largest measurable consequences, and it deserves more space than it usually gets. Across the second half of the twentieth century, primary enrollment rose sharply almost everywhere and the gap between girls' and boys' enrollment narrowed substantially in most regions, with the largest remaining gaps at secondary level and in parts of South Asia and sub-Saharan Africa. Female literacy rose correspondingly, and in a number of countries women came to outnumber men in higher education by the 2010s.

The reason this matters beyond itself is the set of correlations attached to it, which are among the most robust in development research. Women with more schooling marry later, have their first child later, have fewer children overall, and their children have lower mortality and more schooling in turn. Be careful with the causal claim, because schooling, income and health all move together and disentangling them is genuinely hard, but the association is strong, consistent across countries, and it is the mechanism connecting this section to the fertility decline in the Topic 9.1 chapter.

The institutional machinery followed the demand rather than creating it. The United Nations ran a Decade for Women from 1976 to 1985 with world conferences at Mexico City, Copenhagen and Nairobi; the Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Discrimination against Women was adopted in 1979 and entered into force in 1981; and the Fourth World Conference at Beijing in 1995 produced a platform for action that became the standard reference for national policy. Each of these is a standard of the kind section 01 described, and each is monitored by organizations that publish.

What did not move

Three things resisted, and naming them is how a student earns the qualification point.

Unpaid work. Time-use surveys across a wide range of countries consistently find women performing substantially more unpaid domestic and care work than men, and the gap narrowed slowly where it narrowed at all. Because paid employment rose without a matching redistribution at home, the common result was a double burden rather than a transfer. Political representation. Formal eligibility did not produce proportional presence, and the countries that moved fastest generally did so through deliberate mechanisms, quotas in party lists or reserved seats, rather than through gradual change; Rwanda's parliament, following constitutional quotas adopted in 2003, became the first in the world with a majority of women. Law and enforcement. Legal equality on paper coexisted in many jurisdictions with unequal inheritance, nationality, divorce or property rules, and with laws that were equal on the page and enforced unevenly.

Common mistake to avoid

Do not write that global feminism was one movement with one program. It was not, and the disagreements were substantive. At the United Nations conferences of the 1970s and 1980s, delegates from newly independent and socialist states argued that the priorities put forward by many Western organizations, focused on individual legal equality and reproductive autonomy, were secondary to development, colonialism, apartheid and access to water, food and health care. Women's organizations in postcolonial states frequently framed their demands in terms of national development or religious tradition rather than individual rights. Writing that Western feminism "spread" to the rest of the world both misstates the history and erases movements with their own origins, such as the Egyptian feminist movement of the 1920s or the Indian women's organizations active in the independence struggle.

Use this in your answer
Strongest tool, with its mechanism
Formal right, then access, then distribution. The mechanism is that a legal change costs a state a statute, expanded access costs it schools and clinics, and redistributing unpaid work costs it nothing it can legislate at all, so the three move at completely different speeds. That ordering explains almost every apparent contradiction in this topic, including how a country can have equal suffrage, majority-female universities and a large gap in domestic labor at the same time.
Best evidence of exclusion or limit
The sequence is a pattern rather than a law, and quotas are the clearest counterexample: where states deliberately intervened in representation, the change was rapid rather than gradual, which shows the slow parts are slow by default rather than by necessity.
Comparison hook
Against Neo-Confucian gender norms in Topic 1.1: there the hierarchy was maintained inside the household, mother to daughter, with no official ever appearing, which is exactly why it outlasted every dynasty that endorsed it. The twentieth century changed what the state required and left much of what households do untouched, and the persistence of the unpaid-work gap is the same mechanism at work.

Terms to use precisely

CEDAW
The Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Discrimination against Women, adopted 1979 and in force 1981, the principal binding treaty on gender equality.
Beijing Platform for Action
The program agreed at the Fourth World Conference on Women in 1995, which became the standard reference for national gender policy.
Double burden
Paid employment added to an unredistributed load of domestic and care work, the common result where women entered the workforce without change at home.
Gender quota
A reserved share of candidacies or seats, the mechanism behind the fastest increases in women's political representation, as in Rwanda from 2003.
Time-use survey
The instrument that measures unpaid domestic and care work, and the evidence base for the claim that this gap moved slowest.
03

Taking Hierarchy Out of the Law

1948 to 1994  ·  Apartheid, civil rights, reservation

Three societies wrote racial or caste hierarchy into their statute books and three dismantled it, by three different methods, within roughly half a century. Comparing the methods is more useful than narrating any one of them, and the shared conclusion is uncomfortable: repealing the law was the fast part.

South Africa

Apartheid was not an old custom formalized. It was built rapidly after the National Party took power in 1948, through statutes that classified every person by race, restricted where each classification could live and work, controlled movement by pass laws, and reserved political rights for the white minority. It is important to say that it was legislation, because it means it could be repealed, and because it makes South Africa the clearest available case of a modern state constructing a racial order deliberately.

The resistance ran through mass protest, armed struggle and international pressure at once. Police killed sixty-nine people at Sharpeville in March 1960 during a protest against pass laws; the African National Congress was banned and turned to armed struggle; Nelson Mandela was sentenced to life imprisonment in 1964; and the Soweto uprising of June 1976, begun by schoolchildren protesting compulsory instruction in Afrikaans, brought a new generation into the movement. Externally, sporting boycotts, arms embargoes, divestment campaigns on university campuses and the United States Comprehensive Anti-Apartheid Act of 1986, passed over a presidential veto, raised the cost of the system to the South African economy and to the government's standing.

Mandela was released in February 1990, the first non-racial election was held in April 1994, and a Truth and Reconciliation Commission chaired by Desmond Tutu operated from 1995, offering amnesty in exchange for full disclosure rather than prosecuting. That trade is worth arguing about in a checkpoint: it prioritized a documented public record and a peaceful transition over punishment, and victims' families have criticized it for exactly that. And the qualification: political equality arrived and the distribution of land, capital and income did not follow, so South Africa has remained among the most unequal countries in the world by measured income inequality since the transition.

The United States and India

In the United States, legally enforced segregation in the southern states was attacked through litigation, mass nonviolent protest and federal legislation. The Supreme Court's decision in 1954 that segregated public schools were unconstitutional, the Montgomery bus boycott of 1955 and 1956, and the campaigns of the early 1960s produced the Civil Rights Act of 1964, which barred discrimination in public accommodations and employment, and the Voting Rights Act of 1965, which put federal machinery behind registration and voting in jurisdictions with a record of exclusion. Those are two separate statutes in two separate years and confusing them is a common and avoidable error.

India took a different route: it wrote the remedy into the constitution rather than fighting for it afterwards. The constitution of 1950, whose drafting committee was chaired by B. R. Ambedkar, himself born into a community treated as untouchable, abolished untouchability and provided reservation, quotas in legislatures, public employment and public higher education for Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes. The Mandal Commission, reporting in 1980 and implemented from 1990, extended reservation to a much larger category of Other Backward Classes, provoking intense protest and litigation, and the Supreme Court upheld it with limits in 1992.

Reservation is the most interesting case here precisely because it is the opposite policy to the American one. The United States removed race from the law and then argued for decades about whether race-conscious remedies were permissible; India put caste into the law as the basis of a remedy and has argued ever since about how large the categories should be and for how long. Both are still arguing, which tells you something about the difficulty rather than about either country.

Common mistake to avoid

Do not end any of these three narratives at the legislation. The strongest version of this topic is the gap between formal and substantive equality, and each case supplies it: South Africa's political transition of 1994 was not followed by a comparable redistribution of land or wealth, and it has remained among the most unequal countries measured by income; American measures of the wealth gap between Black and white households showed persistent and large differences decades after 1965; and caste continued to shape marriage, occupation and violence in India despite constitutional abolition. This is not a claim that the reforms failed. It is the specific claim that removing legal exclusion changes what is permitted and does not by itself change what has accumulated, and that distinction is the qualification a checkpoint is asking for.

The ideas that made the claims sayable

Two intellectual movements gave people a language for these arguments and both are named in the key concept. Negritude, developed in Paris in the 1930s by Aimé Césaire of Martinique, Léopold Sédar Senghor of Senegal and Léon Damas of French Guiana, refused the colonial framing in which African cultures were a deficit to be corrected and asserted a shared Black cultural inheritance with its own value. Senghor became the first president of independent Senegal in 1960, which is a useful reminder that the poets of one decade were the state builders of the next.

Liberation theology emerged in Latin America, crystallizing at the conference of Latin American bishops at Medellín in 1968 and in Gustavo Gutiérrez's book of 1971. Its central move was to read the Christian scriptures as speaking primarily to material poverty and injustice, which converted a religion often allied with landowners into a language of demand. Its most famous figure, Archbishop Óscar Romero of El Salvador, was assassinated while saying mass in March 1980. It also drew sustained criticism from the Vatican in the mid-1980s for its use of Marxist analysis, which is part of the history and should be included rather than smoothed over.

Use this in your answer
Strongest tool, with its mechanism
Repeal changes permission, not accumulation. The mechanism is that a discriminatory legal order produces two things at once: a rule about what people may do, and a stock of accumulated advantage in land, capital, education and networks. Repealing the rule stops the flow and leaves the stock, so measured inequality between the groups persists for generations after the statute is gone. This is why every case in this section shows rapid legal change and slow distributional change.
Best evidence of exclusion or limit
Legal change is not merely symbolic and should not be described that way. Ending pass laws, segregated schooling and voting exclusion changed millions of lives immediately, and the argument here is about what repeal cannot reach, not about whether it mattered.
Comparison hook
Against abolition in Topic 6.5 and Unit 5: emancipation ended a legal status and left, in most cases, no land, no capital and no compensation for the freed while compensating the owners, and the resulting patterns of landholding and poverty persisted for generations. The structure of the outcome is the same in 1834, in 1865 and in 1994, and holding the three together is a strong continuity argument.

Terms to use precisely

Apartheid
The legislated racial order built in South Africa from 1948, dismantled through mass resistance and international pressure, with the first non-racial election in 1994.
Reservation
India's constitutional quotas in legislatures, public employment and higher education for Scheduled Castes and Tribes, extended to Other Backward Classes from 1990.
Truth and Reconciliation Commission
The South African body from 1995 that offered amnesty for full disclosure, trading prosecution for a documented public record.
Negritude
The movement developed in 1930s Paris by Cesaire, Senghor and Damas asserting the value of a shared Black cultural inheritance against colonial framing.
Liberation theology
The Latin American reading of Christianity as addressed to material poverty and injustice, crystallized at Medellin in 1968 and in Gutierrez's 1971 book.
04

When the Target Is the System Rather Than the State

1971 to the 2020s  ·  Greenpeace to fair trade

The movements in this section had a harder problem than the ones above. A campaign against a law has an address to send its demand to. A campaign against the distribution of environmental and economic costs across a global economy has no such address, and how those movements solved that problem is the useful part.

Three ways to campaign without a government to petition

Attach the global claim to a local one. The Chipko movement, from 1973 in the Himalayan foothills of what is now Uttarakhand, opposed commercial logging by villagers physically placing themselves between the trees and the contractors. Its demands were about local rights to forest resources, and its effect included a moratorium on felling in the region. Wangari Maathai's Green Belt Movement, founded in Kenya in 1977, organized rural women to plant trees, which addressed fuelwood, erosion and income simultaneously and put those women into an organized relationship with the state; Maathai received the Nobel Peace Prize in 2004. Both are environmental campaigns that worked because they were also livelihood campaigns, and neither began by addressing an international body.

Make the global harm visible. Greenpeace, founded in 1971 when activists sailed toward a United States nuclear test site off Alaska, built its method on producing images that carried an argument. Sailing a small boat between a whale and a harpoon, or between a test site and a warship, generates a photograph that needs no explanation, and the target of the campaign is public opinion in the countries whose governments and companies are responsible. The method's strength is reach and its weakness is that a photograph makes an argument about a moment and not about a system.

Use the market as the lever. Fair trade certification, developed from the late 1980s with the first labeling initiative launched in the Netherlands in 1988 and international bodies including the World Fair Trade Organization following, tries to route a higher and more stable price to producers by giving consumers a label to choose. Independent evaluations have generally found modest and real benefits for participating producers, concentrated in cooperatives, and limited effects on the wider market, which is roughly what the mechanism would predict: a scheme that depends on consumers paying more can only reach as far as consumers willing to pay more.

What the record supports about when reform movements succeed

Pulling the whole chapter together, the campaigns in it did better under four conditions, and this is a genuinely useful analytical tool rather than a list.

They did better when there was a specific rule to change rather than a general condition to improve, because a rule has a decision-maker attached. They did better where the target cared about reputation, whether a government seeking loans and legitimacy or a brand selling to consumers who read labels. They did better where the movement could impose a concrete cost, a boycott, a strike, a divestment campaign, litigation, rather than only moral pressure. And they did better where they could point to a standard the target had already accepted, which returns to section 01.

The apartheid case has all four, which is why it appears in every textbook, and it is worth noticing that it also took over four decades. The campaigns that changed the distribution of economic costs have generally had the fewest of the four, which is a large part of why the Topic 9.7 chapter is about people who went from petitioning to protesting in the street.

How we know: measuring what a movement achieved is genuinely hard

Attributing an outcome to a movement is one of the harder problems in modern history, because movements campaign during periods when many other things are changing. Historians of the anti-apartheid campaign, for example, weigh international sanctions against the internal costs of township revolt, against the collapse of the Soviet Union removing the government's argument that the ANC was a communist front, and against the economic pressure of capital flight, and they reach different conclusions about the balance. The honest formulation for any of these cases is that a movement was one contributing cause among several, and that the strongest evidence is usually a documented change in the target's own calculations, such as a company's decision to divest or a minister's memorandum, rather than the coincidence of a protest and a policy change.

Use this in your answer
Strongest tool, with its mechanism
Finding an address for a systemic grievance. The mechanism is that political pressure needs a decision-maker who can grant the demand, so a movement against a diffuse global harm has to convert it into something with an owner: a specific statute, a named company, a licensing decision, a purchasing choice. Chipko turned deforestation into a felling contract, and fair trade turned commodity prices into a label on a bag. The conversion is the strategy.
Best evidence of exclusion or limit
The conversion also shrinks the claim. A moratorium on logging in one district, or a premium for certified cooperatives, addresses a fraction of what the movement was actually objecting to, and the gap between the demand and the winnable version of it is the standing frustration of every campaign in this section.
Comparison hook
Against the abolitionist campaigns of Topic 5.7 and Topic 6.5: the sugar boycott, the petitions, the parliamentary lobbying and the survivor testimony are the same four conditions being assembled two centuries earlier, against a system that was also global and also profitable. Reading the anti-apartheid divestment campaign as the direct descendant of the sugar boycott is defensible and makes a strong continuity argument.

Terms to use precisely

Chipko movement
The Himalayan campaign from 1973 in which villagers physically obstructed commercial logging, framing forest protection as a local livelihood right.
Green Belt Movement
Wangari Maathai's Kenyan organization founded in 1977 combining tree planting with rural women's income and organization; Nobel Peace Prize 2004.
Fair trade certification
A labeling scheme from the late 1980s routing a higher price to certified producers, with benefits found to be real, modest and concentrated in cooperatives.
Divestment
Withdrawing investment from a target to impose a financial cost, used against apartheid from the 1970s and against fossil fuel holdings later.
Substantive equality
Equality of actual outcomes and capabilities, distinguished from formal equality, which is equality of legal status.

Building an Answer That Scores

Each card is a claim, the evidence, and the mechanism. The last card is the qualification that most often separates a top answer from a competent one, and it works on nearly every reform question in the course.

Mechanism

An unenforceable standard changes politics by making criticism a demand for consistency

The Universal Declaration of December 1948 created no court and bound nobody, and forty-eight states voted for it while eight abstained, the Soviet bloc over its emphasis on civil and political rights, South Africa over the equality clause, Saudi Arabia over marriage and religion. Its force came later and indirectly. When thirty-five states including the Soviet Union signed the Helsinki Final Act in 1975, the human rights section was accepted precisely because it was unenforceable, and within two years citizens had formed monitoring groups in the Soviet Union and Charter 77 in Czechoslovakia demanding compliance with a document their own governments had signed and published. Their members were imprisoned, so the mechanism is not magic. But a demand for consistency is a much stronger position than a demand for change, and it is why publishing a standard is never costless.

Comparison

Three states removed hierarchy from law by three different methods

South Africa's apartheid was legislated rapidly after 1948 and dismantled through mass resistance, armed struggle and external pressure including divestment and the United States sanctions act of 1986, ending with Mandela's release in 1990 and a non-racial election in 1994. The United States removed legally enforced segregation through litigation from 1954, mass nonviolent protest, and two separate statutes, the Civil Rights Act of 1964 and the Voting Rights Act of 1965. India took the opposite approach and wrote the remedy into the constitution of 1950, abolishing untouchability and reserving legislative seats, public jobs and university places for Scheduled Castes and Tribes, extended to Other Backward Classes from 1990. Removing a category from the law and building a remedy on the category are both defensible strategies, and both countries are still arguing about theirs.

Causation

Female education is the reform with the widest downstream effects

Primary enrollment rose almost everywhere across the second half of the twentieth century and the gap between girls and boys narrowed substantially in most regions, with the largest remaining gaps at secondary level in parts of South Asia and sub-Saharan Africa; in a number of countries women outnumbered men in higher education by the 2010s. The associations attached to it are among the most consistent in development research: more schooling goes with later marriage, later first birth, lower total fertility, lower child mortality and more schooling for the next generation. Be careful with the causal language, since schooling, income and health move together and are hard to separate, but the association is strong enough across countries to be the mechanism connecting this topic to the fertility decline in Topic 9.1.

Qualification

Repeal ends the rule and leaves what the rule accumulated

A discriminatory legal order produces two things: a rule about what people may do, and a stock of advantage in land, capital, education and connections. Repeal stops the first and leaves the second, so measured gaps persist for generations. South Africa achieved political equality in 1994 without a comparable redistribution of land or wealth and has remained among the most unequal countries by measured income inequality; American measures of the household wealth gap between Black and white families showed large persistent differences decades after 1965; caste continued to shape marriage, occupation and violence in India after constitutional abolition. The same shape appears after emancipation in Unit 5 and Topic 6.5, where freed people received no land and former owners received compensation. This is the qualification, and it applies to almost every reform question the exam can ask.
