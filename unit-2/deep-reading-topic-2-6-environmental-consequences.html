The Cargo Nobody Ordered

A network that moves silk moves everything else on the same path, including a bacterium that killed a third of the people between China and Ireland. This chapter follows the pandemic from its reservoir to its consequences, then turns to the slower environmental costs of connection, and ends with how any of this can be known.

01

The Pathway

1338 to 1353  ·  From the steppe to the Atlantic

The pandemic was a consequence of integration, not an accident that happened during it. The disease had a home, the trade gave it a road, and the road ran everywhere the trade did.

The organism and its reservoir

The Black Death was caused by Yersinia pestis, a bacterium that lives in populations of burrowing rodents and circulates among them through fleas. In its bubonic form it reaches a human when an infected flea bites, producing swollen lymph nodes and a high mortality rate; in its pneumonic form it settles in the lungs and passes directly between people through the air, killing faster and needing no flea at all. Both forms were present in the fourteenth century, which is part of why it moved so quickly through crowded settlements in winter.

Long-standing rodent reservoirs exist in Central Asia, and it is there that the current evidence places the beginning. That combination, a durable animal reservoir plus a route to carry infected rodents, fleas and people out of it, is what turns a regional disease into a pandemic.

How we know: a cemetery in Kyrgyzstan and the genomes in it

In a valley in what is now Kyrgyzstan, a cemetery contains headstones dated to 1338 and 1339, some inscribed in Syriac saying the person died of pestilence. In 2022 researchers recovered Yersinia pestis DNA from remains at that site and found it sits at the point in the bacterium's family tree from which the strains of the later pandemic descend. That is a remarkable convergence of three independent sources: dated inscriptions, an archaeological deposit and a genome. It does not prove the pandemic started in that valley, and the researchers do not claim it did, but it places an outbreak of the right organism at the right moment in a trading community on the routes described in the Topic 2.1 chapter.

The route west

The disease moved along the commercial arteries: westward through the territories of the Golden Horde, reaching the Black Sea ports where Italian merchants traded. In 1346 it was in the army besieging Kaffa, the Genoese colony in the Crimea described in the Topic 2.1 chapter. From the Black Sea, Genoese shipping carried it to Constantinople and to the Mediterranean, and by 1347 it was in Sicily, Alexandria and the Levantine ports. Through 1348 and 1349 it moved across Italy, France, Iberia, England, the German lands, Egypt and Syria, and by 1351 to 1353 it had reached Scandinavia and Russia.

Notice the shape of the spread. It jumps along shipping lanes rather than crawling across the land, appearing first in port cities and then radiating inland, which is exactly the signature of a disease traveling in cargo and in the ships' rats rather than walking with refugees. The map of the plague is a map of the trade.

Common mistake to avoid

The story that the besieging army catapulted plague corpses over the walls of Kaffa, infecting the defenders and starting the European outbreak, comes from a chronicle written by a man who was not there, from secondhand report. Two problems: the mechanism is unlikely, since plague does not transmit efficiently from a corpse, and it is unnecessary, because rats, fleas and infected people were already moving through a besieged trading port constantly. Use Kaffa, which is genuinely the gateway, and describe the mechanism as infected fleas and rodents traveling in ships' cargo. That version is both better supported and more useful, because it explains why the disease appeared in ports first.

Best evidence of exclusion or limit
The origin question is still open. The Kyrgyzstan evidence is strong for an outbreak of an ancestral strain in 1338 to 1339 and is not a proof of where the pandemic began, so say what it shows rather than more than it shows.

Against the Columbian Exchange in Unit 4: both are cases of pathogens moving into populations with no prior exposure along new routes of contact, and the comparison sets up the single most important continuity between Unit 2 and the rest of the course.

02

The Same Mortality, Opposite Results

1347 to 1450  ·  Europe, Egypt, China

A third to a half of the population died across the affected zone, and the consequences differed completely from region to region. That is the most instructive fact in the topic, because it proves that what a shock does depends on the structure it lands on.

Western Europe: labor becomes scarce and expensive

The Topic 1.6 chapter sets this out in full and it is the standard case. Mortality of roughly a third to a half left the land, tools, mills and livestock intact with far fewer people to work them, so labor became scarce and expensive while land became abundant and cheap. Wages rose, rents fell, and a peasant who disliked his terms could walk to a manor that would take him. Legislation attempting to freeze wages at pre-plague levels failed, risings followed in France in 1358 and England in 1381, and serfdom in western Europe dissolved over the following century.

In eastern Europe the same shortage produced the opposite outcome, because peasants could not move and landlords held more leverage over weaker monarchies, so obligations tightened instead. Two regions, one shock, two results, decided by whether labor had an exit.

Mamluk Egypt: labor becomes scarce and the land goes out of use

Now the case that makes the argument general, and it is the reason this section exists. Egypt suffered comparable mortality, and the results were not a labor windfall for survivors but a long agricultural decline. The reason is in the landscape. Egyptian agriculture depended on the Nile flood managed through a system of canals, dikes and basins that required constant collective maintenance: clearing silt, repairing embankments, coordinating the flood across whole districts. That work needs a large workforce mobilized at particular times, and it produces nothing at all if only part of it is done.

Kill a third of the population and a wage laborer in England becomes more valuable to a landlord. Kill a third of the population in Egypt and the irrigation system loses the labor it needs to function, land falls out of cultivation, the tax base shrinks, and a ruling elite that held land as revenue assignments squeezes what remains harder to maintain its income, which drives more cultivators away. Repeated later outbreaks made recovery worse. The result is a state and an agricultural system that were weaker at the end of the fifteenth century than at the start of the fourteenth.

Hold the two cases together and you have a claim worth building an essay on: whether a labor shortage empowers workers or destroys production depends on whether production requires collective infrastructure. Where a peasant can farm alone, scarcity is bargaining power. Where a peasant needs a canal that a thousand people maintain, scarcity is collapse.

How we know: contemporaries counted, badly, and consistently

Chroniclers on both sides of the Mediterranean give mortality figures, and taken literally they are unusable: numbers are rounded, exaggerated for effect and sometimes theological rather than statistical. What historians rely on instead are administrative records that were kept for other purposes, English manorial rolls recording tenancies falling vacant, tax registers, cathedral records of clergy replaced, and later Egyptian survey data on land under cultivation. When several independent bureaucratic sources point the same direction, an estimate becomes usable, and it is that convergence rather than any chronicler's figure that supports the one-third to one-half range.

China, and the political consequences everywhere

In China the fourteenth century brought epidemic disease alongside catastrophic Yellow River flooding, famine, and the collapse of the Yuan paper currency, and the combination fed the risings that ended Mongol rule in 1368. Disentangling the plague's specific contribution there is genuinely difficult and historians disagree, which is worth saying rather than asserting a clean causal line.

Everywhere, the social consequences ran along existing fault lines. Religious authority was strained when prayer and clergy could not stop the deaths and clergy died in disproportionate numbers through their duties. Scapegoating followed, and in the German lands accusations that Jews had poisoned wells produced massacres in 1348 and 1349, in several cases before the disease had even arrived. Movements of extreme public penance spread and were suppressed. And a striking amount of art and literature turned to death as a subject in a way that had not been usual before.

Strongest tool, with its mechanism
Structure decides what a shock produces. The mechanism is that mass mortality leaves land and tools intact, so where a household could farm independently the survivors gained bargaining power, and where farming depended on canal systems requiring collective maintenance the loss of labor took the infrastructure and then the land out of use.
Best evidence of exclusion or limit
Attribution is hard. In China the plague arrived alongside floods, famine and currency collapse, and historians disagree about its independent weight, which is a limit to state rather than to hide.
Comparison hook
Against western and eastern Europe: three outcomes from one pandemic, wages up and serfdom gone in the west, serfdom hardened in the east, agricultural decline in Egypt. Any prompt asking why one cause had different effects can be answered from this trio alone.

Terms to use precisely

Labor scarcity
The condition after mass mortality in which surviving workers are fewer than the work available, raising wages where labor can bargain.
Basin irrigation
The Egyptian system of canals, dikes and flood basins requiring large-scale collective maintenance, and therefore vulnerable to population loss.
Iqta
The revenue assignment by which Mamluk officers were paid, which gave them an incentive to squeeze a shrinking tax base harder after the plague.
Scapegoating
The assignment of blame to a minority in a crisis, as in the 1348 and 1349 massacres of Jewish communities in the German lands.
Recurrence
The repeated later outbreaks that prevented demographic recovery for generations and made the first wave’s effects permanent.
03

Forests, Water, and Soil

c. 1000 to 1450  ·  What production did to landscapes

Trade did not only carry things, it created demand, and demand reshaped landscapes thousands of miles from the buyer. Three mechanisms cover most of it: cutting, watering and specializing.

Deforestation, and what it is actually for

Forests came down for four reasons in this period and it is worth separating them, because a specific reason is evidence and "deforestation" alone is not. Farmland: the European population roughly doubled between 1000 and 1300 and the extra people were fed by clearing woodland and draining marsh, a program that monasteries organized on a large scale. Fuel: cities burn wood and charcoal for heat and cooking, and the industries that fed the trade, ironworking, glass, ceramics, sugar boiling, brick and lime, consume it in enormous quantities. Timber: ships, buildings and mine props, and shipbuilding on the scale the Mediterranean and Indian Ocean trades required stripped accessible coastal woodland. Grazing: herds kept cleared land from regenerating.

The long-term significance the criterion asks for follows from what forest cover does. Remove it on a slope and rain moves soil into rivers, which silts harbors and irrigation channels downstream and thins the soil upstream. That is why deforestation in one district shows up as a failing port or a blocked canal in another, and it is the kind of connected consequence that turns a list of environmental changes into an argument.

Irrigation, and the salt it leaves behind

Irrigated agriculture fed the cities that the trade made possible, and it carries a specific long-term cost. Irrigation water contains dissolved salts; when it evaporates from a field in a hot, dry climate, the salts stay. Without enough drainage and enough flushing water, the concentration climbs until crops fail, a process called salinization, and the usual response, switching to more salt-tolerant crops, buys time rather than solving it. This is an old problem in Mesopotamia and it recurs wherever intensive irrigation meets aridity.

Add the maintenance problem from section 02. Canals and qanats silt up and must be constantly cleared, so an irrigation system is only as good as the labor and the authority behind it. The Mongol destruction of qanats in Iran and Iraq, the Egyptian labor collapse after the plague, and the silting of Angkor's water network described in the Topic 1.3 chapter are three versions of the same vulnerability: engineered landscapes are permanent commitments, and they degrade the moment the society maintaining them falters.

Specialization, which is the modern-sounding one

Trade makes it rational for a region to produce one thing and buy the rest, and once that happens the landscape is reorganized around a single crop. Sugar estates in Cyprus, Crete and Sicily, described in the Topic 2.5 chapter, are the sharpest case: land, water, fuel for the boiling houses and a compelled workforce all committed to one crop for export. Cotton, pepper and other cash crops did the same on a smaller scale.

Two consequences are worth naming. Monoculture is fragile: a specialized region imports its food, so a bad season, a blockade or a price collapse becomes a subsistence crisis rather than a business problem. And the environmental costs, exhausted soil, felled woodland for the boiling fires, diverted water, are borne where the crop grows while the profit is realized where it is sold. That separation between where a cost falls and where a benefit lands is the beginning of a pattern this course will follow for the next six units.

Strongest tool, with its mechanism
Distant demand reshaping a local landscape. The mechanism is that trade lets a region specialize in one export and buy its food, which commits land, water and fuel to a single crop, exhausts them locally, and delivers the profit somewhere else entirely, so the environmental cost and the economic benefit are separated by the length of the trade route.
Best evidence of exclusion or limit
Do not overstate the scale. These are pre-industrial economies, and much environmental change in the period is driven by population and climate rather than by trade; the trade-driven cases are specific and should be named specifically.
Comparison hook
Against the Americas in Topic 1.4: Maya soil exhaustion and Ancestral Puebloan deforestation happened with no long-distance trade demand at all, driven by local population growth on marginal land. Comparing the two shows which environmental damage requires a world economy and which does not.

Terms to use precisely

Deforestation
The clearing of woodland for farmland, fuel, timber and grazing, whose downstream effects include soil loss, silted harbors and blocked canals.
Salinization
The accumulation of salts in irrigated soil as water evaporates, which reduces and eventually ends crop yields in arid regions.
Siltation
The filling of channels and harbors with eroded soil, one of the connected consequences of clearing upstream slopes.
Monoculture
The commitment of a region to a single crop for export, which raises income and makes subsistence dependent on trade continuing.
Engineered landscape
A terrain whose productivity depends on continuous human maintenance, and which therefore degrades when labor or authority fails.
04

Species on the Move

c. 700 to 1450  ·  Rats, crops, animals

Trade routes are corridors for organisms as well as for cargo, and the organisms that traveled fall into three groups: the ones people carried on purpose, the ones that traveled in the cargo, and the ones that came for the food.

Carried on purpose

Crops are the big category and the Topic 2.5 chapter has the chains: sugarcane from Southeast Asia through India and Persia to the Mediterranean, bananas from Southeast Asia across the Indian Ocean to Africa, cotton, citrus and rice westward through the Islamic world, and Champa rice from Vietnam into Song China. Animals moved too: the camel was introduced into North Africa and became the technology that made the Sahara crossable, and warhorses were shipped across the Indian Ocean in numbers, as the Topic 2.3 chapter describes.

Deliberate introductions change ecosystems as much as accidental ones. A staple crop that thrives where local grains do poorly, as bananas did in wetter parts of Africa, supports a larger population, which then clears more land, which changes the landscape permanently. The chain runs plant, people, forest, and it is worth writing out that way rather than stopping at "bananas spread."

Traveling in the cargo

Rats are the significant case. Ship rats live wherever grain and goods are stored and move with them, and the fleas they carry move with the rats. What the trade routes built was effectively a continuous corridor of rodent habitat, warehouses, granaries, holds and caravanserais, linking regions that had been epidemiologically separate. Section 01 is the result.

Pests and pathogens of plants moved the same way, and so did weeds, whose seeds traveled in grain shipments and in fodder. None of this was chosen by anyone, which is exactly the point: a network optimized for moving valuable things efficiently is equally efficient at moving everything that can hide inside them.

The framing worth carrying forward

Historians usually reserve the term Columbian Exchange for what happened after 1492, and it should be reserved, because that exchange was larger, faster and between hemispheres with no shared biology at all. But the Afro-Eurasian exchange in this unit is the same process at a smaller scale and it establishes the pattern: connection moves organisms, deliberately and accidentally, and the accidental transfers are frequently the more consequential.

One difference is worth stating precisely, because it sets up Unit 4. In Afro-Eurasia, populations had been in intermittent contact for millennia and shared a long history of exposure to each other's diseases, so even the fourteenth-century pandemic met populations that were not entirely naive. When the same process reached the Americas after 1492, it met populations with no prior exposure to a whole suite of Eurasian pathogens, and the mortality was of a different order. Same mechanism, catastrophically different scale, and the reason is the prior isolation rather than anything about the diseases themselves.

Strongest tool, with its mechanism
The trade route as a habitat corridor. The mechanism is that warehouses, granaries, ships' holds and caravanserais form a continuous chain of rodent and pest habitat, so organisms that live alongside stored goods move with them and regions that were biologically separate become one system without anyone deciding it.
Best evidence of exclusion or limit

Comparison hook

Terms to use precisely

Habitat corridor
A continuous chain of suitable environments, such as granaries and ships’ holds, along which a species can move into new regions.
Introduced species
An organism established outside its native range through human movement, whether deliberately as with crops or accidentally as with rats.
Columbian Exchange
The transfer of organisms between hemispheres after 1492, the same process as this unit’s exchanges at a far larger scale.
Prior exposure
A population’s history of contact with a pathogen, the variable that explains why comparable diseases produced incomparable mortality in different regions.
Staple crop
A food that supplies the bulk of a population’s calories, whose introduction can change settlement patterns and land use permanently.
05

The Archive That Nobody Wrote

Evidence  ·  Rings, ice, pollen, genomes

Most of what this chapter claims is known from physical records rather than from documents, and those records have a decisive advantage: nobody made them to persuade anybody of anything.

Four archives and what each one holds

Tree rings. A tree adds a ring each year, wide in a good year and narrow in a poor one, and the pattern is shared across a region. Overlapping patterns from living trees, dead wood and construction timbers produces a continuous year-by-year record of growing conditions reaching back centuries, and dates any beam to the year its tree was felled. It is how the droughts behind the Ancestral Puebloan migrations in Topic 1.4 and the failures of Angkor's water system in Topic 1.3 are dated.

Ice cores. Snow falling on a glacier traps air, dust, ash and industrial pollution, and the layers can be counted and dated. Volcanic ash in the ice dates eruptions, and their sulfur explains cold years far away.

Pollen cores. Pollen grains preserved in lake and bog sediments are identifiable by species, so a dated column of sediment shows what was growing nearby, century by century. Forest species giving way to cereals and weeds is a picture of land clearance; the reverse is a picture of land being abandoned. Pollen is how the reforestation of abandoned farmland after the plague is measured.

Ancient DNA. Genetic material recovered from human, animal and microbial remains identifies pathogens directly and reconstructs their family trees, which is how the plague's identity, ancestry and probable geography were established, as section 01 describes.

How we know: the lead in the Alpine ice stopped

Medieval silver smelting released lead into the air, and that lead settled onto Alpine glaciers and stayed in the ice, layer by dated layer. A core taken from high in the Alps shows lead pollution present through the medieval centuries and then falling to near nothing in the years around 1349 to 1353, before recovering afterward. Nobody wrote that down. It is a direct physical measurement of an economy stopping: the mines closed because the miners were dead or scattered, and the sky over Europe was briefly cleaner than it would be again for centuries. When a question asks for evidence about the plague's economic impact, that is the single most striking piece available.

How to use physical evidence well

Two rules. First, physical archives are excellent for what and when and poor for why. Tree rings can tell you that rainfall failed for twenty-three years; they cannot tell you whether a society responded by migrating, fighting or reorganizing, and only documents and archaeology can. The strongest arguments in this unit pair the two: the drought record plus the abandoned settlement, the lead record plus the tax rolls.

Second, be careful with correlation. A cold decade and a rebellion in the same years is a hypothesis, not a finding. What upgrades it is a mechanism, harvest failure, then grain prices, then unrest, with evidence at more than one step. That is exactly what the good work on the fourteenth century does, and it is a standard you can apply to any environmental claim you meet for the rest of the course.

Use this in your answer
Strongest tool, with its mechanism
Unintentional evidence. The mechanism is that tree rings, ice layers, pollen and genomes were not created to communicate anything, so they carry no author's interest to correct for, which makes them independent checks on written sources rather than another opinion to weigh against them.
Best evidence of exclusion or limit
They are strong on what and when and weak on why. A physical record supplies the conditions and never the human response, so it needs documents and archaeology beside it to become history.
Comparison hook
Against the written sources in Topic 1.4, where almost everything about the Americas was recorded by conquerors: physical archives are the reason we can say things about societies whose own records were destroyed, which is why archaeology carries so much of the argument in that topic and so little in Song China.

Terms to use precisely

Dendrochronology
Dating and climate reconstruction from tree rings, accurate to the year and continuous over centuries.
Ice core
A cylinder of layered glacial ice preserving trapped air, dust, ash and pollution in dated sequence.
Pollen core
A dated column of lake or bog sediment whose preserved pollen shows which plants grew nearby, century by century.
Proxy record
A physical measurement standing in for something not directly observed, such as ring width for rainfall or ice-borne lead for mining activity.
Correlation and mechanism
The difference between two things happening together and a demonstrated chain connecting them; the second is what makes an environmental claim an argument.

Building an Answer That Scores

Each card is a full comparison: the claim, the specific evidence, and the reason. The second one is the strongest causation argument available anywhere in Unit 2, and it is worth learning by heart.

Networks and disease

The pandemic followed the trade because it traveled in the cargo

Plague reached Kaffa on the Black Sea in 1346, Constantinople, Sicily and Alexandria in 1347, and most of Europe, Egypt and Syria by 1349, appearing in port cities first and radiating inland from each one. That pattern is the signature of transmission by infected rodents and fleas in ships' cargo rather than by people walking overland. The difference matters because it explains the speed and the geography at once: a disease moving in trade goods travels at the speed of a merchant fleet and lands wherever the fleet unloads, which is why the map of the Black Death is a map of the fourteenth-century economy.

Causation

One pandemic, three outcomes, and the structure explains all of them

In western Europe, mortality of a third to a half made labor scarce and land abundant, so wages rose, wage-freezing legislation failed, risings followed in 1358 and 1381, and serfdom dissolved. In eastern Europe the same shortage hardened serfdom, because peasants could not move and lords held more leverage over weaker monarchies. In Mamluk Egypt it produced agricultural decline, because the Nile basin system needed constant collective canal maintenance that a reduced population could not supply, so land went out of cultivation and the tax base shrank. The difference exists because of what production required and whether labor had an exit, which is the general lesson: a shock has no single effect, only the effect a given structure converts it into.

Environment

Trade moved the cost of production away from the place that paid it

Sugar estates in Cyprus, Crete and Sicily committed land, water and woodland fuel to a crop consumed a thousand miles away, and the exhausted soil and felled forest stayed where the cane grew while the profit landed where it was sold. Deforestation for shipbuilding and fuel silted the harbors and canals downstream of the slopes that were cleared. The difference from subsistence agriculture is that the people bearing the environmental cost were not the people making the decision, because distant demand made the decision for them, and that separation is the pattern this course will follow through the plantation systems and industrial economies of later units.

Using evidence

The best evidence in this topic was never written down

Tree rings date the droughts behind the Ancestral Puebloan migrations and the failure of Angkor's water system to the year. Pollen cores show farmland reverting to forest after the plague. Ancient DNA identified Yersinia pestis and placed an ancestral strain in a Kyrgyzstan cemetery dated 1338 to 1339 by its own headstones. Alpine ice shows lead pollution from silver smelting falling to nearly nothing in the plague years and recovering afterward. None of it was created to persuade anyone, which is precisely why it settles questions that chroniclers, all of whom had reasons to exaggerate, cannot.
