The Silk Roads: Exchange Across Afro-Eurasia

Networks of Exchange · c. 1200 to c. 1450

The Problem

Overland trade required solutions no single government provided

  • Camels were expensive capital, not free transportation
  • No insurance industry existed to cover the risk of loss
  • No single currency was shared across the whole route

How We Know

Part Five

Why It Faded

Overland trade declined for economic and political reasons, not because any single power closed the routes.

Common Mistake to Avoid

No single power closed the Silk Roads

Common Mistake to Avoid

The Ottoman conquest of Constantinople in 1453 did not close the Silk Roads. Ottoman rulers wanted the customs revenue, and kept trade flowing through territory they now controlled.

Key Takeaway

Explain the decline as a shift in relative cost over time, not as a single closure event.

What Happened, 1335–1450

Three causes combined to reduce overland trade

Political fragmentation

Ilkhanate ends 1335, Yuan falls 1368, Timur's wars follow

The plague

Killed the populations that sustained caravan towns

The sea got cheaper

Ming China reoriented toward the coast after 1368

Key Takeaway

Political fragmentation, plague mortality, and cheaper sea routes combined to reduce overland trade. No single cause explains the decline alone.

Primary Source

What the road looked like from inside it

Marco Polo, The Travels of Marco Polo (Il Milione), dictated c. 1298

“At every twenty-five miles there stands a station where fresh horses await any messenger who carries the Khan's seal… Along the roads between the great cities, travelers find rest houses called caravanserais, where merchants may sleep and feed their animals. The roads are safe under the Khan's protection.”

Key Takeaway

Infrastructure (the yam relay), protection, and caravanserais, described from inside the system, in one paragraph.
