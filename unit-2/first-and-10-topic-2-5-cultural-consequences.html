Topic 2.5, Cultural Consequences of Connectivity  |  AP World History: Modern

First & 10 Reading

Goods Were Never the Only Cargo

Connectivity changes what societies know, and what they become. After 1200, the same routes that carried silk, spices, and gold carried beliefs, technologies, and travelers, and each of them left a mark on the places they reached.

CausationCultural InteractionContinuity & Change

The Story to Hold Onto

Bigger networks → More contact → Diffusion + adaptation → Cultural and intellectual change. Every example in this reading follows that chain. Use it to organize what you meet.

Question for Today

When more people and goods moved across Afro-Eurasia, what else moved with them, and how did it change the societies that received it?

1

Beliefs

Merchants Carried More Than Goods

By 1200, the Silk Roads, the Indian Ocean, and the trans-Saharan routes carried more traffic than ever. But goods were never the only cargo. Merchants, pilgrims, missionaries, and scholars traveled the same routes, and they carried their beliefs with them. More contact meant more chances for an idea to move, which historians call diffusion.

Buddhism kept shaping East Asia, where monasteries, art, and schools of Buddhist thought grew in China, Korea, and Japan. In Southeast Asia, Hinduism and Buddhism took root through trade with India. The temple at Angkor Wat, in the Khmer Empire, was built for the Hindu god Vishnu and later became a Buddhist site.

Islam spread into West Africa and across Asia. In West Africa and Southeast Asia it spread mostly through traders, scholars, and teachers rather than through armies. Rulers in Mali and in port cities like Malacca often converted first, partly because Islam connected them to Muslim merchants and to a wider world of law and learning.

AP Thinking, Causation

Name the carrier. "Islam spread" is not an explanation. Say who carried it, along which route, and why the people receiving it had a reason to adopt it. Network → contact → diffusion.

2

Technology

Inventions Moved, and Changed as They Moved

Technologies traveled the same way beliefs did. Paper, invented in China, had spread into the Islamic world centuries earlier, where cheap paper helped fill libraries in cities like Baghdad. From there it spread into Europe, where mills began making it from linen rags.

Gunpowder, another Chinese invention, reached Southwest Asia and Europe in the 1200s, along routes the Mongols had tied together. Societies that received it did not simply copy Chinese uses. Over time, armies in Europe and the Islamic world built it into cannons and new kinds of warfare.

This is adaptation: a receiving society takes something from outside and reshapes it for its own needs. Diffusion explains how an idea or tool arrived. Adaptation explains what it became once it got there.

AP Thinking, Causation

Finish the chain. Do not stop at "gunpowder spread to Europe." Explain how the society that received it changed it, and what that change made possible. Contact → diffusion → adaptation → change.

3

Cities

Connected Cities Could Rise, and Could Fall

The same traffic made cities rich. Hangzhou, the capital of the Southern Song, became one of the largest cities in the world, fed by productive rice farming and busy trade. Samarkand, in Central Asia, grew into a great Silk Road market where merchants, goods, and ideas met. This growth of cities is called urbanization.

But a connected city could also fall. In 1258 the Mongols sacked Baghdad, killed the caliph, and ended the Abbasid Caliphate, which had ruled from the city for about five hundred years. Baghdad had been one of the great centers of learning in the Islamic world, and it never fully recovered its old place.

Samarkand shows both sides. The Mongols destroyed it in 1220, and more than a century later it was rebuilt as a magnificent capital. When routes or rulers changed, a city's fortunes could change with them.

AP Thinking, Continuity & Change

City growth is not automatic. Explain what made a city grow, trade plus productive farming, and what could reverse it, war, conquest, or shifting routes. The same network that fed a city could expose it.

4

Travelers

More People Traveled, So More People Wrote It Down

Because more people traveled, more people recorded what they saw. Their writings are called travel accounts, and they let us see this connected world through the eyes of people who crossed it.

Ibn Battuta, a Muslim scholar from Morocco, spent about thirty years traveling through North and West Africa, the Middle East, India, and China, often working as a judge in Muslim communities along the way. Marco Polo, a merchant from Venice, described his years in Mongol China, and his book made Europeans curious about Asia. Margery Kempe, an English Christian, went on pilgrimages to Jerusalem and Rome and told her story in a book that is often called the first autobiography in English.

They traveled for trade, faith, and service, and they wrote for readers back home. That makes their accounts valuable evidence and also means each one shows the world from one traveler's point of view.

AP Thinking, Evidence

The accounts are evidence twice. What they describe tells us about distant places. The fact that so many were written tells us contact was growing. Connectivity changes what societies know.

BeReady: 10-Second Takeaway

Bigger networks meant more contact, and more contact meant diffusion and adaptation. Buddhism, Hinduism, and Islam spread, mostly through merchants and scholars. Paper and gunpowder moved west and were reshaped by the societies that took them in. Trade built cities like Hangzhou and Samarkand, while the Mongol sack of Baghdad in 1258 showed that connected cities could also fall. And travelers like Ibn Battuta, Marco Polo, and Margery Kempe wrote down the connected world they crossed.
