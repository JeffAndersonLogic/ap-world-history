### Source Set A: The Spread of Buddhism Across Asia

Image: `assets/images/maps/foundations-2/buddhism-spread-asia.webp`

**1. (q001, F2, causation)** The map shows Buddhism established in the Silk Road oasis towns of Kashgar, Khotan, Kucha and Dunhuang before it is established in China. What does that sequence best indicate about how Buddhism traveled?

- A) It moved outward from Chinese monasteries, so the oasis towns were supplied by missions sent from Chang’an.
- B) It moved with pilgrims returning from China, so the oasis towns kept teachings that China had since dropped.
- **C) It moved along established commercial routes, so towns that served merchants received it before China did.**
- D) It moved ahead of merchants along military roads, so garrison towns received it before the Chinese interior.

  - _A:_ This reverses the direction. The map’s arrows run toward China, not outward from it.
  - _B:_ Pilgrims did travel back west, but that is a later movement and does not explain why the oases are dated earlier.
  - _C:_ Correct. The oasis towns were commercial waypoints first. Buddhism reached them early because it moved with the people and institutions that used the route.
  - _D:_ The route was commercial before it was military, and the map shows no army carrying the tradition east.

**2. (q002, F2, comparison)** The map traces Theravada, Mahayana and Vajrayana along different routes and from different centuries. Which conclusion does that pattern best support?

- **A) A belief system can take distinct forms as separate networks carry it into different regions.**
- B) A tradition’s form followed climate, so the colors match the environments each route crossed.
- C) Each region received the same tradition, and the colors record the order in which it arrived.
- D) A tradition divides once a state adopts it, so the colors track episodes of royal patronage.

  - _A:_ Correct. Different routes, different centuries and different receiving societies produced Theravada, Mahayana and Vajrayana as distinct forms.
  - _B:_ Vajrayana and Mahayana both crossed varied environments. Climate does not sort the colors.
  - _C:_ The three colors are three traditions, not three arrival dates for one tradition.
  - _D:_ Patronage mattered a great deal, but the map separates the colors by route and date rather than by ruler.

**3. (q003, F2, continuity and change)** Mahayana Buddhism is marked in China around the first century CE, in Korea around the fourth, and in Japan around the sixth. What does that spacing best show?

- A) Diffusion stalled wherever a strong state existed, which held it back in Korea and Japan.
- B) Diffusion depended on conquest, so each date marks the arrival of a Buddhist army.
- **C) Diffusion continued across centuries through connections that already linked those regions.**
- D) Diffusion ran fastest by sea, so the dates record new maritime routes opening east.

  - _A:_ Korea and Japan both had organized states, and both adopted the tradition rather than blocking it.
  - _B:_ No Buddhist army is involved in this diffusion, which is one of the points the map is used to make.
  - _C:_ Correct. Five centuries separate the first date from the last, and the tradition moved along ties that already ran between China, Korea and Japan.
  - _D:_ The Mahayana route to China on this map is the overland one through the oases.

**4. (q004, F2, causation)** Short arrows fan out across South Asia in the third century BCE, the century in which Ashoka funded monasteries and sent out missions. Which mechanism of spread does that pattern best illustrate?

- A) Merchants carrying a tradition along the trade routes into neighboring commercial markets.
- B) Monks migrating away from persecution toward regions holding no established authority.
- **C) Rulers spending state resources on a tradition and extending it inside their own territory.**
- D) Farmers taking up a tradition that was believed to protect harvests in irrigated valleys.

  - _A:_ Merchants carried Buddhism a long way, but these arrows are short and internal, which is the signature of patronage rather than trade.
  - _B:_ The arrows spread from a center of power outward, which is not the shape of flight from persecution.
  - _C:_ Correct. Royal patronage is a distinct mechanism, and it is the one that fits a burst of short-range expansion in Ashoka’s century.
  - _D:_ The map records where the tradition became established, not agricultural practice.

- C) Finding a single written script able to record every language spoken across the territory.
- **D) Gathering information and enforcing decisions across distances a messenger needs weeks to cross.**

**8. (q008, F4, causation)** One set of arrows on the map runs from April to September and a second set runs from November to February in the opposite direction. Why did that pattern matter so much to merchants?

- **A) It let them plan an outward voyage and a return voyage around winds they could count on.**
- B) It let them sail in whatever direction they chose at any point in the year without waiting.
- C) It let them keep clear of coastal ports and cross open water between any two harbours.
- D) It let them carry heavier cargo, because these winds were gentler than Mediterranean gales.

  - _A:_ Correct. Predictability is the whole mechanism. A wind that reverses on schedule turns a one-way gamble into a planned round trip.
  - _B:_ The reversal is seasonal, so waiting for the turn was exactly what merchants had to do.

  - _D:_ Monsoon winds are strong, and cargo capacity was set by the ships rather than by the weather.

**9. (q009, F4, causation)** The map traces a route running from Kilwa through Aden and Calicut to Melaka and Quanzhou. Historians call this relay trade. What does that term describe?

- A) Goods traveling under naval escort, each state guarding the leg inside its own waters.
- **B) Goods passing between merchants who each specialized in one leg of the longer route.**
- C) Goods moving on a fixed timetable, with ships leaving every port on the same date.
- D) Goods carried the entire distance by crews who repaired their vessels at each port.

  - _A:_ No state controlled the whole ocean, which is part of why the relay structure existed.
  - _B:_ Correct. No single ship ran Kilwa to Quanzhou. The route worked because it was a chain of specialists, each with local knowledge.
  - _C:_ Departures followed the monsoon, and the monsoon does not arrive on the same date at every port.
  - _D:_ This is the misconception the term relay is meant to correct.

**10. (q010, F4, causation)** A merchant who sailed east in June could not sail home until the winds reversed months later. Which effect of that delay did the most to change the port cities themselves?

- **C) Merchants stayed a whole season, and resident foreign communities grew up in the ports.**
- D) Merchants raised their prices, and the ports lost their share of long-distance trade.

  - _A:_ The route grew rather than emptied over this period.
  - _B:_ Some ports were fortified, but the seasonal wait is not what drove it.
  - _C:_ Correct. Waiting for a wind means living somewhere. That is how quarters of foreign merchants, and then intermarriage, language and religion, entered the port cities.
  - _D:_ Prices moved, though that changes ledgers rather than the make-up of a city.
