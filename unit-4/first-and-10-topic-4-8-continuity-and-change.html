First & 10 Reading

Economic Change, Social Consequence

Topic 4.8 asks one synthesis question: how did economic developments from c. 1450 to c. 1750 affect social structures over time? Plantation growth, silver extraction, mercantilism, and intensified production changed demand for labor, created or reshaped hierarchies, altered elite power, and affected families — while older peasant, artisan, and elite structures also persisted.

Continuity & ChangeCausationArgumentation

Before You Read

For every economic development, ask what happened to people and hierarchy: who had to work, who gained wealth or status, whose family or community changed, and which older structures remained useful enough to survive.

Reading Target

By the end, you should be able to connect specific economic developments to changes in labor, slavery, casta and elite power, or gender/family patterns, then write a continuity-and-change argument explaining why some social structures changed while others persisted.

1

Economic Development

A Larger Economy Creates New Demands

From c. 1450 to c. 1750, global silver flows, mercantilist competition, chartered companies, plantation agriculture, and growing demand for textiles and other consumer goods expanded production and moved wealth through new channels.

Those economic developments mattered socially because they changed who needed labor, who controlled revenue, and who could convert economic advantage into political or social privilege. Topic 4.8 is about that economic development -> social structure mechanism.

AP Thinking, Causation

Do not list an economic change and a social change separately. Explain the connecting mechanism: new demand or revenue -> changed labor, status, family, or elite power.

2

Labor and Slavery

Plantations and Extraction Reshape Labor Status

Expanding plantation economies increased demand for enslaved labor in the Americas, expanding hereditary racialized chattel slavery. Silver mining and colonial agriculture also relied on systems such as the mita, encomienda, and hacienda.

At the same time, older peasant and artisan labor continued and often intensified in Europe, India, and China as global demand for wool, linen, cotton, silk, food, and manufactured goods grew.

AP Thinking, Continuity & Change

Change: Atlantic plantation slavery expanded dramatically. Continuity: most production still depended on peasant and artisan labor, often working harder for larger markets.

3

Hierarchy

Economic Change Creates New Elites and Reworks Old Ones

Colonial conquest and extraction contributed to new hierarchies such as the Spanish American casta system, where ancestry, legal status, wealth, and access to office became intertwined.

Existing elites also adapted. Local nobles, merchants, landowners, company directors, and state servants could gain or lose influence depending on how well they fit new imperial and commercial systems. Centralizing rulers sometimes weakened independent elites while creating new routes to status through service or court access.

AP Thinking, Social Structure

Economic opportunity creates hierarchy when wealth or access to revenue becomes a route to status — and when states write those differences into law or officeholding.

4

Family and Synthesis

Demography Changes Families — but Not Everywhere in the Same Way

The Atlantic trade in enslaved people changed demographics in parts of Africa and could alter gender and family responsibilities, household labor, and marriage patterns. Forced migration also created new communities and cultural synthesis in the Americas.

But the period did not produce one universal social transformation. Older labor systems, patriarchal institutions, village structures, and elite groups persisted because expanding states and markets often still depended on them.

A strong Unit 4 synthesis therefore argues for uneven change: economic developments transformed slavery, colonial hierarchy, and some family patterns while intensifying or adapting older peasant, artisan, and elite structures.

AP Thinking, CCOT

The governing claim should answer degree: where did economic change produce major social restructuring, and where did older structures persist because the new economy still needed them?

BeReady: 10-Second Takeaway

Economic developments from 1450 to 1750 affected social structures unevenly. Plantation growth and extractive economies expanded coerced labor and Atlantic chattel slavery; colonial conquest and commerce helped create new casta and merchant elites; the slave trade altered gender and family patterns in parts of Africa. At the same time, peasant and artisan labor continued and intensified, and many existing elites adapted to centralizing states and expanding markets. The Unit 4 CCOT answer is therefore both change and continuity, explained through the economic mechanisms that made each possible.
