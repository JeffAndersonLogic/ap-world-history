First & 10 Reading

Follow the Trade to the People

Between 1450 and 1750, ships joined the two halves of the world, and new trade in sugar, silver, and cloth needed more workers than ever. That changed who did the work, who rose to the top, and how families lived, most of all where the work was forced. But most people still farmed, and much of the old world kept working the old way, only harder.

1

The Question

Today You Are the Historian

Topic 4.7 ended with a question. Trade, work, and the ladder itself all moved between 1450 and 1750. Which change mattered most, and what stayed the same?

This topic gives you no new empire and no new war. It gives you a question, and you already have the evidence from Topics 4.1 to 4.7. The College Board asks how economic developments, big changes in how people made, bought, and sold things, affected social structures between 1450 and 1750. A social structure is the way a society is organized: who does the work, who rules and rises, and how families and communities live. Your job is to answer with an argument, and the trick is to follow the trade all the way to the people.

None of it could happen until ships could cross an ocean and come back (Topic 4.1). Europe built those ships from borrowed knowledge: the compass from China; the astrolabe, a Greek idea that astronomers in the Islamic world made precise; tables of the sky built by Muslim and Jewish astronomers in Toledo; and the triangular lateen sail, common across the Mediterranean and the seas of the Islamic world. It built new ships around them, the caravel and the carrack, and around 1600 the Dutch fluyt, which carried a large cargo with a small, cheap crew and made shipping goods much cheaper. And its sailors learned the Atlantic's winds, swinging far out to sea to catch a wind that blew them home. Once ships went and came back again and again, the two halves of the world were joined, and trade was transformed.

AP Thinking, Continuity and Change

Name both halves. The question asks how economic developments affected social structures over time. A strong answer says what changed, what stayed the same, and why. As you read, keep two lists: changes and continuities.

2

The Money

Sugar, Silver, and Cloth

Three economic developments came down that ocean road. The first was new demand. Europeans wanted sugar and tobacco grown on plantations, huge farms in the Americas growing one crop to sell (Topic 4.3). Buyers in Asia, Africa, and Europe wanted cotton cloth woven in India and silk woven in China.

The second was silver. From 1545 the mountain at Potosí in the Andes, and from 1546 the mines at Zacatecas in Mexico, poured out silver, and a large share of it ended up in China, where the government collected most of its taxes in silver (Topic 4.5). The third was a set of tools that rulers used to pull that wealth toward themselves: mercantilist laws, and chartered companies such as the Dutch East India Company, the VOC.

The money was worth fighting over, and rivals did. A Portuguese fleet defeated an Egyptian and Gujarati fleet off Diu, in India, in 1509. Morocco's army crossed the Sahara and defeated the Songhai Empire at Tondibi in 1591, partly to take control of West Africa's gold and salt trade. VOC officials on the spice island of Ambon executed English traders in 1623. And England and the Dutch Republic fought three wars at sea between 1652 and 1674, largely over trade and shipping.

AP Thinking, Continuity and Change

Do not stop at the trade. New demand, silver, and rulers' tools are the economic developments, and ships were the road they came down. They are the start of the answer, not the end. The question is what they did to people, so the next step is always: who had to do the work?

3

The Workers

More Demand Meant More Work

All that demand meant someone had to grow more, mine more, and make more. The College Board's words are that "the demand for labor intensified." It was met in three ways.

Plantations and the slave trade. Sugar needed enormous numbers of workers, and planters chose enslaved Africans. Historians estimate that in the whole 1500s, fewer than 300,000 Africans were forced onto ships bound for the Americas. In the fifty years from 1701 to 1750, more than 2.5 million were (Topic 4.4). On the plantations they were held in chattel slavery: owned as property for life, and their children too.

Old forced labor, put to new work. The Inca had required communities to send people to work for the state in turns, a duty called the mit’a. In the 1570s, Spain turned it into a draft that sent thousands of Andean men each year to the silver mine at Potosí (Topic 4.4).

Peasants and artisans, working more. In western Europe, merchants paid families in the countryside to spin and weave wool and linen at home. In India, weavers made more and more cotton cloth for the European companies. In China, peasant families raised silkworms and wove silk, some of which was traded for American silver, and they needed silver to pay most of their taxes (Topic 4.5). This is peasant and artisan labor. The work was old. There was more of it, for buyers farther away.

AP Thinking, Continuity and Change

One change can hold a continuity. The mit’a was an old Inca duty with a new master and a new job. Peasant and artisan work was old work done in larger amounts. When you find a case like this, say both halves: what was old, and what was new.

4

What Changed

Masters, Servants, and Slaves

In September 1647 an Englishman named Richard Ligon landed on Barbados, a small English island in the Caribbean. He found that sugar-making was still new there. Planters had been making sugar for only a few years, using cane plants brought from Brazil, and were still learning to make it well. He lived on a sugar plantation that his traveling companion, Thomas Modyford, had bought a half share of, helped run it, and stayed about three years. In the book he published in 1657, he wrote that "the Island is divided into three sorts of men, viz. Masters, Servants, and slaves." ("Viz." means "namely.") The masters were the planters, the plantation owners. The servants were indentured servants, most of them poor people from England, Scotland, and Ireland, bound to work for a set number of years, many of them to pay for their voyage. But "the slaves and their posterity," meaning their children and grandchildren, were "subject to their Masters for ever."

Sugar made the masters rich. Ligon wrote that one planter, James Drax, had begun on the island with no more than 300 pounds and had raised a great fortune, "all by this plant of Sugar." Over the next few decades, the planters bought more and more enslaved Africans, and in 1679 a count of the island found about 37,700 enslaved people and about 20,000 white settlers. Sugar had made a new kind of society: a small, rich group of planters at the top, and an enslaved majority at the bottom, held for life, by inheritance, and by race.

In Spanish America, the silver mines and the great estates sat on top of a mixed society of Spaniards, Indigenous peoples, and Africans, and their children. Spanish colonial society ranked people by their ancestry, the casta system, and silver, land, and trade made many creole families, people of Spanish ancestry born in the Americas, rich (Topic 4.7). The empire shaped its peoples, and its peoples shaped the empire: new categories grew up for the mixed society in its colonies.

The same trade changed Africa. About two of every three people carried away in the Atlantic slave trade were male. In parts of Africa, especially West Central Africa, that left villages with more women than men, and historians argue that women did more of the farming and that more women and girls were enslaved inside Africa itself (Topic 4.5). The trade made some African states stronger: Asante rose by controlling gold and buying guns from Dutch and English traders (Topic 4.4). It could also tear at a kingdom. In 1526, Afonso I of Kongo complained that Portuguese traders were carrying off "the children of our nobles and vassals."

AP Thinking, Argumentation

Connect the evidence. Ligon's Barbados is one place. When you find a new hierarchy in one place, look for the same pattern somewhere else you have studied, and ask what economic development built each one. A pattern found in two places is stronger evidence than one case alone.

5

What Stayed

The Old World Kept Working

Now look at what did not change. Most people still farmed. In 1750, as in 1450, most of the world's people grew food, and the College Board's own sentence about this period begins with that fact: the world's work "continued to be heavily centered on agriculture."

Much of the work itself stayed the same. A spinner in Holland, a weaver in India, and a silk family in China did work their grandparents would have known, at home and as a family, with women and girls doing much of the spinning and weaving. Global demand made them work more. It did not replace them.

And the ladder stayed. Almost everywhere, a ruler who inherited the throne still sat at the top, and old nobles kept most of their privileges even when stronger kings squeezed them (Topic 4.7). Older forms of slavery went on inside Africa, and Asian merchants kept the Indian Ocean trading (Topic 4.4). So the continuity is large: most people, most work, and most rulers looked much as they had in 1450. The change was real too, but it was not the same everywhere. Where was it deepest, and why? That is the question your thesis answers.

AP Thinking, Continuity and Change

Change can sit inside a continuity. A Chinese silk family was still a peasant family, working at home in its village. But it now needed silver for most of its taxes and sold silk to buyers far away. Name the continuity, then the change inside it.

6

The Argument

From Evidence to a Thesis

A list of evidence is not an argument. An argument explains how the pieces of evidence relate to each other. Three relationships do most of the work. The first is this led to that: Europe's demand for sugar led to plantations in Barbados and Brazil, which led to a huge slave trade, which left villages in West Central Africa with more women than men. One chain links three continents. The second is the same pattern, two places: a spinner in Holland and a weaver in India both did old work in larger amounts, for buyers far away. The third is a contrast: an Andean man drafted through the mit’a was taken far from his village to dig silver, while a weaver in India kept working at home, making more cloth than before for buyers overseas. A contrast shows where the new trade changed people's lives most and where it changed them least, and that is what lets an answer say how much changed.

Put the evidence together, and you can write a thesis, a claim that answers the question and that you can defend with evidence. A strong thesis names a change, a continuity, and the reason for the difference: where did the new trade change social structures most, where did it change them least, and why? You can argue more than one thesis from the same evidence. What you cannot do is leave out the evidence on the other side.

Economic change reached into everyday social life: who worked, who ruled, and how families and communities were organized. How much it changed is the argument you make, with evidence.

By 1750 the oceans were joined and states were stronger than ever. Next: who do those states belong to? That is Unit 5.

AP Thinking, Argumentation

Test your thesis. A strong thesis answers the whole question (how economic developments affected social structures, and how much stayed the same), takes a position someone could argue against, and points to the evidence you will use. If yours only lists changes, add the continuity.

BeReady: 10-Second Takeaway

Between 1450 and 1750, ships built from borrowed knowledge joined the two halves of the world, and new demand for sugar, silver, and cloth needed more workers than ever. Planters bought millions of enslaved Africans, Spain drafted Andean men for the silver mines, and peasants and artisans in Europe, India, and China worked more for faraway buyers. Where the work was forced, society was remade: Barbados became a society of masters, servants, and slaves, Spanish America ranked people by ancestry, and the slave trade changed families and states in Africa. But most people still farmed, much of the work stayed family work done the old way, and rulers and nobles stayed on top. Economic change reached into everyday social life, and how much it changed is the argument you make with evidence.
