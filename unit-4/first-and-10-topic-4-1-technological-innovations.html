First & 10 Reading

Getting Home

In the Indian Ocean the wind turned around on a schedule. In the Atlantic it never did, so the hard part of an ocean voyage was getting home. Europe solved that with tools borrowed from older worlds, new ships, and a map of the wind, and the ocean became a road that ran both ways.

ContextualizationCausation

The Story to Hold Onto

Borrowed tools → New ships → Learn the wind → The ocean goes both ways → New routes for trade and travel. Every example in this reading is one link in that chain.

Question for Today

How did knowledge from older worlds let European ships cross the ocean and come back, and what changed when they could?

1

The Problem

Getting There Was Easy. Getting Home Was Not.

In Unit 2 you saw how the Indian Ocean ran on a schedule. The monsoon winds blow from the northeast in winter and turn around to blow from the southwest in summer. A merchant could sail to East Africa, trade, wait a few months, and ride the reversed wind home. Indian Ocean sailors already used the compass and the astrolabe, and in the early 1400s the Ming admiral Zheng He led large fleets as far as East Africa.

Now stand on the coast of Portugal around 1430. The Atlantic Ocean is in front of you, and it does not work like that. Off the northwest coast of Africa, the trade winds blow from the northeast nearly all year. Sailing south along the coast is easy. Sailing back north means fighting that same wind the whole way, and it never turns around for you. Far from the coast there are no islands to hop between and no landmarks to steer by.

So the hardest part of exploring the Atlantic was not getting somewhere new. It was getting home again. Every tool in this reading is part of the answer to that problem.

AP Thinking, Contextualization

Describe the setting before the change. The context for this topic is an older world of ocean travel that Europe sat at the edge of. Say what Indian Ocean sailors already knew, and why the Atlantic was a harder problem.

2

Borrowed Tools

Most of Europe's Sea Tools Came From Older Worlds

Europe solved its problem mostly with tools it did not invent. The magnetic compass came from China, where sailors were using a magnetized needle at sea by about 1100. Europeans were writing about the compass by about 1190. Historians still debate how it traveled west, but it gave sailors a direction to steer when clouds hid the sun and stars.

The astrolabe began as an idea of ancient Greek astronomers. Astronomers in the Islamic world developed it into a precise instrument, and it reached Europe through Muslim Spain. Portuguese pilots then turned it into a heavy, simple brass version that could be held up on a rolling deck to measure how high the sun stood at noon. By the 1480s they were using it at sea to find their latitude, how far north or south of the equator they were.

A sun reading only helps if you know where the sun should be on that day, so pilots also needed astronomical tables. Those had a family tree, too. Muslim and Jewish astronomers in Toledo, in Spain, built tables of the sky. Astronomers working for a king of Castile built new tables on them. Then a Jewish astronomer named Abraham Zacuto produced an almanac that was printed in Portugal in 1496 and carried on Portuguese voyages.

Finally, the triangular lateen sail lets a ship use wind coming from the side and work its way forward at an angle against it. It was not new. Sailors had used it on the Mediterranean since Roman times, and it was common across the seas of the Islamic world.

AP Thinking, Contextualization

Name the world each tool came from. The College Board says this knowledge came from the Classical, Islamic, and Asian worlds. A strong answer names a tool and its world: the compass from China, the astrolabe from Greek and Islamic astronomy, the lateen sail from the Mediterranean and the Islamic seas.

3

New Ships

Three Ships, Three Jobs

Europe's own contribution was putting these tools together and building new ships to carry them. Each ship did a different job.

The caravel was the explorer. It was small and light, with lateen sails, so it could work against the wind and slip along unknown coastlines. Later caravels added square sails as well.

The carrack was the long-haul ship. It was large, with square sails for running in front of the wind and a lateen sail at the back, and it had room for months of food, water, cargo, and guns.

The fluyt came much later. Dutch shipbuilders designed it around 1600 to do one job very well: carry a large cargo with a small, cheap crew. That is what made ocean trade pay off voyage after voyage.

AP Thinking, Causation

Tie each ship to the problem it solved. Do not just list caravel, carrack, and fluyt. Say what each one made possible: exploring unknown coasts, surviving long voyages, or making trade cheap enough to keep going.

4

The Wind

The One Thing Nobody Could Borrow

The last piece could not be borrowed, because no one had needed it before: a map of the Atlantic's winds. Over decades of voyages in the 1400s, Portuguese sailors learned that the winds of the North Atlantic turn in a giant wheel. Near Africa, the trade winds blow toward the southwest. Farther north, the westerlies blow back toward Europe.

So to get home from West Africa, a ship did something that feels backward. Instead of fighting the wind along the coast, it sailed away from land, out west and north into the open ocean, until it reached the westerlies, and then it rode them home. Portuguese sailors called this loop the volta do mar, roughly "the turn of the sea."

The loop only worked with the other tools. A pilot needed latitude readings to know when to turn, and a ship that could stay at sea for weeks. That is why the College Board lists new tools, new ship designs, and knowledge of winds and currents together.

AP Thinking, Causation

Show how the pieces combine. No single tool made ocean voyages possible. The mechanism is the combination: instruments to know where you are, ships to survive the trip, and wind knowledge to find the way home.

5

The Payoff

The Ocean Became a Road Both Ways

Put it all together and the ocean became a road that ran in both directions. In 1488, Bartolomeu Dias reached the southern tip of Africa. In 1492, Columbus rode the trade winds west across the Atlantic and the westerlies home. In 1497, Vasco da Gama swung so far out into the Atlantic that his ships saw no land for about three months. He rounded Africa and reached India in 1498.

That changed the patterns of trade and travel. Ships could now carry goods and people by sea between Europe and Asia, and across the Atlantic between the Eastern and Western Hemispheres, which had been almost entirely cut off from each other. Trade began to move along ocean routes that had not existed before.

Here is the twist. In 1400 the biggest ships and the longest voyages were in the Indian Ocean and China, not Europe. Europe faced a harder ocean and solved it by combining knowledge from older worlds. The breakthrough was a combination, not a European invention.

Ships could now go and come back. But every voyage cost a fortune. Who paid for them, and what did they want? That is the next topic.

AP Thinking, Causation

Finish the chain. The College Board's question does not stop at the tools. It asks how they changed patterns of trade and travel. End your answer with a route that opened and what could now move along it.

BeReady: 10-Second Takeaway

The Atlantic's wind did not turn around, so the hard part was getting home. Europe borrowed tools from older worlds (the compass from China, the astrolabe from Greek and Islamic astronomy, the lateen sail from the Mediterranean and the Islamic seas), built new ships (caravel, carrack, fluyt), and learned the Atlantic's wind wheel (the volta do mar). Together they made the ocean a two-way road, and new routes for trade and travel opened around Africa to Asia and across the Atlantic.
