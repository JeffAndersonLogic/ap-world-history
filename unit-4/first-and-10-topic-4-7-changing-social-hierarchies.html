Who Belongs, Who Rises

Between 1450 and 1750, every society still ranked people from top to bottom. What changed was who stood where. Empires decided which groups could belong and which were pushed out, conquest and new trade put new groups at the top, and stronger rulers squeezed the old nobles who used to share their power.

The Ladder

The Ladder Stays, the People on It Move

Topic 4.6 ended with a question. Some people resisted the new order. Everyone was sorted by it. Who ended up where?

Every society you have studied ranked people. There were rulers and nobles at the top, priests, scholars, and merchants in the middle, and farmers, laborers, and the enslaved at the bottom. Between 1450 and 1750, that did not change. Nobody in this period built a society of equals, and almost everywhere a king, sultan, tsar, or emperor who inherited his throne still sat on top. That is the continuity.

What changed was who stood on which rung, and why. Empires that conquered new lands and grew rich from new trade made three kinds of change. They decided who could belong: which religious and ethnic groups they welcomed or made use of, and which they pushed out or held down. They created new elites, new groups at the top. And they squeezed old elites, the nobles and warriors who had shared power with earlier rulers.

2

Who Belongs: Pushed Out and Welcomed

Spain Pushes Out, the Ottomans Take In

In 1492, King Ferdinand and Queen Isabella of Spain ordered every Jew in their kingdoms to become Christian or leave by the end of July. Tens of thousands left, and many more converted and stayed. Four years later, Portugal's king ordered the same, and in 1497, instead of letting most of them go, he had them baptized by force. Spain and Portugal wanted one religion in each kingdom, and they suppressed difference by pushing a whole group out or forcing it to convert.

At the other end of the Mediterranean, the Ottoman sultan Bayezid II let the refugees settle in his empire. Thousands did, in Istanbul, in Salonica (today Thessaloniki, in Greece), and in other cities. Salonica became a city where Jews were the largest group, and it stayed that way for centuries. Why would a Muslim sultan take them in? Because they were useful: merchants, doctors, craftsmen, and taxpayers. Jewish refugees soon set up Istanbul's first printing press. Not every exile went east, though. Many went to Portugal, North Africa, or Italy.

The Ottoman Empire ruled Muslims, Orthodox and Armenian Christians, and Jews. Under Islamic law, Christians and Jews were protected subjects. They could practice their religion, and their own religious leaders ran their marriages, inheritances, schools, and family courts, and answered to the sultan for their community's taxes and good behavior. This is accommodation: making room for a group's differences. But it was not equality. Christians and Jews paid a special tax, the jizya. In an Islamic court, their word counted for less against a Muslim's. They could not freely build new churches or synagogues, and the top jobs in the army and government went to Muslims.

AP Thinking, Continuity and Change

Room is not the same as equal. The College Board says states accommodated diversity or used what different groups could contribute. The Ottomans did both: they kept the peace among many faiths, and they gained skilled, taxpaying subjects. What stayed the same was that Muslims ranked above everyone else.

3

Who Belongs: It Depends

Rulers Change Their Minds, and Class Changes Everything

In India, the Mughal emperors were Muslims ruling a mostly Hindu land. The emperor Akbar, who ruled from 1556 to 1605, did more than tolerate Hindus. He ended the jizya in 1564, married daughters of Hindu Rajputs, the warrior kings of northwestern India, and made Rajput princes some of the highest-ranking commanders and officials in his empire. He needed their armies and their loyalty, and he got them. A century later, his great-grandson Aurangzeb brought the jizya back, in 1679, though he still employed many Hindu officers. Same empire, same groups, opposite policy. Making room for a group was a choice a ruler made, and the next ruler could unmake it.

Inside the Ottoman Empire, being a woman meant very different things depending on your class. At the very top, women of the sultan's family held real power. Hürrem began as an enslaved woman in the palace and became the wife of Sultan Suleiman. She paid for a mosque, a school, a soup kitchen, and a hospital in Istanbul. Later, Kösem ran the government as regent for her young sons and grandson, for years at a time between the 1620s and 1651. In the towns, ordinary women owned property and went to the Islamic court to claim their inheritances and debts, and court records show them winning. But the law still treated them unequally: a daughter inherited half what a son did, and a woman's word in court counted for less than a man's. Many other women were enslaved, as servants and concubines, with almost no say over their own lives.

AP Thinking, Continuity and Change

Watch for change inside one empire. Akbar ended the jizya and Aurangzeb brought it back. A queen mother could run the empire while an enslaved woman in the same palace could not leave it. Before you say what an empire did to a group, ask: which ruler, and which members of the group?

4

New Elites

Conquest and Trade Make a New Top

In 1644 the Manchus, a people from north of the Great Wall, took Beijing and founded the Qing dynasty. They were a small minority ruling a huge Han Chinese majority. They kept the Chinese way of governing: the civil service exams, the Confucian officials, and the old law codes, and Han Chinese scholars still filled most government jobs. But they also built a new elite at the top: the banners, hereditary military units of Manchus, Mongols, and Han families who had joined the Manchus early. Bannermen lived in walled quarters inside major cities, were paid by the state, and had legal privileges that ordinary Han Chinese did not.

The Qing also held the Han majority in place. In 1645 they ordered every Han man to shave the front of his head and wear his hair in a long braid, the Manchu queue, or be killed. Many top posts in the capital went to two officials, one Manchu and one Han, so the Manchu could watch the Han. A long line of ditches and willow fences in the northeast kept Han farmers out of the Manchu homeland. These were restrictions on the majority, made by a new elite that wanted to stay on top.

In Spanish America, conquest had brought together Spaniards, Indigenous peoples, and Africans, and their children. The Spanish ranked people by where they were born and by their ancestry, and this ranking is called the casta system. At the top were peninsulares, people born in Spain. The viceroys who ruled for the king were almost always Spanish-born. Next came creoles, people of Spanish ancestry born in the Americas. Silver mines, huge estates, and trade made many creole families rich, a new economic elite, and they resented the newcomers from Spain who got the highest jobs. Below them were people of mixed ancestry, such as mestizos (Spanish and Indigenous) and mulattos (Spanish and African); Indigenous people, who paid tribute to the crown and had their own town governments; and people of African ancestry, some free and many enslaved.

In the 1700s, artists in Mexico painted sets of casta paintings. Each painting showed a mother, a father, and their child, with a label for each person's category, as if society were a neat chart. Real life was messier. Most of the many labels in the paintings did not appear in official records, and how a person was recorded could change with their wealth, job, clothes, and reputation. But ancestry still opened or closed doors, and the top of the ladder belonged to people whose families came from Spain.

Common Mistake to Avoid

Do not treat a casta painting as a census. The paintings show how elite viewers wanted to see colonial society: sorted, labeled, and in order. As you read about any ranking system, ask who made the picture of it, and whether real people fit the picture.

5

Old Elites Squeezed

Cavalrymen, Boyars, and Nobles at Court

For centuries, the core of the Ottoman army was the sipahi, a cavalryman who held a timar: the right to collect the taxes from a piece of land, in return for riding to war when the sultan called. A timar was not his to own, and it was not supposed to pass automatically to his son. By the late 1500s, wars were won by soldiers with guns who were paid in cash, and the sultan needed money more than horsemen. So the state turned more and more timar land into tax farms: the right to collect a district's taxes, sold for cash to whoever would pay. In 1695 it began selling them for life. The sipahis faded. Some families held on to their timars, and the system was not abolished until 1831, but the sipahis no longer led the army, and tax farmers and powerful local families rose in their place.

In Russia, the boyars were the great noble families. They sat on the tsar's council and fought over seats by family rank, until that system was ended in 1682. Then Peter the Great, who ruled from 1682 to 1725, went further. He replaced the old council of boyars with a Senate he appointed, ordered nobles to shave their beards and dress like western Europeans, and made noblemen serve the state, in the army or the government, for life. His Table of Ranks of 1722 set out fourteen ranks of service. A man's place depended on the rank he earned, and a commoner who rose high enough became a noble. The word "boyar" faded away. The nobles did not: they kept their estates and their power over their serfs.

In France, Louis XIV had never forgotten the Fronde (Topic 4.6). He sent royal officials called intendants to run the provinces, and in 1682 he moved his court and government to his palace at Versailles, where great nobles spent their days near the king, competing for his favor instead of raising armies at home. The French nobility kept their titles, their land, and their freedom from the main tax. What they lost was the power to defy the king. But the power of old elites could rise as well as fall. In England in 1688, a group of nobles invited William of Orange to bring an army, King James II fled, and Parliament, a body of landowning nobles and gentry, set limits on the monarchs who came after him. There, the old elite won.

People argued about all this at the time. In the 1550s, Ogier Ghiselin de Busbecq, an ambassador sent to the Ottoman sultan by a European emperor, wrote home that among the Turks "it is by merit that men rise in the service," while in Europe "birth is the standard for everything." Was he right? That is a claim you can test with evidence.

AP Thinking, Claims and Evidence in Sources

Test a source's argument. A piece of evidence can support an argument (it fits), modify it (it is true, but only for some people or some places), or refute it (it shows the argument is wrong). Before you decide, look back through this reading for one piece of evidence about Ottoman officials and one about European nobles. Question 3 asks you to do this with Busbecq.

6

The Pattern

What Stayed, What Changed

Put it together. The ladder stayed: rulers who inherited their thrones at the top, privileged nobles beneath them, most people at the bottom, and a woman's place set largely by her family's class. Who stood where changed, in three ways.

States decided who could belong. Some welcomed or made use of groups, like the Ottomans with Jewish refugees and Akbar with the Rajputs. Others pushed groups out or held them down, like Spain and Portugal with the Jews and the Qing with the Han. Conquest and new trade made new elites: the Qing banners, and the Spanish-born officials and rich creoles at the top of the casta ranking. And stronger rulers squeezed old elites: the sipahis, the boyars, and the French nobles, though old elites rarely disappeared, and in England they won.

Empires decided who could belong and who could rise: new elites appeared, old ones were squeezed, and some groups were welcomed while others were pushed out.

Trade, work, and the ladder itself all moved between 1450 and 1750. Which change mattered most, and what stayed the same? That is Topic 4.8.

BeReady: 10-Second Takeaway

Between 1450 and 1750, societies kept their ladders: rulers who inherited power, privileged nobles, and most people at the bottom. But empires changed who stood where. Spain and Portugal pushed out or forcibly converted their Jews, while the Ottomans took them in and gave Christians and Jews protection without equality. Akbar welcomed Hindu Rajputs into his elite, and Aurangzeb brought back the jizya. Ottoman women's lives depended on their class. The Qing conquest created a new elite, the banners, and held the Han majority in place with the queue. The casta system put Spanish-born officials and rich creoles at the top in Spanish America. And stronger rulers squeezed old elites: Ottoman timar holders, Russian boyars, and French nobles, though in England the nobles won.
