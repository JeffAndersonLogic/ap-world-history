What the Ships Carried

After 1492, ships crossed the Atlantic and came back, again and again. They carried crops and animals on purpose, germs, rats, and mosquitoes by accident, and enslaved Africans by force. What they carried remade populations on both sides of the ocean.

1

The Cause

Two Worlds Apart, Then a Road Between Them

In the last topic, Columbus sailed west looking for a route to Asia. He never found one. What he started instead was something nobody had planned, and that is this topic.

Start with how the world looked before 1492. Since the end of the last Ice Age, about eleven thousand years ago, ocean had separated the Americas from Africa, Asia, and Europe. Each side grew its own plants, kept its own animals, and lived with its own diseases. The Americas had maize, potatoes, cassava, tomatoes, cacao, and tobacco, but almost no large farm animals: llamas and alpacas in the Andes, and no horses, cattle, or pigs anywhere. Afro-Eurasia had wheat, rice, sugarcane, horses, cattle, pigs, and sheep. Its people had also lived for thousands of years in crowded towns beside their animals, and they lived with diseases the Americas did not have, such as smallpox and measles.

Then ships began crossing the Atlantic and, just as important, coming back, again and again, as Europeans built colonies. That new connection is the cause of the Columbian Exchange: the movement of plants, animals, and diseases between the two halves of the world. A historian named Alfred Crosby gave it that name in 1972. From the 1560s, Spanish ships also crossed the Pacific between Mexico and the Philippines, a second road between the two halves of the world.

Things crossed in three ways. Some went on purpose. Columbus's second voyage, in 1493, carried horses, cattle, pigs, wheat seed, and sugarcane, because settlers wanted the foods and animals they knew. Some went by accident: germs in sailors' bodies, rats in the cargo, and, most likely, the eggs of the mosquito that spreads yellow fever, hidden in water barrels. An animal that carries a disease from one person to another, like a mosquito, or a rat and the fleas it carries, is a disease vector. And some went by force. Enslaved Africans were carried across the Atlantic against their will, and African foods crossed on the same ships.

AP Thinking, Causation

Explain the cause, not just the list. "Plants and animals were exchanged" is a list. "Ships began crossing the ocean and coming back, so everything they carried, on purpose or by accident, went across" explains why the exchange happened.

2

Going West by Accident

The Cargo Nobody Chose

The accidents did the most harm. Smallpox reached the Caribbean in 1518 and Mexico in 1520. Measles and malaria followed. The College Board calls the way these diseases crossed "unintentional": nobody planned it, and nobody at the time knew that germs existed.

In June 1520 the people of Tenochtitlan, the Aztec capital, drove the Spaniards out of their city. That autumn, smallpox swept through it. An epidemic is a disease that spreads to many people in one place at the same time. Years later, Nahua people from the city described the epidemic for a Spanish friar, Bernardino de Sahagún. Their account says the sickness "brought great desolation; a great many died of it." People were covered in sores, and the sick lay "no longer able to move or stir." The account also says that "many just starved to death; starvation reigned, and no one took care of others any longer." Their new ruler, Cuitláhuac, died in the epidemic, most likely of smallpox. When the Spaniards came back to besiege the city in 1521, "the Mexica warriors were greatly weakened by it."

Why were these diseases so deadly here? There are two reasons, and you need both. First, nobody in the Americas had had them before. In Afro-Eurasia, most adults had survived smallpox or measles as children and could not catch them again. That protection is immunity. In the Americas, almost everyone fell sick at once, so nobody was left to farm, cook, or care for the sick. Second, the diseases did not arrive alone. They came with war, forced labor, hunger, and people driven from their homes, and sick, hungry, overworked people die more often.

Historians argue about the exact numbers. Most published estimates say central Mexico lost between about two-thirds and more than nine-tenths of its people between 1519 and 1595, and many regions of the Americas lost most of their people. That is what the College Board means by "catastrophic effects in many areas."

AP Thinking, Claims and Evidence in Sources

Find the evidence a source uses. A source makes a claim, then points to things that support it. Later, in Section 3, you will meet José de Acosta, who claims that cattle multiplied in the Americas, and his evidence is a number: one fleet carried almost a hundred thousand hides. Read the Nahua account the same way: find its claim, then the evidence it points to. Naming the evidence a source uses to support its argument is the skill this topic practices.

3

Going West on Purpose

Animals That Multiplied

What Europeans carried on purpose changed the Americas too. Pigs and cattle multiplied fast. A Spanish writer, José de Acosta, reported that wild cattle on the island of Santo Domingo roamed "by thousands," and that one Spanish fleet in 1587 carried almost a hundred thousand cattle hides home from the Caribbean and Mexico. One historian argues that in one valley of central Mexico, sheep grazed the hillsides bare.

Horses had died out in the Americas thousands of years earlier, and the Spanish brought them back. Horses spread north from Spanish settlements, and by the early 1700s peoples of the Great Plains, such as the Comanche, had built new ways of life on horseback, hunting bison and trading across huge distances.

Wheat, grapes, and fruit trees such as oranges went west as well, so that settlers could eat the foods they knew.

AP Thinking, Causation

Not every effect was a disaster. Some effects of the exchange were catastrophic, and some were new chances. The Comanche used the horse to build power on the Plains. A strong answer names the effect and the people it affected.

4

Going West by Force

Rice, Okra, and Sugar

Some of what crossed came with people who were forced to cross. African foods, including okra and African rice, crossed on slave ships, often as food for the voyage, and many enslaved Africans knew how to grow rice from farming it in West Africa. Okra was growing in Brazil by the 1650s. Enslaved people grew these foods in small garden plots to feed themselves, and the foods became part of cooking across the Americas, from Brazil to the Caribbean.

Sugarcane came from Afro-Eurasia, but the Caribbean and Brazil turned out to grow it better than almost anywhere. So Europeans built plantations: huge farms growing one crop to sell. A crop grown to sell rather than to eat at home is a cash crop. Plantations used coerced labor, people forced to work, and on sugar plantations, during the 1600s, those workers became overwhelmingly enslaved Africans. Most of the sugar was shipped to Europe. The College Board's summary for cash crops in this period is that they were exported mostly to Europe and the Middle East. Tobacco, an American plant, became a plantation crop the same way.

You may read that disease "caused" African slavery in the Americas: disease killed the workers, so Europeans brought Africans. That is too simple. Disease did empty land of workers. But before 1492, Europeans were already growing sugar with enslaved workers, including Canary Islanders and Africans, on Atlantic islands such as Madeira, and they chose to bring that system to the Americas. How that forced labor worked is Topic 4.4.

AP Thinking, Causation

Avoid a single cause. "Disease caused slavery" leaves out the choices people made. A stronger sentence: disease emptied land of workers, and Europeans who already used enslaved Africans on island plantations chose to bring that system to the Americas.

5

Going East

New Foods for Afro-Eurasia

Going east, the exchange was mostly a benefit. American crops became staple crops, the main foods people eat every day, in parts of Europe, Asia, and Africa, because they grew where the old crops struggled. Maize grew on dry land. Potatoes grew in cold, wet ground. Cassava grew in poor soil and survived drought. Sweet potatoes grew on hillsides too steep for rice.

Sweet potatoes reached China by the Pacific road: Chinese merchants brought them from the Spanish Philippines, and in southern China officials promoted them in 1594, a famine year. Near the mouth of the Congo River, in Central Africa, Portuguese traders brought cassava from Brazil, and farmers took it up. Cassava survives drought and locusts, which made it a food to fall back on. In Ireland, the potato had become a main food by the 1700s.

Nutrition is how well food feeds the body. A family that planted these crops had more food, more kinds of food, and something to eat when the old harvest failed. Over the next two centuries, these crops helped populations grow in China and Europe, and they fed many people in parts of Africa even as the slave trade carried millions away.

AP Thinking, Causation

Connect the crop to the people. Not "maize spread to Africa," but "maize grew on land where the old grains struggled, so families had more food and more people survived." Cause first, then the effect on people.

6

The Pattern

One Connection, Two Different Results

Put it together. In the Americas, germs that nobody meant to carry killed millions of people, and the animals and crops that people did mean to carry remade the land. In Afro-Eurasia, American crops meant more food and better nutrition.

The ships carried more than anyone meant them to, and what they carried remade populations on both sides of the ocean.

It also left a question. The Americas had lost many of their people, and the new plantations needed more workers than ever. Who would do the work, and who would rule? That is Topic 4.4.

AP Thinking, Causation

Answer both halves. The College Board asks for the causes of the Columbian Exchange and its effects on both hemispheres. Name the cause (ships crossing and coming back, carrying living things, some by accident), then one effect in the Americas and one in Afro-Eurasia.

BeReady: 10-Second Takeaway

Once ships crossed the Atlantic, and later the Pacific, and came back again and again, plants, animals, and germs crossed with them: some on purpose, some by accident, and some with enslaved Africans carried by force. Smallpox, measles, and malaria had catastrophic effects in many parts of the Americas, made worse by war, forced labor, and hunger, and the ships also carried disease vectors such as rats and mosquitoes. Horses, pigs, cattle, wheat, and sugarcane remade American land, enslaved Africans brought African rice and okra, and cash crops such as sugar were grown on plantations with coerced labor and exported mostly to Europe and the Middle East. Going east, maize, potatoes, cassava, and sweet potatoes became staple crops and improved nutrition in Afro-Eurasia. The ships carried more than anyone meant them to.
