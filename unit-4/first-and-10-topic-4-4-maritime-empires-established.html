First & 10 Reading

Building Empires at Sea

After 1500, expanding maritime trade changed state power and labor systems without erasing the older world it entered. European empires seized ports and territories, African states such as Kongo and Asante gained influence through new trading connections, Asian merchants kept Indian Ocean commerce moving, and colonial economies adapted old labor obligations while expanding new forms of coerced labor and slavery.

CausationComparisonArgumentation

Before You Read

Track continuity and change in four places: state power, Indian Ocean trade, labor systems, and slavery. The key question is not simply which European empire used which model; it is what changed when maritime empires expanded and what older systems continued.

Reading Target

By the end, you should be able to explain how maritime trade increased the influence of European and African states, identify continuity in Indian Ocean trade, compare older and newer colonial labor systems, and explain how older forms of enslavement continued while plantation demand expanded Atlantic chattel slavery.

State Building

Maritime Trade Creates New Opportunities for State Power

Portugal built the Estado da India around fortified ports and sea lanes, while Spain conquered and governed large American territories. Dutch, French, and British maritime power also expanded. But expanding trade did not strengthen only European states.

In Africa, participation in Atlantic commerce helped states such as the Kingdom of Kongo and Asante gain access to revenue, weapons, and new diplomatic relationships. The effects were uneven and could become destabilizing, but the important AP point is that maritime networks redistributed political opportunity.

AP Thinking, Causation

State growth followed a mechanism: access to trade -> access to revenue or strategic goods -> greater political influence. Apply that mechanism to both European and African cases.

2

Continuity

Indian Ocean Trade Changes Without Being Replaced

Portuguese and Dutch merchants disrupted parts of the Indian Ocean system by seizing ports, demanding passes, and attempting monopolies. Yet intra-Asian trade continued to flourish.

Gujarati, Omani, Swahili Arab, Javanese, Indian, Chinese, and other merchants kept moving goods through commercial networks that long predated European arrival. European armed commerce became an important new layer, but it did not erase the local knowledge, credit relationships, or regional demand that sustained Asian trade.

AP Thinking, Continuity & Change

Change: Europeans inserted armed trading-post empires and monopoly pressure. Continuity: Asian, African, and Middle Eastern merchants continued to dominate much regional exchange.

3

Labor

Colonial Economies Adapt Old Labor and Expand New Systems

Colonial economies depended on agriculture and extraction, and rulers often adapted institutions that already existed. The Spanish colonial mita, for example, drew on the Incan mit’a labor obligation but redirected it toward colonial mining and imperial revenue.

At the same time, colonial societies expanded or introduced systems such as encomienda, hacienda labor, indentured servitude, and chattel slavery. These systems differed legally, but all organized workers for agricultural or extractive production.

AP Thinking, Comparison

Compare labor systems by the same variables: who owed labor, for how long, under what legal status, and who captured the output.

4

Slavery

Older Enslavement Continues as Atlantic Plantation Slavery Expands

Enslavement in Africa did not begin with the Atlantic system. Older forms continued, including incorporation of enslaved persons into households and export through Mediterranean and Indian Ocean routes.

The major change was the scale and destination of forced migration. Expanding plantation economies in the Americas created enormous demand for enslaved African labor and strengthened hereditary, racialized chattel slavery.

Common Mistake to Avoid

Do not write that slavery was invented after 1500. Explain both continuity in older forms of enslavement and change in the scale, destination, and racialization of Atlantic plantation slavery.

BeReady: 10-Second Takeaway

Topic 4.4 is a continuity-and-change story. Maritime expansion increased the influence of European empires and also created opportunities for African states such as Kongo and Asante. Portuguese and Dutch intrusion disrupted Indian Ocean commerce, but intra-Asian trade and merchants such as Gujaratis, Omanis, Swahili Arabs, and Javanese continued. Colonial economies adapted older labor systems such as the Incan mit’a while expanding encomienda, hacienda, indentured servitude, and chattel slavery. Older forms of enslavement continued in Africa and the Indian Ocean world, while plantation demand dramatically expanded Atlantic slavery.
