Empires from the Sea

From the late 1400s, European states built empires held together by ships: forts on the coasts of Africa and Asia, and colonies in the Americas. But much of the world kept its own rules. Some things changed fast, and some barely moved.

1

Who Ruled: Change

Rivals Build Empires from the Sea

Topic 4.3 ended with a question. Disease had emptied much of the Americas of its people, and the new sugar plantations needed more workers than ever. Who would do the work, and who would rule? The answer reaches beyond the Americas, because European ships were reaching Africa and Asia too.

From the late 1400s on, five European states built empires held together by ships: Portugal, Spain, the Dutch Republic, France, and England (Britain after 1707). An empire like that is a maritime empire. They built them because they were rivals. They competed for power, so each wanted what the others had. They competed over religion: Catholic Portugal and Spain saw their voyages as a war against Islam and a mission to spread Christianity, and after the Reformation, Protestant England and the Dutch fought Catholic Spain. And they competed for money: gold, spices, sugar, and the trade that carried them. When one state found a route or a rich coast, the others came after it.

In Africa and Asia, an empire from the sea usually looked like a fort on a coast. Portugal built São Jorge da Mina, a fortress on the Gold Coast of West Africa, in 1482, to buy gold. It took Goa in India in 1510, Malacca, on the narrow strait between the Indian Ocean and the South China Sea, in 1511, and Hormuz, at the mouth of the Persian Gulf, in 1515. A trading post is a small fortified base where a foreign state's merchants buy and sell. These posts made money for the kings who backed them and the merchants who traded through them. The Dutch, and later the English and French, built posts of their own; the Dutch often did it by taking Portugal's.

The Americas were different. There, disease and conquest had broken the great Indigenous states, and Spain and Portugal took whole territories and the people living on them.

AP Thinking, Continuity and Change

Name the change, then its cause. "Europeans built forts in Asia" is a fact. "Rival European states, competing for power, religion, and trade, built forts on the coasts of Africa and Asia" is a change with its cause attached.

2

Who Ruled: Continuity

Asia Sets the Terms

The strongest states in Asia did not let Europeans in on Europe's terms. Ming China let the Portuguese settle at Macau, a small port on China's south coast, from 1557, and kept them under Chinese officials and Chinese rules.

Japan went further. Its rulers, the Tokugawa shoguns, feared that Christianity, which Portuguese and Spanish missionaries had spread in Japan, would divide the country. In 1635 the shogun's senior councilors sent orders to their officials at Nagasaki, the main port where Portuguese and Chinese ships traded. The first order reads: "Japanese ships are strictly forbidden to leave for foreign countries." Another says that any Japanese who returns from overseas after living there "must be put to death." Other orders told the officials to search every arriving ship for Christians. In 1639 the councilors banned Portuguese ships from Japan altogether.

After 1639, the only Europeans allowed to trade in Japan were the Dutch, and from 1641 they were confined to Dejima, a small artificial island in Nagasaki harbor. Chinese merchants kept trading at Nagasaki too. Europe's empires grew, but China and Japan still decided how far into their countries Europeans could reach.

AP Thinking, Sourcing and Situation

Ask who wrote it, for whom, and why. The 1635 orders were written by the shogun's councilors (the authors), for their officials at Nagasaki (the audience), to keep Christianity out and foreign trade under the government's control (the purpose), at a time when the shogunate saw Christian converts as a danger (the situation). Naming those four things is the skill this topic practices.

3

Trade: Change and Continuity

The Old Ocean Keeps Trading

Long before the Portuguese came, the Indian Ocean was crowded with merchants from East Africa, Arabia, India, Southeast Asia, and China (Topic 2.3). The Portuguese tried to control it with guns. They made other ships buy a pass, called a cartaz, and attacked ships that sailed without one. That was a real change: a European navy was now taxing and threatening Asian shipping.

But the old ocean kept trading. Gujarati merchants from western India kept shipping cotton cloth across the ocean, often by buying a pass and sailing on. Swahili Arab traders on the East African coast kept trading. Omani Arabs drove the Portuguese out of Muscat in 1650, and in 1698 they took the Portuguese fortress at Mombasa, on the Swahili coast. Javanese merchants kept trading from the ports of Java.

Much of the ocean's trade went on being trade between one Asian port and another, which historians call intra-Asian trade, and European merchants often made their money by joining it, carrying Asian goods from one Asian port to the next. The College Board's words for this trade are that it "continued to flourish."

AP Thinking, Continuity and Change

One case can hold both. The cartaz was a change. The Gujarati, Swahili, Omani, and Javanese merchants who kept trading were a continuity. A strong answer names both and says which one lasted.

4

Who Ruled: African States

Kongo and Asante Grow Stronger

The same trade made some African states stronger. The Kingdom of Kongo, near the mouth of the Congo River in Central Africa, welcomed the Portuguese in the 1480s, and its king was baptized a Christian in 1491. His son Afonso I, who became king in the early 1500s, made Christianity the religion of the royal court and traded with Portugal for goods, priests, teachers, and weapons that strengthened his rule. Kongo's influence grew.

About two hundred years later, on the Gold Coast of West Africa, the Asante kingdom rose by controlling gold and by buying guns from Dutch and English traders on the coast. In 1701 Asante defeated the stronger kingdom of Denkyira at the battle of Feyiase and became the main power in the region.

In 1526 Afonso wrote a letter to King João III of Portugal. He complained that Portuguese traders were carrying away "our native-born countrymen and the children of our nobles and vassals as well as relatives of ours," because thieves were seizing free people of Kongo and selling them to those traders. He asked the Portuguese king to send "neither traders nor trading goods." Two more facts matter when you read the letter: Afonso himself sold captives taken in his wars outside Kongo to the Portuguese, and Kongo had enslaved people of its own.

AP Thinking, Sourcing and Situation

Purpose changes meaning. Read the letter twice. First read it alone. Then read it again knowing who wrote it, who he was writing to, and what he wanted that reader to do. Question 2 asks you what changes.

5

Work: Old and New

Labor in the Colonial Americas

The colonies in the Americas made their money mostly from farming, growing sugar, tobacco, and wheat and raising cattle, and from mining. They needed workers, and they got them in two ways.

Some labor systems already existed. The Inca had required each community to send people to work for the state in turns, a labor duty called the mit’a. In the 1570s the Spanish viceroy of Peru, Francisco de Toledo, turned that duty into a draft that sent thousands of Andean men each year to the silver mine at Potosí. An old system kept its name and changed its master.

Other systems were new. Encomienda gave a Spanish colonist the labor and tribute of a group of Indigenous people. On a hacienda, a large estate, Indigenous and mixed-race workers farmed and herded for the owner, often for wages and sometimes tied to the estate by debt. In the English colonies, many poor Europeans paid for their voyage with four to seven years of work under a contract, which is indentured servitude. And on the plantations, enslaved Africans were held in chattel slavery: owned as property for life, and their children too.

AP Thinking, Continuity and Change

Compare by the same questions. For each labor system, ask who did the work, for how long, whether they were free, and who took what they produced. The mit’a is a continuity with a change: the same duty, a new master, a new mine.

6

Slavery: Old and New

Older Forms Continue, Plantations Change Everything Else

Slavery was not new in Africa. In many African societies, enslaved people were taken into households, and over time they or their children could become part of the family. Captives were also carried north across the Sahara to the Mediterranean world, and east across the Indian Ocean to Arabia, Persia, and India. Those older forms went on through this whole period.

What changed was the plantations. A plantation economy is one built on huge farms growing one crop to sell, and sugar, and later tobacco, needed enormous numbers of workers. Historians estimate that in the whole 1500s, fewer than 300,000 Africans were forced onto ships bound for the Americas. In the fifty years from 1701 to 1750, more than 2.5 million were.

The Atlantic slave trade carried more people across an ocean than any slave trade before it. Slavery in the Americas was for life and inherited (laws in the English colonies made the children of enslaved women enslaved too), and it came to be tied to race. Africans and their descendants became most of the people in the British, French, and Dutch Caribbean colonies and a large part of Brazil's population, and their languages, beliefs, and foods came with them. That is what the College Board calls "significant demographic, social, and cultural changes."

Common Mistake to Avoid

Do not write that slavery began with the Atlantic trade. Older forms of slavery continued in Africa. What changed was the scale, the destination, and the kind of slavery: lifelong, inherited, and tied to race on American plantations.

7

The Pattern

Some Things Changed Fast, and Some Barely Moved

Put it together. Europeans built empires from the sea: new forts, new colonies, new labor systems, and a far larger slave trade. But much of the world kept its own rules. China and Japan set the terms of trade. Asian and African merchants kept the Indian Ocean busy. Kongo and Asante grew strong. An old Inca labor duty was reused, and older forms of slavery went on in Africa.

Europeans built empires from the sea, but much of the world kept its own rules: some things changed fast, and some barely moved.

It also leaves a question. The empires existed. How did their rulers make them pay, and what did the money do to the people inside them? That is Topic 4.5.

AP Thinking, Continuity and Change

Answer all three. The College Board asks about three things in this topic: how states were built, how trade and labor changed and continued, and how slavery changed and continued. For each one, name one change and one continuity.

BeReady: 10-Second Takeaway

Driven by political, religious, and economic rivalries, Portugal, Spain, the Dutch, France, and England built maritime empires: trading posts on the coasts of Africa and Asia, and colonies in the Americas. Ming China and Tokugawa Japan limited European trade on their own terms, and Gujarati, Swahili Arab, Omani, and Javanese merchants kept Indian Ocean trade flourishing. Kongo and Asante grew stronger through the new trade. Colonial economies in the Americas reused the Inca mit’a and added encomienda, hacienda labor, indentured servitude, and chattel slavery. Older forms of slavery continued in Africa, while the plantation economy made slavery in the Americas far larger, lifelong, inherited, and tied to race. Some things changed fast, and some barely moved.
