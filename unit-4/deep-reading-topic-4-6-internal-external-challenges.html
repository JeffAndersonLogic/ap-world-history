The Cracks in the Map

An empire on a map looks solid. In 1680 Spain lost the whole of New Mexico to a rising organized across dozens of autonomous towns speaking unrelated languages, and did not get it back for twelve years. In Brazil a community of escaped people held out for nearly a century. And the Portuguese network fell to a Dutch company with better capital. Three kinds of challenge, one structural weakness.

01

The Revolt That Took Twelve Years to Undo

1680 to 1692  ·  New Mexico

The Pueblo Revolt is the most successful Indigenous rising against a European power in North America, and it is worth studying less for the fighting than for the organizing, which solved a coordination problem most rebellions never solve.

Three grievances, and why they arrived together

Spanish New Mexico was a poor, remote colony, valuable to the crown mainly as a mission field, and its pressure on the Pueblo peoples came in three forms that a good answer names separately.

Religious suppression was the sharpest. Franciscan missionaries did not merely preach; they raided kivas, the underground ceremonial chambers at the center of Pueblo religious life, destroyed masks and sacred objects, and prosecuted ceremonial practice as idolatry. In 1675 the governor had forty-seven Pueblo religious leaders arrested, three hanged and the rest publicly whipped. One of those whipped was a Tewa man from Ohkay Owingeh named Pope.

Labor and tribute were the second. The encomienda and repartimiento of the Topic 4.4 chapter reached New Mexico as demands for woven cloth, maize and labor on Spanish fields and in Spanish households, extracted from communities that farmed a marginal, arid landscape with no surplus to spare.

Drought and its consequences were the third, and the timing is what made the other two intolerable. A severe drought through the 1660s and 1670s brought famine, and famine brought intensified Apache and Navajo raiding on weakened towns. Spanish authorities could not protect the Pueblos from the raids and would not reduce the tribute, which broke the one argument colonial rule had in its favor. And because Pueblo religion is centrally concerned with rain and agricultural cycles, the drought was itself evidence: the ceremonies had been suppressed, and the rain had stopped.

The organizing problem

Here is what Pope actually had to do. There were roughly two dozen or more autonomous Pueblo towns spread across several hundred miles, speaking languages from at least four distinct families, some of them mutually unintelligible, with their own histories of rivalry, and with no tradition of unified political authority. There was no confederation to activate. Several towns had Spanish garrisons or resident priests, and there were Pueblo individuals who had converted sincerely and might warn them.

The solution was a communications technology and a piece of security design at once. Runners carried cords knotted with a number of knots to each participating town. Each day, one knot was untied. When the last knot was gone, everyone rose. No date had to be spoken aloud, no writing existed to be intercepted and read, and a captured runner carried an object whose meaning was useless without the instruction that came with it.

It nearly failed, which is the detail that proves the design. Two runners were captured and interrogated shortly before the appointed day, so Pope moved the rising forward. On 10 August 1680 the Pueblos rose more or less together, killed around four hundred colonists including twenty-one of the thirty-three Franciscans, besieged Santa Fe and cut its water supply, and drove roughly two thousand survivors down the Rio Grande to El Paso.

How we know: an inquiry designed to assign blame

Most of what is known about the revolt comes from Spanish records, above all the interrogations conducted at El Paso and during the reconquest, in which Pueblo prisoners were questioned about the causes and the organization. These are not neutral sources: they were produced by an administration explaining a catastrophic failure to its superiors, and they were extracted from prisoners under duress who had every reason to shape what they said. Read against that, some things stand out precisely because they are inconvenient for the people recording them, and the repeated testimony that the destruction of kivas and the whipping of religious leaders were central causes is one of them. Pueblo oral tradition and the archaeology of resettlement after 1680 supply what the Spanish record cannot.

What happened next, and what Spain changed

Twelve years of independence followed, and they were not easy. Pope reportedly ordered the removal of everything Spanish, including crops and livestock that the Pueblos had adopted and depended on, which was unpopular. The alliance that had held for one day did not hold for a decade; old rivalries reasserted themselves, the drought continued, and raiding did not stop. Pope died before the reconquest.

Diego de Vargas returned in 1692 and re-established Spanish authority over several years, with fighting rather than the bloodless reconquest Spanish accounts claimed. But the colony that returned was materially different, and this is the part the success criteria ask for. Encomienda was not restored in New Mexico. Tribute demands were reduced. And, most tellingly, missionaries largely stopped raiding kivas and tolerated the continuation of Pueblo ceremonial life alongside Catholic practice, which is why Pueblo religion survived into the present in a way it did not in many other mission regions.

That is the argument to write: the revolt was militarily reversed and politically successful. Spain won the territory back and lost the ability to govern it the way it had. A rebellion that changes the terms of rule has accomplished something even when the flag goes back up.

Use this in your answer
Strongest tool, with its mechanism
The knotted cord. The mechanism is that a rising across dozens of autonomous towns with no shared language needs a synchronized start date that cannot be spoken, written or extracted from a captured messenger, so a cord with one knot untied per day carries the schedule as an object rather than as information, and an interceptor holds a piece of string.
Best evidence of exclusion or limit
It coordinated one day and could not sustain a decade. The alliance fractured under drought, old rivalries and unpopular policy, which is why the reconquest succeeded twelve years later.
Comparison hook
Against Inca quipu in Topic 1.4: both are knotted-cord information systems in the Andes and the North American Southwest, and the resemblance is coincidental rather than connected. What they share is the insight that a physical object can carry precise information without a writing system, which is worth stating because textbooks treat literacy as the threshold for complex administration.

Terms to use precisely

Pueblo Revolt
The 1680 rising that expelled Spain from New Mexico for twelve years, the most successful Indigenous revolt in North America.
Pope
The Tewa religious leader from Ohkay Owingeh, publicly whipped in 1675, who organized the coordinated rising.
Kiva
The underground ceremonial chamber at the center of Pueblo religious life, raided and destroyed by missionaries before the revolt.
Knotted cord
The runner-carried schedule, one knot untied per day, which synchronized towns speaking unrelated languages.
Reconquest
Vargas's re-establishment of Spanish rule from 1692, on materially reduced terms with no encomienda and tolerated ceremonial practice.

The Republic in the Forest

c. 1600 to 1694  ·  Palmares and the maroon world

Maroon communities were the largest and most durable form of resistance to Atlantic slavery, and Palmares in Brazil was not a hideout. It was a polity, with towns, fields, a government and an army, that outlasted several European wars.

What a maroon community was

The word comes from the Spanish cimarron, used for escaped livestock and then for escaped people, and maroon communities existed wherever plantation slavery met terrain a colonial army could not easily enter: the Brazilian interior, the Jamaican Cockpit Country, the Surinamese rainforest, the Colombian coast, the Florida swamps, the mountains of Hispaniola.

The geographic pattern is the mechanism, and it should go in an answer. Maroon settlements survived where three conditions held together: terrain that neutralized European military advantages, distance from the coast great enough to make an expedition expensive, and land that could feed a community without trade. Where all three held, a settlement could last generations. Where any failed, it was destroyed within a season.

Most maroon communities also traded, raided plantations for supplies and recruits, and often negotiated. Several signed treaties: the Jamaican Maroons in 1739 and the Saramaka and Ndyuka of Suriname secured recognized autonomy and land, and the terms of those treaties are the difficult part of this section.

Palmares

Palmares, in the Brazilian northeast, is the largest known maroon polity in the Americas. It grew from the late sixteenth or early seventeenth century, and at its height, according to Portuguese and Dutch estimates, held something on the order of ten to twenty thousand people across a network of fortified settlements linked by paths and palisades.

It was governed. There was a paramount ruler, a title recorded as ganga zumba, subordinate settlement heads, an agricultural economy of maize, manioc, beans and bananas, iron-working, and a military organization that used the terrain, the palisades and ambush against colonial expeditions. Its political and religious forms drew heavily on Central African, particularly Kongolese and Angolan, models, which is unsurprising given where most of the enslaved population of Brazil came from.

Portuguese and Dutch forces attacked it repeatedly across most of a century and were repeatedly defeated. In 1678 the governor negotiated a treaty with Ganga Zumba offering freedom to Palmares-born residents and land, in exchange for submission and the return of future runaways. That last clause split the community: Zumbi, a military leader, rejected the treaty on the grounds that a freedom that required returning others to slavery was not freedom, and led the resistance that continued.

The end came in 1694, when the Portuguese hired bandeirantes, frontier expedition leaders experienced in Indigenous slave-raiding, and brought artillery capable of breaking the palisades. The main settlement fell; Zumbi was killed in 1695 and his head displayed publicly to disprove the belief that he was immortal. He is now a national figure in Brazil and the date of his death is marked as a day of Black consciousness.

Common mistake to avoid

Do not write maroon communities as either an unqualified triumph or a futile gesture. The success criteria ask for both, and Palmares supplies both in one story. Nearly a century of independent existence, a functioning polity of many thousands, and colonial armies defeated repeatedly is a real achievement that plantation societies feared enormously. And it ended in destruction, and the treaty that split it required the return of future runaways, which is what it cost to be recognized. Treaties elsewhere carried the same clause: the Jamaican Maroons secured autonomy in 1739 partly by agreeing to return escapees and to help suppress revolts. Autonomy purchased at the price of other people’s freedom is the honest version, and writing it is not a criticism of the people who took the deal.

Use this in your answer
Strongest tool, with its mechanism
Terrain as a defensive technology. The mechanism is that European military advantage rested on artillery, cavalry and formation, all of which require open ground and supply lines, so a community in mountains, swamp or dense forest at a distance that makes an expedition expensive can defeat a force it could never meet in the field, which is why maroon settlements map onto terrain rather than onto anything about the people in them.
Best evidence of exclusion or limit
The advantage held only until an opponent adapted. Palmares fell when Portugal hired frontier specialists who fought in that terrain and brought artillery to break the palisades.
Comparison hook
Against the Mapuche in Topic 4.4: forest and a decentralized political structure with no capital to capture kept Spain out of southern Chile for three centuries, and the mechanism is identical. In both cases what defeats an imperial army is not superior force but a landscape and a political form that give it nothing to strike.

Terms to use precisely

Maroon
A person who escaped enslavement and lived in an independent community, from the Spanish cimarron.
Palmares
The Brazilian maroon polity of up to twenty thousand people, independent for most of a century until 1694.
Zumbi
The military leader who rejected the 1678 treaty because it required returning future runaways, killed in 1695 and now a Brazilian national figure.
Quilombo
The Brazilian term for a maroon settlement, of Central African origin, still used for descendant communities today.
Maroon treaty
An agreement granting autonomy and land in exchange for submission and, characteristically, the return of future escapees.
03

Rivals, and the Ships States Hired

c. 1570 to 1700  ·  Dutch displacement and privateering

The most effective challenge to the Portuguese and Spanish empires came from other Europeans, and it took two forms: a company with more capital that simply out-competed and out-shot the Estado da India, and a legal instrument that let a state attack shipping without admitting it was at war.

How the Dutch took the network

The Portuguese empire of Topic 4.4 had a structural weakness the VOC was built to exploit. It was a thin chain of forts held by a country of about a million and a half people, financed by a crown that was chronically short of money, and dependent on each link holding. The VOC had permanent capital, a bigger and cheaper merchant fleet, better ships, lower borrowing costs, and shareholders who let it reinvest for decades.

So the Dutch did not need to defeat Portugal. They needed to take links, and they did so one at a time: Amboina in 1605, Batavia founded in 1619, Malacca in 1641, Ceylon's coast through the 1650s, Cochin in 1663, and the Cape in 1652 as a resupply station on the route. Each loss made the next one easier, because the network's value was in its completeness.

They also attacked Portugal in the Atlantic, holding northeastern Brazil for two decades and taking the Angolan slaving ports for a period, on the correct analysis that Brazilian sugar and Angolan captives were two ends of one system. Portugal recovered both, but the pattern is the same: a thinly held network is attacked where it is thinnest.

It helped enormously that Portugal was under the Spanish crown from 1580 to 1640, which made Portuguese possessions legitimate targets in the Dutch war of independence against Spain, and it helped that Spanish and Portuguese resources were committed to European wars throughout.

The privateer

A privateer was a privately owned armed ship operating under a letter of marque, a government commission authorizing it to attack the shipping of a named enemy and keep a share of what it took. That commission is the entire difference between a privateer and a pirate, and it is a legal difference rather than a behavioral one: the same crew doing the same things is one or the other depending on whether it holds a piece of paper and whether the issuing state is currently at war.

The attraction to a government is that it is empire-fighting at no cost. The crown supplies a document; investors supply the ship, the crew and the risk; the crown takes a share of the proceeds and can disown the whole enterprise diplomatically if convenient. Francis Drake's raids on Spanish ports and his capture of treasure returns made a fortune for Elizabeth I among others, and he was knighted for it while Spain regarded him as a criminal.

The strategic effect was disproportionate to the force involved. Spain had to convoy its silver fleets, fortify Havana, Cartagena and Portobelo, and maintain a permanent naval presence, and the cost of that protection was borne every year whether or not an attack came. A handful of raiders imposed a permanent tax on the entire imperial system, which is the sentence worth having.

Common mistake to avoid

Do not treat piracy as lawlessness at the edges of empire. For most of this period it was an instrument of state policy: governments issued letters of marque deliberately, licensed raiders as a cheap navy, and prosecuted them as pirates only when a peace treaty made them inconvenient. The wave of genuinely stateless piracy in the Caribbean around 1715 to 1725 that produced the familiar legends is largely what happened after a war ended and thousands of trained privateers were dismissed with no employment, and the same states then hunted them down. Privateering is a policy; piracy is frequently its unemployment.

Use this in your answer
Strongest tool, with its mechanism
The letter of marque. The mechanism is that a state which cannot afford a navy large enough to attack a rival’s trade issues commissions to private investors instead, so the ships, crews and risk are privately financed while the state takes a share of the prizes and retains the option to disavow the whole thing, which converts naval warfare into an investment product.
Best evidence of exclusion or limit
A privateer serves its investors, not a war plan, so it takes profitable prizes rather than strategic ones, and demobilizing a privateer fleet at the end of a war reliably produces the piracy the same state then has to suppress.
Comparison hook
Against the chartered company in Topic 4.4: both are delegated violence financed by private investors under a state license, and the difference is duration. A letter of marque authorizes a raid; a charter authorizes an administration. One produces a prize, the other produces Batavia.

Terms to use precisely

Privateer
A privately owned armed ship attacking enemy shipping under government commission, keeping a share of what it takes.
Letter of marque
The commission that legally separates a privateer from a pirate, naming the enemy whose shipping may be taken.
Prize
A captured ship and cargo, condemned by a court and divided among the crown, investors and crew.
Convoy system
Spain's escorted treasure fleets, the defensive response that made raiding costly to the empire even when it failed.
Dutch displacement
The VOC's piecemeal capture of Portuguese links, Amboina 1605 to Cochin 1663, exploiting a network whose value lay in completeness.
04

Why Maritime Empires Crack

c. 1500 to 1750  ·  The common structure

Three very different challenges, a Pueblo rising, a maroon republic and a Dutch company, exploited the same weakness. A maritime empire is a set of points connected by long lines, and everything about it that makes it cheap to build makes it fragile to hold.

The four weaknesses, and which challenge used which

The empire is thin. It occupies ports, mines and plantations and leaves most of the map alone, which means there is always terrain nearby that it does not control. Maroons and the Mapuche used exactly this; so did every Indigenous nation the colonial map claimed and never administered.

The empire is slow. Reinforcement travels at sailing speed, so a rising has months before an answer arrives. New Mexico is not merely far from Madrid, it is far from Mexico City, which is why twelve years passed.

The empire's force is borrowed. Colonial rule almost everywhere depended on local allies, Indigenous auxiliaries, converted intermediaries and enslaved people set against each other. Any of those alignments can change, and the Pueblo Revolt succeeded partly because Spanish rule had exhausted its usefulness to the people it depended on: it could not stop the raids and would not reduce the tribute.

The empire is a chain. When value comes from a network of chokepoints, each link is worth more than it can defend, and losing several makes the rest less defensible. That is what happened to the Estado da India, and it is why the Dutch strategy of taking one port at a time worked against an empire they never defeated in a decisive battle.

What resistance changed, and what it did not

None of these challenges ended European empire in the Americas or Asia, and it is worth being straightforward about that. New Mexico was retaken. Palmares was destroyed. The Dutch replaced the Portuguese rather than removing European power from the Indian Ocean.

What they changed were the terms. Spain governed New Mexico afterward without encomienda and with Pueblo ceremonial life tolerated. Colonial governments across the Americas negotiated treaties with maroon communities they could not defeat, and permanently carried the cost of policing against escape. Spain paid for convoys, fortifications and garrisons every year for two centuries because of raiders it mostly could not catch. Empires adjusted continuously to resistance they could not eliminate, and the record of those adjustments is where the resistance is visible in the sources.

That is the frame worth carrying into Unit 5, where the Haitian Revolution shows what happens when a colonial power's borrowed force is not merely realigned but reversed, and when the population it depends on outnumbers it ten to one.

Use this in your answer
Strongest tool, with its mechanism
Borrowed force. The mechanism is that a colonial administration too small to govern by itself rules through local allies, intermediaries and auxiliaries whose cooperation is transactional, so the empire is stable only while it delivers something those partners want, and when it stops delivering, as Spanish New Mexico did when it could not stop Apache raiding and would not reduce tribute, the same structure that carried its authority carries the revolt.
Best evidence of exclusion or limit
It explains why revolts start and succeed initially, not why they usually fail afterward. Holding a coalition together across years is a different problem from assembling one for a day, which is what happened to the Pueblo alliance.
Comparison hook
Against the land-based empires of Unit 3: an Ottoman or Qing province had a garrison, a governor, a tax register and a road, so a revolt met a response in weeks. A maritime empire meets a revolt in months and often on someone else’s terrain, which is why the same amount of discontent produces a twelve-year expulsion in New Mexico and a suppressed disturbance in Anatolia.

Terms to use precisely

Overextension
Holding more points than can be defended, the structural condition of a network empire.
Auxiliary
A locally recruited force on which colonial rule depended, and whose realignment could reverse it.
Negotiated autonomy
The recognition of a community an empire could not defeat, as in the maroon treaties, and evidence of the limits of control.
Frontier
The zone an empire claimed and did not administer, which every challenge in this chapter used.
Fortification cost
The permanent expense of convoys, walls and garrisons imposed by raiding, paid every year whether or not an attack came.

Building an Answer That Scores

Each card is a full explanation: the claim, the specific evidence, and the reason. The first two answer the success criteria almost directly, so learn the details in them.

Causation

Three grievances arrived at once, and a knotted cord synchronized the answer

Franciscans in New Mexico raided kivas and destroyed sacred objects, and in 1675 the governor hanged three Pueblo religious leaders and had forty-seven whipped, one of them Pope. Encomienda and repartimiento demanded cloth, maize and labor from an arid landscape with no surplus. Severe drought through the 1660s and 1670s brought famine and intensified Apache raiding that Spain could not stop while refusing to reduce tribute. Pope coordinated more than two dozen autonomous towns speaking languages from several families by sending runners with knotted cords, one knot untied per day, so no date was written or spoken. On 10 August 1680 they rose, killed about four hundred colonists and drove two thousand survivors to El Paso.

Evaluation

Spain took New Mexico back and could not take back the terms

Vargas re-established Spanish authority from 1692 after twelve years of Pueblo independence, so the revolt was militarily reversed. But encomienda was not restored in New Mexico, tribute demands were reduced, and missionaries largely stopped raiding kivas and tolerated Pueblo ceremonial practice alongside Catholicism, which is why that religious tradition survived where it did not in many other mission regions. Judge a revolt by the terms of rule afterward rather than by whose flag flies, and this one succeeded at what its grievances were about.

Evaluation

Palmares proves both the reach of resistance and its price

Palmares held perhaps ten to twenty thousand people across fortified settlements in the Brazilian northeast for most of a century, with a paramount ruler, an agricultural economy, iron-working and an army that defeated Portuguese and Dutch expeditions repeatedly. It fell in 1694 only when Portugal hired bandeirantes who could fight in that terrain and brought artillery to break the palisades. The 1678 treaty offered freedom to Palmares-born residents in exchange for returning future runaways, and Zumbi rejected it on exactly that ground, while the Jamaican Maroons accepted comparable terms in 1739. Autonomy was reachable and was routinely priced in other people’s freedom.

Structure

A network empire loses one link at a time

The Dutch never defeated Portugal in a decisive war. The VOC, with permanent capital, cheaper credit and better ships, took Amboina in 1605, founded Batavia in 1619, took Malacca in 1641, the Ceylon coast in the 1650s and Cochin in 1663, and each loss made the next easier because the Estado da India’s value lay in the completeness of the chain rather than in any single fort. Meanwhile privateers under letters of marque forced Spain to convoy its silver and fortify Havana, Cartagena and Portobelo, a cost paid annually whether or not a raider appeared. Thinness is what makes a maritime empire cheap to build and is the same property that makes it fragile to hold.
