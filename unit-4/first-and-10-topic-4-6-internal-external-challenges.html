First & 10 Reading

Push and Pushback

Between 1450 and 1750, states pulled power into the hands of their rulers and pushed out to rule new lands and peoples. Every push cost somebody something, and all over the world, people pushed back: nobles and judges, peasants and Cossacks, kings and queens, Indigenous towns, and enslaved Africans.

CausationContextualization

The Story to Hold Onto

States pushed to expand and centralize → the push landed on a local group → the group pushed back → the state answered. For every case in this reading, ask four things: what did the state push for, who pushed back, how, and what happened next?

Question for Today

What did states do between 1450 and 1750 that made people resist, who resisted, and what did their resistance change?

1

The Push

Two Ways to Grow a State

Topic 4.5 ended with a problem. States got richer and reached further. Not everyone accepted it.

Between 1450 and 1750, states grew stronger than they had ever been, in two ways. The first was centralization: a king or emperor pulled power into his own hands and away from the people who used to share it, such as great nobles, judges, towns, and village leaders. Topic 3.2 showed some of the tools, like Louis XIV's court at Versailles and the Ottoman devshirme. The second was expansion: states pushed out over land and sea to rule people who had never been theirs, as Topics 4.2 to 4.5 showed.

Both pushes cost somebody something. Centralizing meant new taxes, new laws, and lost privileges. Expanding meant tribute, forced labor, lost land, attacks on a people's religion, and for millions of Africans, slavery. So people pushed back, and they came from every kind of group: rich and poor, nobles and peasants, kingdoms and villages, free and enslaved. The harder states pushed to expand and centralize, the harder people at the local level pushed back.

AP Thinking, Contextualization

Every revolt sits inside something bigger. A revolt is not just a moment when people got angry. To put it in context, name the bigger development it grew out of: a state pulling power to the center, or a state pushing out over new people. As you read each case, ask which push it answers.

2

Inside the State: Nobles and Peasants

France and Russia Push Their Own People

In France, the crown had been fighting Spain for years, and war cost money. In 1648, while Louis XIV was still a boy and his mother ruled for him with her chief minister, Cardinal Mazarin, the government demanded new taxes. The judges of the Parlement of Paris, a powerful court, refused to approve them. When the government arrested one of the judges, the people of Paris put up barricades in the streets, and in January 1649 the royal family fled the city. Then some of the greatest nobles in France rose up too. This string of revolts, from 1648 to 1653, is called the Fronde. It failed. The rebels quarreled with each other, and the crown won. But Louis XIV never forgot it, and he grew up to be a king who kept power in his own hands.

In Russia, the tsar's Law Code of 1649 finished turning peasants into serfs, people bound to their lord's land. A serf who ran away could now be hunted down and returned, no matter how many years had passed. Many ran anyway, south to the Don River, where communities of free frontier fighters called Cossacks lived beyond the tsar's reach. In 1670 a Don Cossack named Stenka Razin led a huge revolt up the Volga River. Towns opened their gates to him, and tens of thousands of peasants and non-Russian peoples of the Volga rose up. The tsar's army beat the rebels at the town of Simbirsk. Other Cossacks handed Razin over, and in 1671 he was executed in Moscow. Serfdom stayed.

AP Thinking, Causation

Follow the chain. In each case, a state's push landed on a group, and the group pushed back. Ask what the state wanted, what that cost the group, and how the group answered. Then ask what happened next, because resistance has effects too.

3

At the Edges: Asia and Africa

A Warrior Kingdom and a Queen

Some pushback came from the edges of growing empires, from people the empire was trying to conquer. The Mughal emperor Aurangzeb pushed his empire south into the Deccan, the high plateau of central India. There the Marathas, led by Shivaji, had built a kingdom of hill forts and fast horsemen, and in 1674 Shivaji was crowned king. In 1681 Aurangzeb moved his court south to finish them. He conquered two other kingdoms of the Deccan, but he never defeated the Marathas, who fought from their forts and raided instead of meeting his huge army head on. He spent the rest of his life fighting in the south and died there in 1707. After that, the Marathas grew stronger and the Mughal emperors grew weaker.

In West Central Africa, the Portuguese colony at Luanda pushed inland into the kingdom of Ndongo, largely to take captives for the Atlantic slave trade. In 1622 Njinga, the king's sister, went to Luanda to bargain with the Portuguese governor, and she was baptized there with the name Ana de Sousa. In 1624 she became Ndongo's ruler. For almost forty years she fought the Portuguese and bargained with them. She conquered the kingdom of Matamba as a new base, joined forces with the Dutch after they captured Luanda in 1641, and made peace with Portugal in the 1650s. She died in 1663, still ruling Matamba. Resisting Portugal did not mean resisting the slave trade: her own wars took captives, and Matamba sold them.

AP Thinking, Causation

Resistance has effects. The College Board asks for the effects of state power, and resistance is one of them. But resistance had its own effects on the state. The Mughals spent decades and a fortune in the Deccan and never won there. Portugal ended up making peace with a queen it had set out to conquer.

At the Edges: The Americas

The Pueblo Revolt and Metacom's War

In 1598 Spain planted a colony in the Rio Grande valley of New Mexico, among the Pueblo peoples, who lived in towns of stone and adobe. Under the encomienda, the Pueblos had to hand over part of their crops to Spanish settlers each year, and they were forced to work without pay. Spanish friars tried to stamp out the Pueblo religion. From the 1650s they banned the sacred dances and burned masks and prayer sticks, and in 1675 the governor arrested forty-seven Pueblo religious leaders, hanged three, and had the rest whipped. This religious suppression came on top of years of drought and famine, and raids by Apache neighbors that the Spanish did not stop.

One of the whipped men was Popé (today often spelled Po'pay), a religious leader from the town of Ohkay Owingeh, who began to plan. Runners carried knotted cords from town to town. A town that agreed to join untied one knot as its sign, and the knots that were left counted the days to the same morning. When the Spanish caught two of the runners, the Pueblos struck early, on August 10, 1680. In the Pueblo Revolt they killed about 400 colonists, including 21 friars, surrounded the capital at Santa Fe, and cut off its water. The surviving colonists fled south to El Paso, and for twelve years the Pueblos ruled themselves. When the Spanish came back in the 1690s, they did not bring back the encomienda, and the friars pushed far less hard against the Pueblo religion than before.

In the same years, in New England, English colonists were taking more and more Wampanoag land and putting the Wampanoag under colonial courts. In 1675, after Plymouth Colony executed three Wampanoag men, war broke out between the colonists and the Wampanoag, led by Metacom, whom the English called King Philip. Other Native nations joined in. Metacom's War destroyed more than a dozen English towns. But the colonists, with Native allies of their own, won. Metacom was killed in 1676, and many Native captives were sold into slavery in the Caribbean.

AP Thinking, Causation

Causes come in kinds. The Pueblo Revolt had religious causes, economic causes, and political causes, all at once. A strong answer names more than one kind and explains how they worked together. Question 2 asks you to go one step further and put the revolt in its bigger context.

Enslaved People Organize

Towns in the Hills, a Treaty, and a March

The plantation colonies were built on slavery (Topic 4.4), and enslaved people pushed back in every way they could. The College Board's word for what this topic asks about is organized resistance: not one person running away, but many people acting together in a way that challenged the authorities.

In the hills behind Brazil's sugar coast, people who escaped slavery built Palmares, a cluster of fortified towns that held thousands of people and lasted for most of the 1600s. Communities like this, built by people who escaped slavery, are called maroon communities, and in Brazil a quilombo. Palmares farmed, traded with nearby settlers, and beat back one army after another. In 1678 its leader, Ganga Zumba, made a treaty with the colonial government, but many in Palmares rejected it, and the next leader, Zumbi, kept fighting. In 1694 a large colonial army captured Palmares's main town, and in 1695 Zumbi was killed.

In Jamaica, maroon communities in the mountains fought the British for years. In 1739 the colony made treaties with them. The Maroons led by Cudjoe were promised freedom and 1,500 acres of land forever. But the same treaty required them to send back any enslaved person who ran away to them and to help put down other revolts. Freedom for one community came with a promise to guard slavery for everyone else.

In North America, on September 9, 1739, about twenty enslaved people near the Stono River in South Carolina seized guns from a store and marched south toward Spanish Florida, which had promised freedom to people who escaped from the English colonies. As they marched, others joined, until there were as many as a hundred. The colony's militia broke up the march that afternoon and hunted down the survivors over the days that followed. Somewhere between twenty and forty white colonists died, along with dozens of rebels; accounts give different numbers. South Carolina answered the Stono Rebellion with the Negro Act of 1740, a law that tightened control over every enslaved person in the colony.

Common Mistake to Avoid

Do not write that maroons were just runaways hiding in the woods. As you read about Palmares and the Jamaica Maroons, look for what they built together and what they forced a colony to do.

6

The Pattern

Push, Pushback, and What Came Next

Put it together. States pushed, inward and outward. The push landed on someone: new taxes on judges and townspeople, serfdom on peasants, conquest on kingdoms, tribute and attacks on religion in the Pueblo towns, lost land for the Wampanoag, and slavery for Africans. Those groups pushed back by revolt, by war, by escape, and by bargaining.

And the pushback had its own effects. Sometimes the state crushed it and came out stronger: the Fronde, Razin's revolt, Metacom's War, and Stono. Sometimes the state had to bargain: Njinga's peace, the Jamaica treaties, and the Spanish return to New Mexico on new terms. And sometimes the resistance wore the state down, as the Marathas did to the Mughals.

The harder states pushed to expand and centralize, the harder people at the local level pushed back, and the pushback changed the states too.

Some people resisted the new order. Everyone was sorted by it. Who ended up where? That is Topic 4.7.

AP Thinking, Causation

Answer the whole question. The College Board asks you to explain the effects of the development of state power. A complete answer has three parts: what the state did, how people resisted, and what that resistance changed.

BeReady: 10-Second Takeaway

Between 1450 and 1750, states centralized and expanded, and the push cost local groups taxes, freedom, land, faith, and labor. People pushed back all over the world: judges, Parisians, and nobles in the Fronde; peasants and Cossacks under Stenka Razin; the Marathas against the Mughals; Njinga against Portugal; the Pueblos in 1680 and the Wampanoag in Metacom's War. Enslaved people organized too, in maroon communities like Palmares and the Jamaica Maroons, and in revolts like Stono in 1739. Some resistance was crushed, some forced a bargain, and some wore a state down.
