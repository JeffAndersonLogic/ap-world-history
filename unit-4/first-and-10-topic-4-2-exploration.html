First & 10 Reading

Who Paid?

Ships could now cross the ocean and come back, but every voyage cost a fortune. Rulers backed the voyages, because they wanted the trade of Asia. Portugal went around Africa, Spain went west, and England, France, and the Dutch went north, and every voyage that paid made the next one easier to fund.

CausationMaking Connections

The Story to Hold Onto

Rulers backed voyages → Voyages came back with gold, spices, or news → The next voyage was easier to fund → Rival rulers wanted voyages of their own. For every state in this reading, ask three things: who paid, what did they want, and what did they get?

Question for Today

Why did European rulers pay for ocean voyages, and what did those voyages bring back?

1

The Cause

Pepper Was Cheap in India and Expensive in Europe

In the last topic, borrowed tools, new ships, and the volta do mar (a wide loop out to sea to catch the winds that blow toward home) let a ship sail out into the Atlantic and come home. But a voyage cost a fortune: ships, food for months, wages for dozens of sailors, and a real chance that nobody came back with anything to sell. So who paid, and what did they want?

Start with what Europe wanted: the goods of Asia, above all spices. This was the spice trade. Pepper grew on the southwest coast of India and cost little there. By the time it reached a market in Europe, it had passed through many hands. Indian Ocean merchants carried it to the Red Sea or the Persian Gulf. It crossed Egypt or Syria. Venetian merchants bought it in ports such as Alexandria and shipped it to Europe. Each of these middlemen took a profit, so pepper in Europe cost many times what it cost in India.

Whoever could sail straight to India could buy at the low price and sell at the high one. That is the main economic cause of the voyages in this topic.

You may have heard that the Ottomans captured Constantinople in 1453 and "closed" the spice road, so Europe had to go by sea. Historians reject that story. The spice trade through Egypt and Venice kept running, and Portugal's voyages down the coast of Africa had already been going for decades before 1453. The main economic reason was the cost of all those middlemen, along with Portugal's hunt for West African gold.

AP Thinking, Causation

State the cause as a mechanism. "Europeans wanted spices" is not yet a cause. "Spices passed through many middlemen, so a sea route straight to India would let a ruler buy cheap and sell dear" explains why someone would pay for a voyage.

2

The Role of States

Rulers Backed the Voyages

A voyage into unknown water might take years and come back with nothing at all. Few merchants would risk that on their own. So rulers stepped in. This is state sponsorship: a government backing exploration. Sometimes a ruler paid for the ships outright. Sometimes a ruler gave a merchant or a captain official permission to explore, and the right to the trade, in return for a share of whatever came back. Either way, the ruler set the terms and claimed the results.

Rulers also paid in things that cost them nothing up front. They promised explorers titles, a share of future profits, and the right to govern whatever lands they found. And they kept the biggest prize for themselves: a royal monopoly, the crown's sole right to a trade. Rulers wanted the voyages for more than money, too. Spreading Christianity and beating rival kingdoms mattered to them. But the trade is what kept the money coming.

AP Thinking, The Role of States

Name the state's role. The College Board asks about the role of states, not just the explorers. A strong answer says which ruler backed the voyage, what the ruler gave (money, ships, permission, or promises), and what the ruler wanted back.

3

Portugal

Step by Step Around Africa

Portugal went first, and it went step by step. In 1415 Portugal captured Ceuta, a Muslim port in North Africa. From the 1420s, a royal prince named Henry paid for voyage after voyage down the coast of West Africa. He did not go on those voyages himself, and the famous "navigation school" at Sagres is a legend written down centuries later. Portuguese sailors built light caravels, mapped the coast as they went, and learned the Atlantic's winds, and each voyage's charts helped the next one go farther.

Each voyage went a little farther and came back with something to sell: gold, ivory, and pepper from the West African coast, and, from the 1440s, enslaved Africans. Each return made the next voyage easier to pay for. In 1482 Portugal built a fort at Elmina, on the Gold Coast, to control a royal trade in gold. In 1488 Bartolomeu Dias sailed around the southern tip of Africa.

In 1498 Vasco da Gama reached Calicut, a busy trading port on the southwest coast of India. He lost two of his four ships and more than half his men, but the spices he brought home showed how much money the sea route could make. Lisbon became a major market for pepper, and the king's trading house ran the pepper trade as a royal monopoly.

Portugal then seized a chain of fortified ports from Africa to India and on to Southeast Asia, including Goa in 1510 and Malacca in 1511. It did not conquer large lands. It held ports and tried to control the sea trade between them. This is the trading-post empire the College Board names. How those posts worked, and how Asian and African rulers answered them, is Topic 4.4.

AP Thinking, Causation

Follow one state from cause to effect. For Portugal: the crown backed voyages because it wanted gold and then spices, Portuguese ships and navigation skills carried them step by step around Africa to India, and the effect was more trade with Africa and Asia and a global trading-post empire.

4

Spain

A Gamble on Sailing West

Christopher Columbus, a sailor from Genoa, wanted to reach Asia by sailing west. Portugal's king turned him down, because his experts judged that Columbus had badly underestimated the distance to Asia, and they were right. Portugal also already held the route south along Africa. If Spain wanted its own way to Asia, it would have to go west.

In 1492, right after conquering Granada, Queen Isabella and King Ferdinand signed a contract with Columbus. If he succeeded, he would be "Admiral of the Ocean Sea," govern the lands he found, and keep a tenth of the wealth from them. Columbus reached the Caribbean in October 1492, not Asia, and brought back little gold. But his letter about the voyage was printed again and again across Europe, and Spain's next fleet, in 1493, was at least seventeen ships instead of three.

Spain and Portugal now both claimed newly found lands, so in 1494 they signed the Treaty of Tordesillas. It drew a line through the Atlantic: lands to the west for Spain, lands to the east for Portugal. Nobody asked the people who lived in those lands.

Spain kept going, across the Pacific too. In 1519 Ferdinand Magellan, a Portuguese captain working for Spain, sailed west to reach the Spice Islands. Magellan was killed in the Philippines in 1521. In 1522 Juan Sebastián Elcano brought one ship and eighteen men home to Spain, completing the first circumnavigation, a voyage all the way around the world. The College Board names the effect of Spain's voyages: they dramatically increased European interest in ocean travel and trade. What happened in the Americas next is the next three topics.

AP Thinking, Making Connections

Connect one state's decision to another's. Spain's choice did not happen alone. Portugal turned Columbus down and already held the African route, so Spain's only way to Asia was west. Showing how one development led to another is the skill this topic practices.

5

The Northern Crossings

Looking for Another Way to Asia

England, France, and the Dutch wanted the trade of Asia too. But Portugal held the route around Africa, and Spain held the route west. So they backed voyages across the North Atlantic, looking for another way to Asia over the top of the world: a Northwest Passage above North America, or a northeast route above Russia.

In 1496 England's king gave John Cabot, an Italian sailor, permission to sail west and claim lands for England, and Cabot and his backers paid for the voyage themselves. In 1497 he crossed the North Atlantic and reached the coast of North America. France sent Giovanni da Verrazzano along that coast in 1524, and Jacques Cartier up the St. Lawrence River in the 1530s. Dutch voyages under Willem Barentsz in the 1590s tried to sail around the north of Russia. In 1609 Henry Hudson, sailing for a Dutch trading company, explored the river that now carries his name.

None of them found a sea route to Asia. The land of North America and the Arctic ice blocked every way through. But the voyages paid in other ways. The sea off Newfoundland was full of cod, and within a few decades dozens of French ships a year were fishing there. Along the St. Lawrence, the French traded with Indigenous peoples for furs, and the fur trade grew. And each state claimed North American coasts where it would later build colonies.

AP Thinking, Causation

A voyage can miss its goal and still have an effect. The northern crossings failed to find a route to Asia, but they produced fish, furs, and land claims. When you explain an effect, say what the voyage actually produced, not just what it was looking for.

6

The Pattern

Every Voyage That Paid Brought More Ships

Put the three stories side by side and the same pattern repeats. A ruler backed a voyage. A voyage came back with gold, spices, or news of new lands. That return made the next voyage easier to fund, and it made rival rulers want voyages of their own.

Portugal got a trading-post empire from Africa to Asia. Spain got lands across the Atlantic and the first European crossing of the Pacific, and all of Europe got interested. England, France, and the Dutch got no route to Asia, but they got fish, furs, and claims.

Columbus was looking for a route to Asia. What he started was an exchange of plants, animals, and diseases that nobody planned. That is the next topic.

AP Thinking, Causation

Answer both halves. The College Board asks for the role of states and for the economic causes and effects. One strong sentence does both: who backed the voyage, what they wanted, and what they got.

BeReady: 10-Second Takeaway

Asian spices passed through many middlemen, so a sea route straight to India promised huge profits. Few merchants would carry that risk alone, so states backed the voyages, with money, ships, permission to explore, titles, and shares. Portugal went step by step around Africa to India and built a trading-post empire. Spain backed Columbus's westward gamble, and its voyages across the Atlantic and Pacific made all of Europe interested. England, France, and the Dutch looked for a northern route to Asia, never found one, and got fish, furs, and land claims instead. Every voyage that paid made the next one easier to fund.
