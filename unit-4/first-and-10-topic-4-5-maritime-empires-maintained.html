Making Empires Pay

An empire held together by ships cost money every year. European rulers built new tools to make their empires pay, and the silver, goods, and people those tools moved changed work, families, and faith around the world.

1

Rulers' Tools: An Idea

Sell More Than You Buy

Topic 4.4 ended with a question. European states had built empires from the sea: forts on the coasts of Africa and Asia, and colonies in the Americas. An empire like that is expensive. Ships, soldiers, and forts cost money every year. So how did rulers make their empires pay, and what did the money do to the people inside them?

The rulers' first tool was an idea. Most European rulers and their advisers believed that a kingdom grew strong by bringing in more gold and silver than flowed out. To do that, a country had to sell more to other countries than it bought from them, and it had to keep its colonies' trade for itself. Later writers named this set of ideas mercantilism.

An English merchant named Thomas Mun, a director of the English East India Company, wrote the rule down in a book printed in 1664, after his death. To grow rich, he wrote, England "must ever observe this rule; to sell more to strangers yearly than wee consume of theirs in value." ("Strangers" meant foreigners.)

Rulers turned the idea into laws. Spain sent its trade with its American colonies through one office in Seville, the Casa de Contratación, founded in 1503, and American silver crossed the Atlantic in fleets guarded by warships. England's Navigation Act of 1651 said that goods from Asia, Africa, and the Americas could come into England only on English ships. In this way of thinking, colonies existed to send raw goods home and to buy the home country's goods.

AP Thinking, Claims and Evidence in Sources

Find the claim first. A claim is what an author wants you to believe. Read Mun's sentence again and ask: what does he want his reader to believe about how a country grows rich? Question 2 asks you to put it in your own words.

2

Rulers' Tools: A Company

Companies That Could Go to War

The rulers' second tool was a new kind of company. A voyage to Asia cost a fortune, and ships were often lost. A joint-stock company spread that risk by selling shares to many investors, who split the profits and the losses.

Queen Elizabeth I gave the English East India Company its charter in 1600. In 1602 the government of the Dutch Republic chartered the Dutch East India Company, known by its Dutch initials, the VOC. Each was a chartered monopoly company: its charter gave it the only right, in its own country, to trade with Asia. The VOC's charter also let it build forts, keep soldiers, make treaties with Asian rulers, and fight wars.

That made a company into a weapon a ruler could aim at another ruler. In 1614 Jan Pieterszoon Coen, a leader of the VOC in Asia, wrote to the company's directors that "trade cannot be kept up without war, nor war without trade."

AP Thinking, Continuity and Change

Name what was new. Merchants had pooled money for voyages for centuries. What was new was a company with a government's monopoly, its own soldiers, and the right to make war, sent by one state to compete with another.

3

Rivals: Old Fights, New Tools

Economic Disputes Become Wars

Rival states fought over who would control a trade. In the Indian Ocean, the Portuguese tried to take over the spice trade with guns, and Muslim states fought back. A fleet from Mamluk Egypt and the sultan of Gujarat, in India, fought the Portuguese off the port of Diu in 1509 and lost. After the Ottoman Empire conquered Egypt in 1517, Ottoman fleets fought the Portuguese in the Red Sea, the Persian Gulf, and the Indian Ocean through the 1500s.

In Africa, the sultan of Morocco, Ahmad al-Mansur, wanted the gold of West Africa and the salt mines of the Sahara. In 1591 his army crossed the desert and defeated the Songhai Empire at the battle of Tondibi. (Topic 3.1 told the battle. Here the question is why: gold and the salt trade were a large part of the reason.)

Europeans fought each other too. The Dutch and English companies fought over the spice islands of Southeast Asia, and in 1623 VOC officials on the island of Ambon executed English company men they accused of plotting against them. England and the Dutch Republic then fought three wars at sea between 1652 and 1674, largely over trade and shipping.

AP Thinking, Continuity and Change

One case can hold both. States fighting over trade routes was a continuity: rulers had done it for centuries. The tools they fought with were the change: laws written to keep wealth at home, and companies built to compete.

4

Exchange: Silver

Silver Goes Around the World

In 1545 Spanish colonists found an enormous silver mountain at Potosí in the Andes, and in 1546 another great silver strike came at Zacatecas in Mexico. (Topic 4.4 showed how the mit’a sent Andean men to work at Potosí.) Spanish America became the world's largest source of silver. Some of that silver crossed the Atlantic to Seville. A large share of it ended up in China.

Why China? In the 1500s the government of Ming China began collecting its taxes in silver, in a reform called the Single Whip. Millions of Chinese families now needed silver to pay their taxes, and China mined little of its own, so silver bought more in China than it did in Europe.

From 1571, when the Spanish founded the city of Manila in the Philippines, Spanish galleons carried silver from Acapulco in Mexico across the Pacific Ocean to Manila. There Chinese merchants sailed in with silk and porcelain to trade for it. Silver that reached Europe kept moving too. Europeans had few goods that Asians wanted to buy, so Portuguese, Dutch, and English merchants carried silver around Africa to pay for Asian spices, cotton cloth, silk, and porcelain. Silver from the Americas now reached Europe, Asia, and Africa.

AP Thinking, Continuity and Change

Follow the pull, not just the push. Spain had the mines, but China's demand is what pulled the silver around the world. A strong answer names both ends: where the silver came from, and why it went where it went.

5

Exchange: Change and Continuity

The Atlantic System, and Markets That Kept Going

In the Atlantic, ships moved goods, wealth, and people. This is the Atlantic trading system. Sugar and tobacco went to Europe. Guns, iron, alcohol, and cloth went to Africa, and much of that cloth was cotton woven in India. Cowrie shells from the Maldive Islands in the Indian Ocean, carried by Dutch and English ships, were used as money on the coast of West Africa. And enslaved Africans were forced onto ships to the Americas to work on the plantations (Topic 4.4). People often draw this as a neat triangle. It was messier: many ships sailed straight from Brazil to Angola and back, and the goods came from all over the world.

None of this replaced the trade that already ran across Africa and Asia. Regional markets kept flourishing, with their own merchants and their own long-standing ways of doing business, such as credit, partnerships, and family firms. Gujarati, Chinese, and Armenian merchants kept trading. European ships became one more carrier inside these markets. Dutch ships carried Japanese silver to Taiwan to buy Chinese silk, Japanese silver and copper to India, and Indian cloth to Southeast Asia, earning money from trade between Asian ports.

AP Thinking, Continuity and Change

Change and continuity side by side. The Atlantic system was new: it tied Africa, Europe, and the Americas into one system of trade across the ocean. The regional markets of Africa and Asia were a continuity: they kept their merchants and their old ways, and European ships joined them.

6

Work: Old and More of It

More Spinning, More Weaving

All this trade meant more demand for food and for goods, and that meant more work for peasants and artisans, the people who farmed or made things by hand at home and in workshops. This is peasant and artisan labor. The work was old. There was more of it, for buyers farther away.

In western Europe, merchants paid families in the countryside to spin and weave wool and linen in their own homes. In India, weavers made cotton cloth for buyers in Asia, Africa, and Europe, and the European companies bought more and more of it. In China, peasant families raised silkworms and wove silk, and some of that silk sailed to Manila to be traded for American silver. Much of this spinning and weaving was done by women and girls.

AP Thinking, Continuity and Change

Two words, both halves. The College Board says this labor "continued and intensified." Continued is the continuity: the same work, done the old way. Intensified is the change: more of it, for distant markets.

7

Society: Families and Cultures

Who the Trade Took, and What Mixed

About two of every three people carried away in the Atlantic slave trade were male. In parts of Africa, especially West Central Africa, that left communities with more women than men. Historians argue that this changed family life there: women did more of the farming, more women and girls were enslaved inside Africa itself, and, some historians argue, more men took several wives (polygyny). The trade changed who lived in a village, and that changed how families worked. These are the "demographic changes in Africa" that the College Board names.

In the Americas, Africans, Indigenous Americans, and Europeans lived side by side, often by force, and each group shaped what came out of it. New dishes mixed African, American, and European foods. New languages formed, called creoles, with most of their words from a European language and some of their grammar and sounds shaped by African languages. Music and religion mixed too. Historians call this cultural synthesis. No one group made it. The College Board's words are "with all parties contributing."

Common Mistake to Avoid

Do not write that European culture simply replaced African and Indigenous cultures in the Americas. Enslaved Africans and Indigenous Americans kept and remade their own ways of life, and the new Atlantic cultures came from all of them.

8

Faith: Compare

Religions Spread, Clash, and Blend

Religions traveled with the ships. Christianity spread to the Americas, the Philippines, and parts of Africa, and Jesuit missionaries reached the court of Ming China. Islam kept spreading in Southeast Asia along its older trade routes.

Contact also caused religious conflict. In Peru, starting around 1610, Spanish priests ran campaigns against what they called idolatry. They destroyed Andean sacred objects and punished people who kept the old religion. (In Topic 4.4, Japan's rulers banned Christianity for their own reasons.)

And contact produced syncretism, the blending of two or more religions into new beliefs and practices. At Tepeyac, a hill near Mexico City, Nahua Christians prayed to the Virgin of Guadalupe. In the 1570s a Spanish friar named Bernardino de Sahagún wrote that the Nahua had once honored a goddess at that hill whom they called Tonantzin, "our mother." (The Skill Builder asks what he claimed about the people praying there in his day.) In Kongo, Christian crucifixes were made by Kongo artists in Kongo style. In the 1700s, a painter in Potosí showed the Virgin Mary as the silver mountain itself. And in the 1700s, enslaved Africans in the French colony of Saint-Domingue (today's Haiti) built a religion called Vodou from West and Central African beliefs and Catholic saints.

AP Thinking, Comparison

Similar and different. The College Board asks how belief systems affected societies in similar and different ways. To compare, put two cases side by side and ask the same questions of each: who carried the religion, who received it, and what happened next. Something the two share is a similarity; a place where they part ways is a difference.

9

The Pattern

Tools to Make Empires Pay, and What They Changed

Put it together. Rulers built new tools to make their empires pay: mercantilist laws, and chartered companies that they aimed at each other. Those tools helped move silver around the world, goods and enslaved people across the Atlantic, and more work into the hands of peasants and artisans, while the old regional markets kept trading. And the silver, goods, and people they moved changed society: families in parts of Africa, a mixed Atlantic culture, and religions that spread, clashed, and blended.

Rulers built economic tools to keep their empires paying, and the money, goods, and people those tools moved reshaped work, families, and faith.

It also leaves a problem. States got richer and reached further. Not everyone accepted it. That is Topic 4.6.

AP Thinking, Continuity and Change

Answer all four. The College Board asks four things in this topic: how rulers used economic tools to hold power, how networks of exchange changed and continued, how society changed, and how belief systems affected societies. For each one, name something new and something that kept going.

BeReady: 10-Second Takeaway

European rulers used mercantilism and chartered joint-stock companies such as the VOC to make their empires pay and to compete with each other, and economic disputes became wars, from the Indian Ocean to the Sahara to the spice islands. American silver flowed around the world toward China, whose taxes were paid in silver, and the Atlantic trading system moved goods, wealth, and enslaved people, while regional markets in Africa and Asia kept flourishing. Peasant and artisan work continued and grew. The slave trade reshaped families in parts of Africa, Africans, Indigenous Americans, and Europeans all made a mixed Atlantic culture, and religions spread, clashed, and blended.
