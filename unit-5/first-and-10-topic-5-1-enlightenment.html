First & 10 Reading

By What Right?

In 1750 most rulers said their power came from God. Then a group of writers asked every old authority a dangerous question: by what right? Their answer, reason and the consent of the people, spread across the Atlantic world before its revolutions. Over the next century, the people it left out pressed the same words until rights reached further. This reading follows the question and its answer to 1900.

1

The Question

Who Does a State Belong To?

Unit 4 ended with a question. By 1750 the oceans were joined and states were stronger than ever. Who do those states belong to?

In 1750 most rulers had one answer: to the king, because God put him there. You met this idea, divine right, in Topic 3.2, when Louis XIV of France claimed that his power came from God and that no one could take it away. The Church taught what was true about God and the world, and in most countries one church was the law. Nobles were born into their privileges. Peasants, many of them serfs bound to work their lord's land, were born into their work. Authority came from God, from tradition, and from birth.

Then a group of writers began asking every one of those authorities the same question: by what right? This reading follows their answer, and what happened to it over the next 150 years.

AP Thinking, Continuity and Change

Start with what was already true. You cannot explain a change until you can say what it changed. Here, the starting point is the old answer: power came from God, tradition, and birth.

2

The Method

A New Way of Knowing

The new question came from a new way of finding out what is true. In 1687 the English scientist Isaac Newton showed that a few laws, worked out with mathematics and tested against what astronomers could see, explained how the planets move and why an apple falls. You did not need an old book or a priest to tell you. You needed reason and evidence.

The English philosopher John Locke argued that everything we know comes from experience. At birth, he wrote, the mind is like "white paper." Learning from evidence and experience, instead of accepting what authorities say, is called empiricism. Thinkers began to ask: if reason and evidence can explain the planets, can they also explain people, and how people should live together and be governed?

They asked it about religion, too. In 1762 a Protestant merchant in the French city of Toulouse, Jean Calas, was tortured and executed after being wrongly accused of killing his son to stop him from becoming a Catholic. The writer Voltaire, one of the most famous in Europe, campaigned for three years to clear his name, and in 1763 he published a Treatise on Tolerance. His argument was religious toleration: no government should force a faith on anyone or punish people for their beliefs. Most of these writers still believed in God. Some were followers of deism, the belief that God created the world and then let it run by natural laws. What they questioned was not faith itself, but religion used as a power of government.

AP Thinking, Continuity and Change

Name what changed, exactly. Religion did not disappear; most people kept their faith, a continuity. What changed was the role religion played in public life: more and more thinkers argued that faith was a private choice, not something the state should force.

3

The New Answers

Rights, Consent, and Limits

Then these thinkers turned the method on government. They began with the individual person, not the king, and came up with three new political ideas.

The first was natural rights. In 1689, Locke argued that people are born free, with rights to their "lives, liberties and estates" (an estate is what you own). No government gives these rights, and no government may take them away. Government exists to protect them.

The second was the social contract: government is an agreement. People give some of their freedom to a government so that it will protect their rights. "No one," Locke wrote, can be put under another person's power "without his own consent." If a government breaks the deal and attacks the people's rights, the people may resist it. In 1762 the French-speaking writer Jean-Jacques Rousseau began his own book on the social contract with a famous line: "Man is born free; and everywhere he is in chains."

The third was limiting power. In 1748 the French writer Montesquieu argued that liberty is safest when the power to make laws, the power to carry them out, and the power to judge are held by different people. This idea is called the separation of powers.

Notice what changed. These ideas did not get rid of kings. They changed what made a ruler legitimate, meaning rightful. The old answer was God and birth. Locke's and Rousseau's new answer was reason and the consent of the people, and Montesquieu added that power must be divided so that no one could abuse it.

AP Thinking, Claims and Evidence in Sources

Find the claim. A claim is what a writer wants you to believe. Montesquieu's claim is that people stay free only when no one person or group holds all the power. To describe a claim, say it in your own words, then say what it argues against: here, letting one ruler make the laws, carry them out, and judge them all at once.

4

The Spread

The Ideas Travel, Before the Revolutions

These ideas were not secrets. They traveled in books, newspapers, and pamphlets; in London's coffeehouses; in letters between writers across Europe and the Atlantic; and in the conversation gatherings of Paris called salons, which were often run by women. From 1751 to 1772, Denis Diderot and his partners published the Encyclopédie, seventeen volumes of articles and eleven of pictures that tried to explain everything by reason, from how to make glass to what a government owes its people. The French government banned it, and more volumes kept coming out anyway. The new thinking questioned old traditions everywhere: in science, religion, law, farming, and trade. In 1784 the German philosopher Immanuel Kant summed it up in a motto: "Dare to know," which he explained as having the courage to use your own understanding.

And the ideas came before the revolutions. The American Revolution began in 1775, the French Revolution in 1789, the Haitian Revolution in 1791, and the wars for independence in Spanish America in 1810. In Bogotá, in Spanish America, a young man named Antonio Nariño secretly printed a Spanish translation of France's new declaration of rights in 1793 or 1794, and was arrested for it in 1794. He later became a leader of independence. The ideas did not cause the revolutions by themselves; Topic 5.2 is about everything else that did. But they gave revolutionaries their words.

Those words raised another question. If a state belongs to its people, which people? More and more, the answer was the nation, a people who saw themselves as one. In 1789 the French revolutionaries wrote that "the principle of all sovereignty resides essentially in the nation" (sovereignty means the highest power in a state). Over the next century, nationalism, loyalty to your nation above a king or an empire, became one of the strongest forces shaping states and empires. It would build new countries and pull old empires apart, which you will see in Topic 5.2.

AP Thinking, Continuity and Change

"Came before" is not the same as "caused." The College Board says Enlightenment thought "often preceded" revolutions. The ideas changed what people believed a government owed them. Whether people actually rebelled depended on other causes too. Say both.

5

Over Time

Pressing the Words Further

Here is the turn. "All men," "the people," "natural rights": the words sounded like everyone. But the men who wrote them often did not mean everyone. Locke himself had owned shares in the Royal African Company, an English company that traded enslaved Africans. Rousseau wrote that "a woman's education must therefore be planned in relation to man." Most of the men who wrote and read these ideas did not mean them for women, enslaved Africans, or peasants. Yet the words were written down, and anyone who read them could ask the philosophers' own question: by what right am I left out?

From the 1780s on, people formed reform movements to make the rights real for more people. They were driven by Enlightenment ideas and also by religious faith. The movement for abolition, ending slavery, is the clearest case. In 1787 twelve men in London, nine of them Quakers, a Christian group that opposed slavery, founded a society to end the British slave trade. Its seal showed a kneeling African man in chains and the words "Am I not a man and a brother?", and the potter Josiah Wedgwood turned it into a medallion that supporters wore. Olaudah Equiano, who had been enslaved and wrote that he was born in Africa, published the story of his life in 1789 and spoke across Britain. Britain banned the slave trade in 1807. In 1834 it began ending slavery in most of its empire, though freed people had to keep working for their former owners as unpaid "apprentices" until 1838. France ended slavery in its colonies in 1848. Brazil, the last country in the Americas, ended it in 1888.

Serfdom, a system in which peasants were bound to the land and owed labor to a lord, ended too. In 1848 the Austrian Empire freed its peasants from the forced labor they owed their lords. In 1861 Tsar Alexander II of Russia freed more than 20 million serfs, partly, he reportedly told Russian nobles, because it was better to end serfdom "from above" than to wait for the serfs to end it "from below."

And suffrage, the right to vote, widened. In 1848 France gave the vote to all adult men. Britain widened voting in steps, in 1832, 1867, and 1884. Each of these changes took decades of campaigning, met resistance, and still did not reach everyone.

AP Thinking, Continuity and Change

Change can be slow. The ideas changed quickly, in a few decades. The rights took more than a century to reach the enslaved, the serfs, and the voters, and many people were still waiting. When you explain change over time, say how fast it came, and for whom.

6

Women's Demands

"All Men and Women Are Created Equal"

Women pressed the same words too, because the men who wrote about the "rights of man" often meant only men. In 1791 the French writer Olympe de Gouges rewrote France's declaration of rights as the Declaration of the Rights of Woman and of the Female Citizen. Its first article, translated from the French, reads: "Woman is born free and remains equal to man in rights." The revolutionary government executed her in 1793.

In 1792 the English writer Mary Wollstonecraft published A Vindication of the Rights of Woman. Rousseau had warned that if women were educated like men, they would have less power over men. Wollstonecraft answered: "I do not wish them to have power over men; but over themselves." Her claim was that women can reason as men can, so they must be educated to think and decide for themselves.

In 1840 the American abolitionist Lucretia Mott, a Quaker minister, went to an antislavery convention in London as a delegate and was refused a seat because she was a woman. Elizabeth Cady Stanton, who was there with her husband, a delegate, sat with Mott and the other women, apart from the men. In July 1848 the two of them, with three other women, organized a women's rights convention in Seneca Falls, New York. Its Declaration of Sentiments borrowed the words of the Declaration of Independence and changed them: "We hold these truths to be self-evident; that all men and women are created equal." It demanded the right to vote. That demand was the only resolution at the convention that did not pass unanimously, and 100 women and men signed the declaration.

This was feminism emerging: the claim that women and men should have equal rights. It challenged political hierarchy, who votes and who rules, and gender hierarchy, who is in charge at home and under the law.

Now look at what stayed. By 1900, kings and emperors still ruled most of Europe and much of the world. Slavery had ended across the Americas, but other kinds of forced labor went on in many places. Many countries let some or all men vote, but women could vote in national elections almost nowhere; New Zealand, in 1893, was the first self-governing country where all adult women could vote in national elections.

So, who does a state belong to? The Enlightenment changed the answer: to its people, by reason and consent, not to a king by God's will. The question it taught people to ask kept working long after the philosophers were gone, as the people left out pressed the same words until rights reached further. That is the change. How slowly the rights reached, and how many people were still waiting in 1900, is the continuity.

By 1789 the ideas were everywhere educated people read. In America, France, Haiti, and Spanish America, people turned them into revolutions. What caused those revolutions, and what did they create? That is Topic 5.2.

AP Thinking, Claims and Evidence in Sources

Compare a claim to the words it borrows. The Seneca Falls declaration copied an older sentence and added two words, "and women." To describe its claim, say what it kept, what it changed, and what it was arguing against.

BeReady: 10-Second Takeaway

Before the Enlightenment, most rulers said their power came from God, tradition, and birth. Enlightenment thinkers used reason and evidence, first to explain nature and then to question every old authority, and they answered with new ideas: natural rights, the social contract, limits on power, and religious toleration. The ideas spread through print, salons, and letters, came before the Atlantic revolutions, and turned "the people" into the nation. Over the next century, reformers driven by these ideas and by religious faith pressed the words further: slavery and serfdom were abolished, voting widened, and women such as de Gouges, Wollstonecraft, Stanton, and Mott demanded equal rights. By 1900 much of the old order still stood. By what right? The answer kept growing.
