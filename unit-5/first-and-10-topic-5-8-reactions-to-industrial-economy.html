First & 10 Reading

Calls for Change

Industry made some people rich and left many working long hours for little pay. It also made a few countries strong enough to push the rest of the world around. This reading follows the people who called for change, why they called, and what their calls changed.

The Pressure

One City, Two Pictures

Topic 5.7 ended with a turn: not everyone gained from the new industrial economy, and not everyone accepted it. This reading is about the people who called for change. In every part, look for the same four things: the pressure that pushed people, who called for change, what they wanted, and what actually happened.

Start with Manchester, England, the great cotton town. In 1845 a young German named Friedrich Engels published a book about the streets where Manchester's mill workers lived. Engels had been sent to Manchester to work in a cotton business his father partly owned. He described crowded houses, filthy streets and a river, the Irk, that was "coal-black, foul-smelling ... full of debris and refuse." He also noticed that the town was built so that "a person may live in it for years, and go in and out daily without coming into contact with a working people's quarter or even with workers."

In the early 1850s, Whellan and Company, publishers of directories for towns in the North and Midlands of England, printed a business directory of Manchester, a book listing the town's firms and their addresses. It opened with a very different picture: "the innumerable large mills and factories whose tall chimneys shoot up in every direction, betoken the activity, enterprise, and wealth of its inhabitants." Same city, two pictures. Which is more accurate? You will decide in the Skill Builder. Either way, industry made some people rich and left many working long hours for low pay in crowded, unhealthy towns. Topic 5.9 takes that city apart. This topic is about what people did about it.

AP Thinking, Sourcing and Situation

Ask four questions of any source. Who made it (point of view)? Why (purpose)? For whom (audience)? When and where (historical situation)? Then say how one of those answers shapes what the source says. A directory publisher's book and a critic's book can describe the same town and both be telling part of the truth.

2

Workers, Part 1

At First, a Crime

A single worker who asked for more pay could simply be fired. So workers looked for strength in numbers. A labor union (in Britain, a trade union) is a group of workers who bargain with their employer together, and who can strike, stopping work together, when bargaining fails.

At first, in Britain, this was a crime. The Combination Acts of 1799 and 1800 made it illegal for workers to join together ("combine") to demand higher wages or shorter hours. Parliament repealed them in 1824. A law of 1825 then limited what unions could do, but workers could now form them. The risks stayed real. In 1834 six farm laborers from Tolpuddle, a village in Dorset, were sentenced to seven years as convicts in Australia for swearing a secret oath when they formed a union. Huge protests followed, and in 1836 they were pardoned.

Unions grew anyway. Skilled workers built lasting unions for their trades, and in 1868 unions formed a national body, the Trades Union Congress. A law of 1871 gave unions legal standing. Unions fought for what the College Board names: better working conditions, shorter hours and higher wages. After years of campaigning by workers and their allies, Parliament passed a law in 1847 that limited women and young people in textile mills to ten hours of work a day from 1848.

AP Thinking, Causation

Start your first row. Workers: what was the pressure, what did they want, and what happened? Notice that one effect (the 1824 repeal) became a cause of the next step (unions growing).

3

Workers, Part 2

A Charter and a Party

Workers also wanted a voice in making the laws. In 1838 a movement of working people called the Chartists took its name from a People's Charter of six demands. The first was a vote for every man over 21; most of the others aimed to let working men sit in Parliament and vote without fear. More than a million people signed the first Chartist petition in 1839, and the Chartists claimed over three million for the second in 1842. Parliament rejected both, and a third in 1848. On 10 April 1848, a photographer took pictures of a great Chartist meeting on Kennington Common in London, and Prince Albert, Queen Victoria's husband, bought them.

Later in the century, workers built political parties. In Germany, two workers' parties united in 1875. The government banned socialist meetings and newspapers from 1878 to 1890, but the party survived. In February 1890, while it was still banned, it won more votes than any other party; that autumn the ban ended and it renamed itself the Social Democratic Party. In Britain, unions and socialists set up a committee in 1900 to elect working men to Parliament; in 1906 it became the Labour Party. In the United States, the American Federation of Labor, founded in 1886, organized skilled workers for better wages and hours.

Many of these parties wanted more than better pay. They wanted a different kind of society. The College Board calls these "alternative visions of society." Where did those visions come from?

AP Thinking, Causation

Effects can be mixed. The Chartists lost every petition, yet in the decades after, Parliament gave the vote to many working men (Section 5), and in 1918 to every man over 21. When you explain an effect, say what changed, and what did not.

4

Thinkers

Imagining a Different Society

Some people did not want to fix the factory system. They wanted to replace it. In the 1820s and 1830s, followers of Robert Owen, a businessman who had managed the New Lanark cotton mills in Scotland with shorter hours and a school for workers' children, began to call their ideas socialism: the idea that land, factories and the other things a society uses to make wealth should be owned and run in common, for everyone, and not by private owners for their own profit.

In February 1848, the same Friedrich Engels and a German writer, Karl Marx, published The Communist Manifesto. Its first section opens:

"The history of all hitherto existing society is the history of class struggles."

Marx argued that industry was splitting society into two classes: the owners of factories, and the workers, who owned nothing but their ability to work. One day, he predicted, the workers would overthrow the owners, end private ownership of factories and land, and build a society with no classes at all. He called that end point communism. The Manifesto ends: "The proletarians have nothing to lose but their chains. They have a world to win. Working Men of All Countries, Unite!" (Proletarians was Marx's word for wage workers.) Marx later wrote Capital (1867) in London.

No revolution like the one Marx predicted won lasting power in an industrial country before 1900. But his ideas spread through workers' parties. In 1891 Germany's Social Democrats adopted a program based on Marx's ideas, while they kept winning votes in elections. In 1889 socialist parties from many countries met in Paris and called on workers in every country to march on May 1, 1890, for an eight-hour working day. Socialists kept marching on May 1 in the years after. Communist governments came later, in the twentieth century.

AP Thinking, Causation

Name the cause exactly. The College Board says "discontent with established power structures" encouraged these ideas: anger at who held power, not only at low wages. Fill in your thinkers row: what pressure, what they wanted, and what their ideas did by 1900.

5

Reformers and Governments

Fixing the Worst of It

Not every call for change came from workers. The College Board names three kinds of reformers, "governments, organizations, and individuals," and four kinds of reform, a change to a law or a system to fix a problem without overthrowing it. Britain had all four:

Political. After a workers' organization, the Reform League, held huge meetings for the vote, Parliament passed Reform Acts in 1867 and 1884 that gave the vote to many working men. No woman could vote in a British national election until 1918. Social. A Factory Act of 1833 banned children under nine from working in textile mills and sent inspectors to check, and a law of 1842, brought in by Lord Ashley, a member of Parliament who campaigned for factory children, stopped women, girls and boys under ten from working inside mines. Educational. An Education Act of 1870, written by a government minister, William Forster, after a campaign by the National Education League, set up public elementary schools where church schools were not enough, and from 1880 children had to attend. Urban. In the hot summer of 1858 the River Thames, which carried London's sewage, smelled so foul that it was called the "Great Stink." London's city government then had its engineer, Joseph Bazalgette, build a huge system of new sewers to carry the waste away from the city.

Sometimes a government reformed to defeat its critics. In Germany, Chancellor Otto von Bismarck banned the socialists in 1878, and then gave workers health insurance (1883), insurance for injuries at work (1884) and pensions for old age and disability (1889). He hoped, in part, to win workers away from the socialists. Reform could answer workers, and also be a weapon against their parties.

AP Thinking, Causation

One cause, several effects. Workers' pressure helped cause reforms, and reforms changed what workers fought for next. In your reformers row, name one reform of each kind you can, and say who pushed it.

Old Empires, Part 1

A Different Pressure: The Ottoman Empire

Outside Europe, the pressure was different. The Ottoman Empire and Qing China had few factories of their own. Their pressure came from the industrial powers: their armies, warships and trade. Like Egypt and Japan in Topic 5.6, both governments tried to reform and modernize their economies and armies. Both met resistance from people inside their own governments and elites.

In the 1790s the Ottoman Sultan Selim III built a new army in European-style uniforms, with imported rifles and French instructors, called the "New Order." The Janissaries, the empire's old army corps, saw it as a threat, and many conservative religious scholars sided with them. In 1807 a revolt overthrew Selim. In June 1826 Sultan Mahmud II destroyed the Janissaries and built a new army.

In 1839 the government announced a program of reforms called the Tanzimat, Turkish for "reorganization." It promised all subjects safety for their lives, honor and property, fair taxes and a regular system of military service. A decree in 1856 promised equal treatment to Christians and Jews as well as Muslims. In the 1830s, before the Tanzimat, Sultan Mahmud II's government had opened a state fez factory in Istanbul; in the Tanzimat years it moved to a new site and got steam engines and machines from Britain and Belgium, and from 1855 the state built telegraph lines. Many Muslims resented the 1856 promise of equality, because it ended their place as the empire's ruling community. In 1876 the empire got a constitution and an elected parliament. Two years later the new sultan, Abdulhamid II, shut the parliament down.

AP Thinking, Causation

Same pattern, different cause. In your fourth row, the pressure is not factory conditions at home but the power of the industrial states. Then look for the College Board's last sentence: reforms were "often resisted by some members of government or established elite groups." Who resisted here?

7

Old Empires, Part 2

Self-Strengthening in Qing China

Qing China was defeated by Britain in the Opium War of 1839 to 1842, and by Britain and France again from 1856 to 1860 (Unit 6), while a huge civil war, the Taiping Rebellion, tore through the country. From the 1860s, officials such as Zeng Guofan and Li Hongzhang led what was called Self-Strengthening: learning Western ways of making weapons and ships while keeping China's own system of rule. They built the Jiangnan Arsenal in Shanghai (1865) and a navy yard at Fuzhou (1866), opened a school for foreign languages in Beijing (1862), and started a Chinese steamship company (1872).

Resistance came from inside. In 1867, when the court planned to teach Western astronomy and mathematics at the language school, Woren, a senior official and the young emperor's tutor, objected: "establishing a country rests on valuing propriety and righteousness, not on schemes and stratagems." The court overruled him and the plan went ahead, but few of the well-educated young officials it hoped for applied. In 1894 and 1895 Japan defeated China's new navy. In 1898 the young Guangxu Emperor ordered sweeping reforms for about a hundred days, until the Empress Dowager Cixi and officials who opposed the reforms stopped them, kept the emperor under guard, and had six reformers executed.

Put the four rows together. Industrial capitalism and discontent with who held power caused workers to organize unions and parties and thinkers such as Marx to imagine a different society, and those calls pushed governments, organizations and individuals into reforms. By 1900 many men could vote, children worked less and went to school more, and socialist parties were among the largest in Europe, but many workers stayed poor and no woman could vote. The power of the industrial states caused the Ottoman and Qing governments to modernize, but resistance from inside slowed or reversed the deepest changes.

Industry had changed who worked, where people lived and how families got by. What kind of society did it leave behind? That is Topic 5.9.

AP Thinking, Causation

Three myths to drop. "Marx's revolution came in the 1800s": no communist party ruled a country until the twentieth century. "Unions were legal once the 1824 law passed": an 1825 law limited them, and full legal standing came in 1871. "China and the Ottoman Empire refused to change": both governments tried to modernize; the reforms were resisted from inside.

BeReady: 10-Second Takeaway

Industrial capitalism made some people rich and left many working long hours for low pay, and workers called for change. In Britain, unions were banned until 1824, grew through the century, and gained legal standing in 1871; the Chartists demanded the vote for working men from 1838; and workers' parties rose in Germany (a socialist party, banned from 1878 to 1890) and Britain (the Labour Party, 1900 to 1906). Discontent with who held power encouraged new ideas: socialism, and the communism of Karl Marx and Friedrich Engels, whose Communist Manifesto appeared in 1848. Governments, organizations and individuals pushed reforms of four kinds: political (the Reform Acts of 1867 and 1884), social (the Factory Act of 1833), educational (the Education Act of 1870) and urban (London's sewers after 1858), and Bismarck gave German workers insurance partly to weaken the socialists. Outside Europe, the power of the industrial states pushed the Ottoman Empire (the Tanzimat, from 1839) and Qing China (Self-Strengthening, from the 1860s) to modernize their armies and economies, against resistance from their own elites. Workers organized. Thinkers imagined. Governments reformed. Old empires modernized, and met resistance.
