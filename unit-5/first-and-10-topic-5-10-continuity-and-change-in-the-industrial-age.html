How Much Changed?

Between 1750 and 1900, industry changed how the world made and moved things. But how far did the change reach, and what kept going beside it? Today you weigh the whole unit and argue how much changed.

1

The Question

Measure a Change

Topic 5.9 ended with a question. Industry changed almost everything in a hundred and fifty years. Or did it?

Today you are the historian. This topic gives you no new revolution and no new machine. It gives you the College Board's question, and you already have the evidence from Topics 5.1 to 5.9: to what extent did industrialization bring change from 1750 to 1900? "To what extent" means how much. To measure a change, ask three things. How big was it? How far did it reach, to which places and which people? And what kept going beside it? What stays the same over time is called continuity.

Start with the starting line. In 1750 almost everything people bought was made by hand, at home or in small workshops, and most of the world's manufactured goods were made in Asia: the historian Paul Bairoch estimated that China made about a third and India about a quarter, while Britain made under 2 percent (Topic 5.4). Almost all energy came from things that grow: muscles, wood, wind and water. A message traveled only as fast as a horse or a ship. Most people farmed. And most rulers said their power came from God and birth (Topic 5.1).

AP Thinking, Continuity and Change

A change needs a starting line. You cannot say how much changed until you know what was true before. Keep 1750 in mind as you read: every change below is measured from it.

2

Making

Factories, Coal, and Cheaper Goods

The first big change was in how goods were made. Steam engines turned the energy stored in coal into motion, and from the 1780s James Watt and Matthew Boulton built engines that turned wheels, so a mill could run wherever coal could be brought (Topic 5.5). By about 1900, coal supplied about half of all the energy used in the world.

Factory owners and investors ran this new economy for profit, a system called industrial capitalism. Factories kept improving how they made things, so consumer goods, the things people buy for themselves, became more available, more affordable and more varied. By 1815 even the coarsest cotton yarn cost about a third of what it had in the early 1780s, after rising prices are taken into account, and soap came in wrapped bars with a brand name, like Sunlight, sold from 1884 (Topic 5.7).

The places that made things moved, too. Britain's share of the world's manufacturing went from under 2 percent in 1750 to about a fifth by 1860, and by 1900 Europe and the United States together made more than four-fifths of the world's manufactured goods (Topic 5.4). And a family's standard of living, how well it lives, rose, for some. One careful estimate finds that British working families' standard of living rose by less than 15 percent from the 1780s to the 1850s; most historians agree it grew much faster from about 1860 to 1900 (Topic 5.7).

AP Thinking, Continuity and Change

Read the College Board's limit. Its sentence says standards of living rose "for some." Those two words are a measure of extent: the change was real, and it was unequal. Write "for whom?" next to this change.

3

Moving

Rails, Steam, and Wire

The second big change was in how goods, people and news moved. Before the railroad, heavy goods moved cheaply only by water, and the interior of a continent, the land far from the sea or a big river, was hard to reach. Then railways spread. Russia had 27 kilometers of railway in 1840 and more than 53,000 in 1900. India's first passenger train ran in 1853. The first railroad across the United States was finished in 1869 (Topic 5.5).

Ships changed too. On the main emigrant route, from Liverpool, Britain's chief emigrant port, to New York, a voyage took more than 40 days on average in 1853, when emigrant ships still went under sail; by 1869, as steamships took over, it took about 16. And news stopped waiting for ships. By the 1840s a telegraph message could travel along a wire in minutes (Samuel Morse's line from Washington to Baltimore opened in 1844); from 1866, cables under the Atlantic carried messages without a lasting break, and in 1870 a cable reached Bombay, in India.

So trade and migration, people moving to live in a new place, grew. In 1870 wheat cost 57.6 percent more in Liverpool, England, than in Chicago; by 1913, just after this unit's period, it cost only 15.6 percent more, because trains and steamships carried North American wheat to British workers. About 55 million Europeans moved to the Americas and Australia between 1850 and 1914, and between 1846 and 1940 more than 29 million Indians and more than 19 million Chinese moved to Southeast Asia and the lands around the Indian Ocean (Topic 5.5).

AP Thinking, Continuity and Change

Compare the two changes. The change in making happened mostly in a few regions. The change in moving reached the interiors of continents around the world. Which one reached further?

4

How Far It Reached

The Other Side of the Shield

In 1898 the naturalist Alfred Russel Wallace, famous for working out the theory of evolution by natural selection on his own, separately from Charles Darwin (their papers were read together in 1858), looked back over his own century. He wrote that "not only is our century superior to any that have gone before it, but that it may be best compared with the whole preceding historical period." In other words: this one century changed as much as all of history before it. Is he right? He went on: "this is only one side of the shield." Put each change beside its limits.

The old makers kept making. India, China and the Middle East did not stop working. Their share of the world's goods shrank, and China's fell from about a third in 1750 to about 6 percent in 1900. But in 1908, in just one province of northern India, about a million people still lived from weaving cloth on handlooms (Topic 5.4).

Most people still farmed. In India, the census of 1901 found about two-thirds of the people living by farming. Even in the industrial United States, about two in five workers still farmed in 1900. And until about 1900, wood and other things that grow were still the world's largest source of energy (Topic 5.5).

Only a few states built industry, and not all of it lasted. Muhammad Ali's Egypt built factories for its army, and most of them had closed by the mid-1840s. Meiji Japan built railways and mills that lasted (Topic 5.6).

The gains went to some. Industry rebuilt the ladder of rank instead of ending it: landed families kept much of their power, and in 1900 Britain's prime minister, Lord Salisbury, was a marquess. In the 1840s a baby born in Manchester could expect to live only about 25 to 28 years, against about 40 to 42 for England and Wales as a whole (Topic 5.9). Wallace went further. He wrote of "the enormous and continuous growth of wealth, without any corresponding increase in the well-being of the whole people." Historians since have found that working families' living standards did rise, slowly before the 1850s and faster after about 1860. So Wallace was partly right: the gains were unequal, but they were not nothing.

AP Thinking, Argumentation

Test a witness against the evidence. Wallace lived through the century, but a witness can overstate. Check his claim against what Topics 5.4 to 5.9 found. Which part holds up, and which part goes too far?

5

The Other Revolution

Ideas and Nations

Not every big change of these years was industry's. The Enlightenment was a movement of thinkers who used reason and evidence, first to explain nature and then to question old authorities, including religion used as a power of government. In 1689 John Locke argued that people are born with natural rights to "lives, liberties and estates," and that government is a social contract that rests on the people's consent (Topic 5.1).

Those ideas came before the revolutions. The American Revolution began in 1775, the French Revolution in 1789, the Haitian Revolution in 1791, and the wars for independence in Spanish America in 1810. The revolutions built new countries: the United States, Haiti and the new republics of Spanish America. Each was a nation-state, a state that claims to belong to one people, its nation. And they left a new loyalty, nationalism, loyalty to your nation above a king or an empire. Nationalism joined most of Italy into one kingdom in 1861 and the German states into an empire in 1871, and it pulled Greeks and other Balkan peoples away from the Ottoman Empire (Topic 5.2).

The revolutions of 1775 to 1826 began while factories were still almost all in Britain. Their causes were ideas and grievances, not machines. Later, the two revolutions met. Railways and iron helped Prussia win the wars that united Germany in 1871. Nationalism pushed some governments, such as Meiji Japan, to build industry to make the nation strong (Topic 5.6), and workers in industrial cities used the language of rights to demand the vote, as Britain's Chartists did (Topic 5.8). And the old order held on in politics too. In 1900 kings and emperors still ruled most of Europe, and women could vote in national elections almost nowhere; New Zealand, in 1893, was the first self-governing country where all adult women could (Topic 5.1).

AP Thinking, Continuity and Change

Sort the changes by cause. Before you credit industry with a change, check its date and its cause. The Atlantic revolutions came before the factories spread, from ideas and grievances. Later changes, like Germany's unification, may have had both kinds of cause.

6

Make the Argument

How Evidence Connects

A list of evidence is not an argument. An argument explains how the pieces of evidence relate. Three relationships do most of the work.

This led to that. Coal and steam engines led to railroads and steamships, which led to cheap wheat from the middle of North America feeding workers in Britain.

The same pattern, in two places. Railways reached the interior of Russia and the interior of India: the same change, in two different empires.

A contrast. Lancashire's power looms made cloth more cheaply than India's hand weavers could, yet a million people in one Indian province still lived from the handloom in 1908. When you set a change beside a continuity, you can say how much changed.

Then weigh it. Your thesis is the claim that answers the question, and you must be able to defend it with evidence. A strong thesis about extent takes a side (to a great extent, to a limited extent, or great in some ways and limited in others), names a change and a continuity, and says for whom or where the change was greatest, and why.

AP Thinking, Argumentation

Say the relationship out loud. "This led to that because ..." "These show the same pattern, because both ..." "This changed, but that kept going, because ..." The linking words are what turn facts into an argument.

7

Put It Together

Deep Change, Uneven Reach

Put your two columns together. Industrialization changed how goods were made, with coal and factories, and how goods, people and news moved, with railroads, steamships and the telegraph. Goods became cheaper and more varied, and standards of living rose for some. But the change reached some places and people far more than others: the old makers kept making by hand, most of the world's people still farmed in 1900, and the gains were unequal. Meanwhile the other revolution, in ideas and governments, began before the factories spread and had causes of its own. Deep change, uneven reach. How much changed is now your argument to make.

Industrial states now needed raw materials and markets, and had new tools to take them. That is Unit 6.

AP Thinking, Continuity and Change

Two mistakes to avoid. "Everything changed" forgets the farmers, the handloom weavers and the kings still on their thrones. "Nothing really changed" forgets the railroads, the telegraph and the price of cloth. The answer is a measurement in between, with evidence.

BeReady: 10-Second Takeaway

To what extent did industrialization bring change from 1750 to 1900? Measure it from the starting line of 1750, when goods were made by hand, mostly in Asia, and energy came from things that grow. Industry changed how goods were made: coal and steam powered factories, consumer goods became cheaper and more varied, Europe and the United States came to make more than four-fifths of the world's manufactured goods, and standards of living rose for some. It changed how goods, people and news moved: railroads, steamships and the telegraph reached the interiors of continents, so trade grew and tens of millions of people migrated. But it reached some places and people far more than others: the old makers kept making by hand, most people in India and many in the United States still farmed in 1900, and the gains were unequal, as Alfred Russel Wallace complained in 1898. The other revolution of the age, in ideas and governments, came from the Enlightenment and from grievances, began before the factories spread, and built new nation-states and nationalism, while kings and emperors still ruled most of Europe in 1900. Deep change, uneven reach.
