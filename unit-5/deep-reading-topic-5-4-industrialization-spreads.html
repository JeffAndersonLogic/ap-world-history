Who Made the World's Goods?

In 1750 most of the world's goods were made by hand, and most of them in Asia. Then the factory left Britain. This chapter follows how it traveled and where it took root, how the world's shares of manufacturing moved, and what the old makers kept making while their share shrank.

01

The World's Makers in 1750

About 1750  ·  The starting point

In 1750 almost everything was made by hand, at home or in workshops, and most of the world's manufactured goods were made in China and India.

Made by hand

In 1750 a shirt, a pot, a plow or a ship was made by people using hand tools, at home or in small workshops. This is hand production. Power came from muscles, animals, wind and water wheels. That was true in Britain, too: Arkwright's mill at Cromford was still twenty years away.

The places that made the most were the places with the most people and the most skilled craftworkers. India's cotton cloth, from fine thin muslins to cheap printed calicoes, was sold across the Indian Ocean, in the Middle East, in West Africa and in Europe. Chinese silk, porcelain and cotton cloth filled China's own huge markets and traveled abroad. Indian shipbuilders built ocean-going ships of teak.

Who made how much

Nobody counted every piece of cloth in the world in 1750. But the economic historian Paul Bairoch made careful estimates, working backward from what is known about people and their work. He estimated that around 1750 China made about a third of all the world's manufactured goods and India about a quarter. All of Europe together made about a quarter. Britain made under 2 percent.

How we know: estimates, not counts

Bairoch's figures were published in 1982 and reprinted in Paul Kennedy's The Rise and Fall of the Great Powers (1987). They are built from guesses about population and output, and historians who use them say they are rough. Use them to show the size of the change, never as exact amounts.

Strongest tool, with its mechanism
Set the starting point. Before you say what changed, say how and where most goods were made in 1750.
Best evidence of exclusion or limit
The 1750 figures are estimates; say "about," and use them for size, not precision.
Comparison hook
Against 1900 (section 04): in 1750 Asia made most of the world's goods; by 1900 Europe and the United States did.

Terms to use precisely

Manufacturing
Making goods, such as cloth, tools and pots, by hand or by machine.
Hand production
Making goods with hand tools, at home or in workshops, without powered machines.
Calico
A cotton cloth, often printed with colored patterns, made in India and later copied in Britain.
02

Keeping the Secret, and Losing It

1789 to 1824  ·  The methods leave Britain

Britain tried to keep its new methods at home by law. They left anyway, first in the heads of skilled people.

The laws

Britain's leaders knew the new machines were worth a great deal. British law banned skilled workers in the textile trades from leaving to work abroad, and banned the export of textile machines. The ban on workers leaving was repealed in 1824; the last bans on exporting machinery were lifted in 1843.

Samuel Slater

Samuel Slater learned the cotton trade as an apprentice at Jedediah Strutt's mill at Belper, in Derbyshire. Strutt was Arkwright's partner, so Slater learned Arkwright's machines from the inside. In 1789 he sailed for the United States without telling anyone what he knew. At Pawtucket, Rhode Island, working with American merchants, he built water-powered spinning machines from memory. The mill that still stands there was begun in 1793 and enlarged several times afterward.

How we know: the mill is still there

The Slater Mill still stands at Pawtucket, part of the Blackstone River Valley National Historical Park, run by the National Park Service. It was begun in 1793 and enlarged several times by the 1830s, so a photograph of it today shows the enlarged mill, not the first one.

John Cockerill

In Belgium, John Cockerill, the son of an English machine maker, began building machines at Seraing, near Liège, in 1817, and added ironworks there in the 1820s. Liège sat on one of the richest coalfields on the continent of Europe, with rivers to carry goods. The pattern from Topic 5.3, coal near water, was waiting there.

03

Machines, Money and Coal

1820s to 1900  ·  Other parts of Europe, the US, Russia, Japan

Machines and money carried the methods too, and most took root near coal, or by rivers that could turn wheels: in other parts of Europe, in the United States, and later in Russia and Japan.

Other parts of Europe

The first railway in the German states, from Nuremberg to Fürth, opened in 1835 with a locomotive built in England. On the continent, factories grew where coal lay, as they had in Britain: around Liège in Belgium, in the Ruhr valley of the German states, and in northern France.

The United States

In the United States, wealthy Boston merchants paid for big cotton mills at Lowell, Massachusetts, from the 1820s, beside a river that could turn their wheels. Most of the workers were young women from New England farms. By 1900 the United States made more of the world's manufactured goods than any other country.

Russia and Japan

Russia and Japan came later, mostly after 1870. A Welsh engineer, John Hughes, built ironworks in the coal country of southern Russia in the early 1870s; the town that grew up around them was named Yuzovka, after him. In Japan the Osaka Spinning Company opened in 1883 with cotton-spinning machines made in Britain, driven by steam power, and it ran day and night.

Common mistake to avoid

Do not write that industry "spread everywhere." The College Board names four places it spread to: other parts of Europe, the United States, Russia and Japan. Within them, it clustered where coal and iron were found. How the Russian and Japanese governments pushed industry on purpose is Topic 5.6.

Terms to use precisely

Steam power
Power from an engine that burns fuel, usually coal, to boil water and turn the steam into motion.
Locomotive
A steam engine on wheels that pulls a railway train.
04

The Shares Move

1750 to 1900  ·  What changed

Steam-powered factories made goods far faster and more cheaply than hands could, so the regions that had them made a fast-growing share of the world's goods, and the share made in Asia fell.

What changed

A share is a part of a whole. In Bairoch's estimates, Britain's share of the world's manufacturing went from under 2 percent in 1750 to about a fifth by 1860. By 1900 Europe and the United States together made more than four-fifths of the world's manufactured goods, and the United States had passed Britain as the largest maker.

Over the same years, China's share fell from about a third to about 6 percent, and India's from about a quarter to under 2 percent.

A share is not an amount

Imagine a pizza that grows much bigger. Your slice can stay the same size while your share of the pizza shrinks. The world was making far more goods in 1900 than in 1750. So a region's share can fall even while it keeps making goods, because the total grows faster. For India, though, Bairoch's estimates suggest it also made less per person over time: it lost ground, not only share.

Common mistake to avoid

Do not write that Asia "stopped manufacturing." The College Board's own sentence says Middle Eastern and Asian countries "continued to produce manufactured goods" while their share "declined." A falling share is the change; the making that went on is the continuity.

Use this in your answer
Strongest tool, with its mechanism
Use two numbers. Give a share for 1750 and a share for 1900, and say "estimated."
Best evidence of exclusion or limit
Shares say nothing by themselves about whether people were better or worse off.
Comparison hook
Against section 05: the share is the change; the weavers and shipbuilders are the continuity.

Terms to use precisely

Share
A part of a whole; here, the part of all the world's manufactured goods made in one region.
Estimate
A careful guess built from the evidence that exists, not an exact count.
05

The Old Makers Kept Making

1750 to 1900  ·  What continued, and why the share fell

India, China and the Middle East kept making cloth, ships and other goods, much of it by hand. Their share shrank because the rest of the world made far more, because machine-made goods were cheaper, and because of the trade rules of the people in power, among other causes historians still argue about.

What continued

In India, hand weaving shrank, but it did not die. Weavers kept working cloth on handlooms, and many switched to cheaper machine-spun thread so they could keep weaving. The economic historian Tirthankar Roy finds that handloom cloth held a roughly steady share of India's cloth market from the 1890s to the 1930s, and in 1908, in one province of northern India alone, about a million people still lived from handloom weaving. In China, farm families kept weaving cloth at home, more and more of it from cheaper machine-made thread. In the Ottoman Empire, the number of handlooms in the Syrian cities of Aleppo and Damascus held steady from 1860 to 1914.

Indian shipbuilders kept building, too. At the Bombay dockyard, the shipyard of the city now called Mumbai, the Wadia family of master shipbuilders built teak warships for Britain's own Royal Navy. One of them, HMS Trincomalee, launched in 1817, is still afloat today in Hartlepool, England, where its museum calls it Britain's oldest warship still afloat.

Why the share fell

One reason the share fell is that the rest of the world made far more goods. Another is that the old makers lost buyers. Cloth from Lancashire's machines was cheaper than cloth woven by hand, even in India itself. In 1815 India sold about £1.3 million worth of cotton cloth abroad and bought only about £26,000 worth from England. By 1832 it sold less than £100,000 worth and bought more than £400,000 worth. From about 1830, India bought more cotton cloth than it sold. India, which had sold cloth to the world, now bought British cloth, and it sold raw cotton to British mills.

Power mattered too. Much of India was ruled by the British East India Company, and British cotton cloth entered India at a tax of only 2½ percent. Meanwhile Britain taxed Indian cloth heavily: around 1813, Indian calicoes paid taxes of about 70 to 85 percent of their value to be sold in Britain. Historians still argue about how much each cause mattered: cheaper machine-made goods, the trade rules of the people in power, and, in India, troubles that had begun before the factories did, such as the breakup of the Mughal Empire and years of drought.

The Middle East felt the same pressure. In 1838 English cotton cloth sold in Egypt for less than cloth from Egypt's own new mills. (Who built those mills, and why, is Topic 5.6.)

Shipbuilding followed the same path. Bombay's teak ships were among the best wooden ships of their day. But by the middle of the 1800s navies were turning to ships of iron, driven by steam, and they came mostly from British shipyards and engine works. Bombay's age of teak warships came to an end.

Common mistake to avoid

Do not give one cause as the whole answer, either way. "British machines were simply better" leaves out the trade rules; "colonial rule alone destroyed Indian industry" leaves out the cheaper machines and the weavers who kept weaving. A strong answer names more than one cause and says historians weigh them differently.

Terms to use precisely

Handloom
A loom worked by hand and foot, with no engine.
Raw cotton
Cotton as it is picked, not yet spun into thread or woven into cloth.
Dockyard
A shipyard where ships are built and repaired.
