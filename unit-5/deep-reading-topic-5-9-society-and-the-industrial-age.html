A New Ladder

Before industry, society was already a ladder of rank. Industry did not throw it away. It added new rungs, moved people up and down, split women's lives by class, and packed people into cities that could not keep up. This chapter asks of each part of society: what changed, and what kept going?

How to Use This

The College Board asks you to explain how industrialization caused change in existing social hierarchies and standards of living. That is a continuity and change question: the word "existing" means there was a ladder of rank before industry, so a strong answer says what changed and what kept going. Its skill is contextualization: placing one specific thing inside the bigger story that explains it.

01

New Classes, Old Families

1750 to 1900  ·  Britain

Industry made a middle class that rose by money and education and a working class that lived on wages. But the landed families who had been on top in 1750 were still near the top in 1900.

The old ladder

Every society has a social hierarchy, a ladder of rank. In 1750 most people in Britain lived in the countryside. At the top were landowning families, the aristocracy (lords with titles) and the gentry (landowners without them), whose wealth and rank came from land they inherited. Below them were farmers, craftsmen and laborers. Most families worked together, on the farm or at home, where women spun thread and children helped.

Two classes grow

Industry made two groups bigger and more important than ever. The middle class included factory owners, merchants and bankers, and the people who ran things for them, from engineers, doctors and lawyers to managers and clerks. Their place came from money they made and the education they paid for, not from land or titles. In 1832 the Reform Act gave the vote, in the towns, to men who occupied property worth ten pounds a year: in effect, most middle-class men. It excluded women by its own wording.

The industrial working class was made up of people who owned no land and no tools, and lived on the wages they earned in mills, mines, docks and workshops, at the hours the employer set. Their unions and parties are Topic 5.8's story.

Who stayed on top

The landed families did not disappear. They kept their land, their titles and much of their power; historians of the landed class describe it as still dominant in wealth and politics until the 1880s. From 1895 to 1902 Britain's prime minister was Lord Salisbury, a marquess and a great landowner. Rich middle-class families often bought country houses, sent their sons to the same schools as the gentry, and married into landed families. They climbed the old ladder rather than breaking it.

Common mistake to avoid

"Industry created social classes." There was a ladder of rank long before the factories, and its top rungs held. What industry did was add new rungs and change how people climbed: by money and education as well as by birth. The opposite mistake is "nothing changed": the middle class won the vote in 1832, and a class that lived only on wages had never been so large.

02

Work and Home, Split by Class

1830s to 1900  ·  Britain, the United States, Japan

Women and children had always worked. Industry moved work out of the home and split women's lives by class: working-class women and children earned wages because their families needed them, and middle-class women, whose families did not, were increasingly kept to the home.

Working-class families

In working-class families one wage was rarely enough to pay for rent and food, so women, and often children, earned too. A survey of factories in 1833 found that women and girls were a majority of the workers in Britain's textile factories. Many more women worked in domestic service, as maids and cooks in other people's houses: the census of 1851 suggests about four in ten women with a recorded job were servants, the largest single job for women. Others sewed or made goods at home, paid by the piece.

Women were usually paid less than men. Historians find that most of the gap came from the jobs women were given, which paid less, rather than from different pay for the same job, though both happened. The pattern spread with industry: in Japan in 1900, women made up about 62 percent of the workers in private factories, most of them young women in textile mills.

The middle-class home

In middle-class families the husband's earnings were enough, so the wife was increasingly expected to stay out of paid work and to run the home and raise the children. Writers called the home a woman's proper "sphere"; historians call the idea separate spheres. In 1861 Isabella Beeton's best-selling Book of Household Management opened: "As with the commander of an army, or the leader of any enterprise, so is it with the mistress of a house. Her spirit will be seen through the whole establishment; and just in proportion as she performs her duties intelligently and thoroughly, so will her domestics follow in her path."

Running such a house was real work, and the "domestics" who followed the mistress were servants, most of them working-class women. One class's home ran on the other class's wages.

How we know: an ideal, not a photograph

Advice books like Beeton's, and pictures like Currier & Ives's The Four Seasons of Life: Middle Age (about 1868), tell us what middle-class families were told a home should be. They do not prove every family lived that way. The historian Amanda Vickery has warned that many women's lives never fit the "separate spheres" picture, which is why the College Board says middle-class women were "increasingly" limited to the home: an ideal that grew over the century.

What kept going for women

In both classes, women were paid less and had fewer rights than men. In England and Wales, a married woman's property passed under her husband's control; the Married Women's Property Act of 1870 let her keep the money she earned, and the Act of 1882 let women married from 1883 hold all their property as their own. No woman could vote for Parliament.

Common mistake to avoid

"In the 1800s women stopped working." Working-class women and children earned wages, and middle-class women ran households, often with servants. The opposite mistake is "all women worked in factories": most working women were servants, and many worked at home. Name the class before you describe a woman's life.

03

Cities That Could Not Keep Up

1773 to 1851  ·  Manchester, England and Wales

Industrial towns grew faster than anyone could build homes, sewers and water pipes for the people who came, and that speed brought the six challenges the College Board names.

How fast

Urbanization is the growth of cities. Manchester, the great cotton town, had about 23,000 people in a count made in 1773, about 70,000 in the township at the first national census in 1801, and more than 300,000 in the borough by 1851. (The numbers depend on where the town's edge is drawn: the borough was larger than the old township.) In 1851 the census found, for the first time, more than half the people of England and Wales living in towns, though only just: one writer of the time put it at "ten millions against ten millions."

Six challenges

Pollution: coal smoke hung over the towns, and waste ran into the rivers. Friedrich Engels described Manchester's river Irk in 1845 as "coal-black, foul-smelling ... full of debris and refuse." Poverty: many wages barely paid the rent. Housing shortages: houses were split into rooms, and whole families lived in one room or a damp cellar. Insufficient infrastructure: the new streets had no sewers, no clean water and no rubbish collection. Public health crises: section 04. Increased crime: below.

How we know: counting crime

The number of people tried for serious crimes in England and Wales rose to peaks in 1842 and 1848, years of hard times, and then fell for the rest of the century (Gatrell and Hadden). But historians warn that these numbers count prosecutions, not crimes: as towns set up police forces, more crimes were caught and charged. So say "more people were charged with crimes," not "crime exploded."

Use this in your answer
Strongest tool, with its mechanism
Name the cause of all six. The College Board's word is "rapid": people arrived faster than homes and pipes were built.
Best evidence of exclusion or limit
"At times," says the College Board: not every town, not every year. The reforms that answered these problems are Topic 5.8's.
Comparison hook
Against Wyld's 1852 watercolor of Manchester: from a hilltop, the town is golden smoke and spires. Up close, it was the six challenges.

Terms to use precisely

Urbanization
The growth of cities, as people move from the countryside to towns.
Infrastructure
The shared systems a city needs: sewers, clean water, streets, rubbish collection.
04

What It Did to Life

1831 to 1854  ·  Manchester and London

Crowded towns with dirty water made disease spread, and in the fastest-growing cities people's lives were shorter: the standard of living, measured in health, fell for many.

Cholera

Cholera, a disease of the gut that spreads through water fouled with human waste, reached Britain in 1831. In 1848 and 1849 about 53,000 cholera deaths were registered in England and Wales. These were public health crises: disasters for a whole population, not only for the sick.

In 1854 the London physician John Snow mapped the cholera deaths around Broad Street in Soho and traced them to one public water pump. His map, printed in a report of 1855, marks each death with a small black bar; the bars stack up around the pump. A cartoon in Punch two years earlier, "A Court for King Cholera" (1852), had blamed crowding and filth. Snow showed the route was the water.

How long people lived

A family's standard of living is how well it lives, including its health and how long its members live. In the 1840s a baby born in Manchester could expect to live only about 25 to 28 years, depending on how the historians count, against about 40 to 42 for England and Wales as a whole.

Common mistake to avoid

"Workers in industrial cities died at 25." Life expectancy at birth is an average, and it is pulled down hard by the many babies and young children who died. A Manchester worker who survived childhood could expect decades more. The gap between the city and the nation is the evidence: the city was far deadlier. The opposite mistake is "cities were as healthy as the countryside."

Use this in your answer
Strongest tool, with its mechanism
Standards of living are the objective's second half. Use cholera or life expectancy as your detail, and say what caused it.
Best evidence of exclusion or limit
Snow's map shows how cholera spread; London's new sewers, the answer to it, are Topic 5.8's.
Comparison hook
Against the middle class of section 01: comfortable families moved to cleaner streets and suburbs, away from the smoke.

Terms to use precisely

Public health
The health of a whole population, and what a community does to protect it.
Standard of living
How well people live: food, home, health and how long they live.
05

Not Only Britain: New York, and 1900

1850 to 1900  ·  New York, Britain

The College Board ties these challenges to "global capitalism." Wherever industry and trade drew people in faster than homes were built, the same story played out, and by 1900 cities were only beginning to catch up.

New York, 1890

Manhattan, the heart of New York City, grew from about 515,000 people in 1850 to about 1.4 million in 1890, many of them immigrants who came for work. In 1890 the reporter Jacob Riis published How the Other Half Lives. "To-day three-fourths of its people live in the tenements," he wrote, "and the nineteenth century drift of the population to the cities is sending ever-increasing multitudes to crowd them." A tenement was a crowded apartment building for poor renters.

Riis went with the police on a visit after midnight and photographed one room on Bayard Street by flash: "In a room not thirteen feet either way slept twelve men and women, two or three in bunks set in a sort of alcove, the rest on the floor ... Most of the men were lodgers, who slept there for five cents a spot."

How we know: a reformer's camera

Riis wanted to shock comfortable readers into supporting reform, and he chose and set up his scenes for that purpose. His book also carries prejudiced lines about immigrant groups. His photographs and counts are strong evidence of crowding; his judgments of the people in them are evidence of his own point of view.

Contextualize it

To contextualize a source, place it inside the bigger story that explains it. Twelve lodgers in one Bayard Street room in 1890 happened inside the rapid growth of industrial cities around the world: people came for work faster than anyone built homes for them, so landlords packed rooms and rented floor space by the night. The same bigger story explains a Manchester cellar in the 1840s.

By 1900

Later in the century, cities began to catch up with sewers, clean water and police (Topic 5.8 has those reforms). Life expectancy in England and Wales began a lasting rise from the 1870s, and wages rose faster after about 1860 (Topic 5.7). But in 1900 many city workers were still poor, and a ladder of rank, rebuilt, still stood.

Building an Answer That Scores

Each card gathers the evidence for one part of the answer. The College Board asks how industrialization changed existing social hierarchies and standards of living, so each card gives what changed and what kept going, and the last asks you to contextualize.

Continuity and Change

Who was on top, and who did the work

Changed: a middle class rose by money and education and won the vote in the towns in 1832; a working class lived on wages, at the employer's hours. Kept going: landed families kept land, titles and power (Lord Salisbury, a marquess, prime minister 1895 to 1902); most people still worked hard for little.

Continuity and Change

Women and children

Changed: working-class women and often children earned wages to add to the family's income (women and girls a majority of textile factory workers in 1833; domestic service; Japan's mills, 1900); middle-class women increasingly kept to the home (Beeton, 1861). Kept going: women paid less, with fewer rights (property laws of 1870 and 1882).

Continuity and Change

Where and how people lived

Changed: rapid urbanization (Manchester about 23,000 in 1773, 300,000+ by 1851; England and Wales half urban in 1851; Manhattan 1.4 million by 1890) brought pollution, poverty, crime charges, cholera (about 53,000 dead, 1848 to 1849), crowded housing and too few sewers. Kept going: poverty and disease, old problems made worse; many still poor in 1900.

Contextualization

Place one thing in the bigger story

Pick Riis's Bayard Street room, a Manchester cellar, Snow's map or Beeton's book. Name it, name the bigger story it sits inside, and explain how the story explains it.
