First & 10 Reading

Freer Trade, Bigger Business

Factories made more goods than ever. Between 1750 and 1900, three things changed around them: the rules of trade, the shape of business, and the goods people could buy. This reading follows all three, and asks what stayed the same.

The Question

Who Owns It, Who Pays, and by What Rules?

Topic 5.6 ended with a question. All this industry made goods, and goods had to be paid for, traded, and financed. Who owned the factories, and how did money move around the industrial world?

This reading answers in three parts. Part one is the rules of trade: what a government let its people buy from other countries. Part two is the shape of business: who owned companies and where they got their money. Part three is the goods themselves: what people could buy, and who could afford it.

In each part, something changed between 1750 and 1900, and something else carried on. Your job is to see both.

2

Trade, Part 1

The Old Rules and a New Argument

In Topic 4.5 you met mercantilism: most European rulers believed a kingdom grew strong by selling more to other countries than it bought from them, and by keeping its colonies' trade for itself. So governments taxed goods coming in from abroad, banned some foreign goods completely, and gave a few companies the sole right to trade in a region. A tax on imported goods is a tariff. Britain's East India Company, for example, had the sole right to carry on British trade with Asia.

In 1776 a Scottish thinker, Adam Smith, published a book called The Wealth of Nations. You met his pin workshop in Topic 5.3. In the same book, Smith attacked what he called "the mercantile system." A sensible family, he wrote, never makes at home what would cost more to make than to buy:

"The tailor does not attempt to make his own shoes, but buys them of the shoemaker."

A country, Smith argued, should act the same way: "If a foreign country can supply us with a commodity cheaper than we ourselves can make it, better buy it of them ..." Tariffs and bans, he said, made ordinary buyers pay more so that a few producers could profit. Let people buy and sell freely, and the whole country would grow richer.

Later writers called ideas like Smith's laissez-faire, a French phrase meaning roughly "let them do it": let people trade without the government steering them. (Smith did not use the phrase himself.) A government that lets goods cross its borders without heavy tariffs follows a policy of free trade.

AP Thinking, Continuity and Change

The before. Your first row starts here. Around 1750: mercantilism, with tariffs, bans, and trading monopolies. Smith's book did not change the rules by itself. Watch for when the rules changed, and why.

3

Trade, Part 2

Britain Changes the Rules

Smith's ideas spread slowly. The biggest test was food. Britain's Corn Laws ("corn" meant grain) kept out cheap foreign grain to keep grain prices high for Britain's landowners, who sat in Parliament. A law of 1815 banned foreign wheat until British prices rose very high; from 1828 a tariff on imported grain took the ban's place.

In 1838 factory owners and merchants in Manchester started a campaign that became the Anti-Corn Law League, led by Richard Cobden and John Bright. Cheap bread, they argued, would help workers, and if Britain bought other countries' grain, those countries could afford to buy British cloth. Then, in 1845, a disease destroyed much of Ireland's potato crop, and famine began. Prime Minister Robert Peel decided the Corn Laws had to go. In 1846 Parliament repealed them, and the fight split Peel's own party.

Britain kept going. By 1860 it had ended its tariffs on imported factory goods, keeping a few taxes, such as those on tea, sugar, and wine, mainly to raise money. So Smith's ideas were part of the reason for the change, but only part: manufacturers who wanted cheap food and open markets, and a famine, mattered too.

Other countries followed, for a while. In 1860 Britain and France signed the Cobden-Chevalier Treaty, cutting tariffs on each other's goods, and in the 1860s a chain of similar treaties lowered tariffs across much of Western Europe. But not everyone kept going. From 1879 Germany put tariffs back on imported grain and iron to protect its farmers and factories, and the United States, which had raised its tariffs on factory goods in 1861, kept them high. Britain stayed with free trade. Western European countries had begun to abandon mercantilism, but not every country, and not for good.

Business, Part 1

New Tools for Big Business

Companies that sold shares to investors were not new. The Dutch East India Company (VOC) had sold shares in 1602 (Topic 4.5), and people bought and sold them in Amsterdam. But from 1720 to 1825, forming that kind of company in Britain usually took special permission from the government. Most businesses were owned by one family or a few partners, as Richard Arkwright's mill at Cromford was (Topic 5.3). If a partnership failed, each partner could lose his house and savings to pay its debts.

Industry needed far more money than a family had. A railway or a steamship line cost more than a few partners could pay. Three practices in banking and finance helped:

Limited liability. A law of 1855 let companies formed by ordinary investors limit what their owners could lose to the money they had put in, not everything they owned, and a law of 1856 let almost any group of seven or more people in Britain register such a company. This is limited liability. France passed similar laws in 1863 and 1867. With less to lose, many more people with modest savings could invest.

Stock markets. A stock market, or stock exchange, is a place where people buy and sell shares, so an investor can get money back by selling shares to someone else, without waiting for the company to close. London had had a stock exchange since the 1700s. In a railway boom in the mid-1840s, crowds of British investors rushed to buy railway shares. An American print from 1886, "The World's Exchanges," shows the exchanges and trading halls of eight cities, from New York and Chicago to London, Paris, Berlin, Hamburg, Frankfurt, and Brussels.

Banks that worked across borders. Banks lent money to companies, changed one country's money into another's, and paid bills between ports thousands of miles apart.

AP Thinking, Making Connections

How one thing relates to another. When you connect two developments, say how they are linked: one caused the other, made it possible, or answered it. A date that comes first is not yet a link; say what the first development changed.

Business, Part 2

Businesses That Crossed Borders

By the late 1800s, trade and production stretched around the world, and some businesses grew to match. A company that works in more than one country is a transnational business.

A bank. The bank known today as HSBC opened in Hong Kong in March 1865, as the Hongkong and Shanghai Banking Company; in 1866 it became the Hongkong and Shanghai Banking Corporation. It was started by Thomas Sutherland, a Scot who worked there for a shipping company, with a committee of merchant firms, to give local businesses better banking for their trade. It opened in Shanghai a month later and in London that summer, and by 1900 it worked in 16 countries and territories. One of the firms on its first committee, Dent and Company, had grown rich trading opium into China, a trade Britain had forced China to accept by war. That is Unit 6.

Soap and margarine. In England, William Lever began selling Sunlight Soap in 1884, made with oils from tropical plants such as coconut and palm kernels. By 1886 he had registered the Sunlight name in the United States, and from 1888 he built a factory town for his workers, Port Sunlight. To pay for growth, in 1890 the business became a limited company, Lever Brothers, Limited, and from 1894 it sold shares to the public. Across the North Sea, in the Netherlands, the Jurgens and Van den Bergh families turned butter trading into margarine factories and sold to England.

In 1929, after the years this unit covers, Lever's company and the Dutch margarine makers joined into one company, Unilever, based in both England and the Netherlands. It ran plantations and trading companies in British West Africa and the Belgian Congo. The big company the College Board names grew out of these nineteenth-century businesses.

AP Thinking, Making Connections

Read the Key Concept as a chain. The College Board says global trade "contributed to" big transnational businesses, which "relied on" new banking and finance. As you read about HSBC and Lever, look for each link of that chain.

6

Goods

Cheaper Goods, for Some

Around 1750, cotton thread was still spun by hand, one thread at a time. Once machines spun it (Topic 5.3), its price fell fast: by 1815 even the coarsest cotton yarn cost about a third of what it had in the early 1780s, counting for rising prices, and cloth kept getting cheaper after that. Factories kept improving how they made things, so goods became more available, more affordable, and more varied. Soap that grocers had once cut from long bars now came in wrapped bars with a brand name, like Sunlight, advertised to every household. Ships and railways (Topic 5.5) brought fruit, tea, and wheat from far away to city markets.

A family's standard of living is how well it lives: what it can afford to eat, wear, and buy. In Britain, one careful estimate finds that working families' standard of living rose by less than 15 percent from the 1780s to the 1850s; most historians agree it grew much faster from about 1860 to 1900. But many city workers stayed poor (Topic 5.9), and many of the people who grew the cotton, palm oil, and tea did not share in the gains (Unit 6). The College Board's words are exact: standards of living rose "for some."

Put the three rows together. What changed: Britain, and for a time much of Western Europe, moved from mercantilism toward free trade; businesses grew bigger and crossed borders, relying on limited liability, stock markets, and banks; goods became cheaper, more plentiful, and more varied. What continued: companies that sold shares were older than industry; many countries kept or went back to tariffs; and the gains went to some people and not others.

Not everyone gained from this new economy, and not everyone accepted it. Workers organized, reformers pushed for new laws, and some thinkers imagined a different system. That is Topic 5.8.

AP Thinking, Continuity and Change

Four myths to drop. "Adam Smith said governments should do nothing": he wrote that government had three duties, defense, justice, and public works that no private person would build. "Free trade won everywhere": Germany brought tariffs back, and the United States kept them high. "Companies that sell shares were invented in the 1800s": the VOC sold shares in 1602. "Industry made everyone richer": the College Board says "for some."

BeReady: 10-Second Takeaway

Between 1750 and 1900 the rules of the economy changed. Adam Smith's Wealth of Nations (1776) attacked mercantilism and argued for buying goods wherever they were cheapest. Partly persuaded by such ideas, partly pushed by manufacturers and by famine in Ireland, Britain repealed its Corn Laws in 1846 and by 1860 had ended its tariffs on imported factory goods; in the 1860s much of Western Europe lowered tariffs, though Germany went back to them from 1879 and the United States kept them high. Practices in banking and finance, limited liability (laws of 1855 and 1856), stock markets, and banks, let businesses grow large and cross borders, like HSBC (1865) and Lever's Sunlight Soap (1884), which later became part of Unilever. Factories made goods cheaper and more varied, and living standards rose, much faster after about 1860, for some, not for all. Freer trade. Bigger business. Cheaper goods, for some.
