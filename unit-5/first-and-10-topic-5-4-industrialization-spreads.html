The Question

Who Made the World's Goods in 1750?

Topic 5.3 ended with a turn. In 1771 Richard Arkwright's mill at Cromford put cotton spinning under one roof, beside one water wheel. That new way of making things did not stay in Britain. This reading follows where it went, and what happened to the places that had made most of the world's goods before.

In 1750 almost everything people bought was made by hand, at home or in small workshops. This is hand production. And most of the world's manufacturing, the making of goods such as cloth, tools, and pots, happened in Asia. The economic historian Paul Bairoch estimated that around 1750 China made about a third of all the world's manufactured goods, and India about a quarter. All of Europe together made about a quarter. Britain made under 2 percent.

India's cotton cloth was sold across Asia, Africa, the Middle East, and Europe, from fine, thin muslins to cheap printed calicoes, cotton cloths painted or printed with colored patterns. Indian shipbuilders built ocean-going ships out of teak, a hard wood that lasts for decades at sea.

Bairoch's numbers are estimates. Nobody in 1750 counted every piece of cloth in the world, and historians who use his figures say so. What the estimates show is the size of the change, not exact amounts.

AP Thinking, Continuity and Change

Two lists. A continuity-and-change question asks what changed and what stayed the same over a stretch of time. This topic has two changes, where goods were made and how, and one big continuity. Keep a list of each as you read.

2

How It Spread, Part 1

Britain Tried to Keep the Secret

Britain's leaders knew the new machines were valuable. British law banned skilled workers, people trained to build or run the machines, from leaving to work abroad, and it banned sending textile machines out of the country. A port could search a crate, but not a memory.

Samuel Slater learned the cotton trade as an apprentice at Jedediah Strutt's mill in Derbyshire. Strutt had been Arkwright's partner, and his mills ran Arkwright's machines, so Slater knew them from the inside. In 1789 he sailed for the United States without telling anyone what he knew. In Pawtucket, Rhode Island, working with American merchants, he built water-powered spinning machines from memory. The mill that still stands there was begun in 1793 and enlarged several times afterward.

In Belgium, John Cockerill, the son of an English machine maker, began building machines at Seraing, near Liège, in 1817, and added ironworks there in the 1820s. Liège sat on one of the richest coalfields on the continent of Europe, and it had rivers to carry goods. The pattern from Topic 5.3, coal near water, was waiting there.

AP Thinking, Making Connections

Look for the pattern. A pattern is something that happens again and again across different cases. Slater and Cockerill were different men in different countries. As you read on, ask what they had in common, and what the later cases share with them. You will name that pattern in Question 01.

3

How It Spread, Part 2

Then Came the Machines and the Money

Britain dropped the ban on skilled workers leaving in 1824 and lifted the ban on sending textile machines abroad in 1843. Machines and money carried the methods too. The first steam railway in the German states, from Nuremberg to Fürth in 1835, ran with a locomotive built in England, a machine the textile ban did not cover.

On the continent, factories grew where coal lay, as they had in Britain: around Liège in Belgium, in the Ruhr valley of the German states, and in northern France. In the United States, wealthy Boston merchants paid for big cotton mills at Lowell, Massachusetts, from the 1820s. Most of the workers were young women from New England farms.

In Russia and Japan, the big growth came later, mostly after 1870. A Welsh engineer named John Hughes built ironworks in the coal country of southern Russia in the early 1870s; the town that grew up around them was named Yuzovka, after him. In Japan, the Osaka Spinning Company opened in 1883 with cotton-spinning machines made in Britain, driven by steam power, and it ran day and night. The governments of Russia and Japan also pushed industry on purpose; that is Topic 5.6.

So, by 1900 the new methods had spread from northwestern Europe to other parts of Europe, to the United States, to Russia, and to Japan.

AP Thinking, Making Connections

What did a factory need to start somewhere new? Think back to Topic 5.3: Britain's factories needed coal, iron, water, and money. As you read each case here, ask two questions: what carried the new methods to this place, and what was waiting for them when they arrived?

What Changed

The World's Shares Move

A steam-powered factory could make cloth far faster than hands could, and far more cheaply. As factories spread, the places that had them made more and more of the world's goods.

A share is a part of a whole. Bairoch's estimates show the shares moving. Britain's share of the world's manufacturing went from under 2 percent in 1750 to about a fifth by 1860. By 1900 Europe and the United States together made more than four-fifths of the world's manufactured goods, and the United States had passed Britain as the largest maker of all.

Meanwhile, China's share fell from about a third in 1750 to about 6 percent in 1900. India's fell from about a quarter to under 2 percent.

AP Thinking, Continuity and Change

A share is not the same as an amount. Imagine a pizza that gets much bigger. Your slice can stay the same size while your share of the pizza shrinks. The world was making far more goods in 1900 than in 1750. So a falling share does not, by itself, mean a region made less. For India, though, Bairoch's estimates suggest it also made less per person over time: it lost ground, not only share.

5

What Continued

The Old Makers Kept Making

The falling shares do not mean that Asia and the Middle East stopped working. They kept making goods, much of it by hand. India even built its own steam-powered cotton mills: the first in Bombay was founded in 1854 and began spinning in 1856.

In India, hand weaving shrank, but it did not die. Weavers kept working cloth on handlooms, looms worked by hand and foot, and many switched to cheaper machine-spun thread so they could keep weaving. In 1908, in just one province of northern India, about a million people still lived from handloom weaving. In China, farm families kept weaving cloth at home, more and more of it from cheaper machine-made thread. In the Ottoman Empire, the number of handlooms in the Syrian cities of Aleppo and Damascus held steady from 1860 to 1914.

Indian shipbuilders kept building, too. At the Bombay dockyard, the shipyard of the city now called Mumbai, the Wadia family of master shipbuilders built teak warships for Britain's own Royal Navy. One of them, HMS Trincomalee, launched in 1817, is still afloat today in Hartlepool, England, where its museum calls it Britain's oldest warship still afloat.

The old makers kept making, but their share of the world's goods shrank. One reason is that the rest of the world's production grew much faster. Another is that the old makers lost buyers, even at home, as the next section shows.

AP Thinking, Continuity and Change

Name the continuity. The College Board says Middle Eastern and Asian countries "continued to produce manufactured goods" while their share "declined." A strong answer says both halves: what continued, and what changed.

6

Why It Changed

Why the Old Makers' Share Fell

Cloth from the machines of Lancashire, the cotton-mill region of northwest England, was cheaper than cloth woven by hand, even in India itself. In 1815 India sold about £1.3 million worth of cotton cloth abroad and bought only about £26,000 worth from England. By 1832 it sold less than £100,000 worth and bought more than £400,000 worth. From about 1830, India bought more cotton cloth than it sold. India, which had sold cloth to the world, now bought British cloth, and it sold raw cotton, cotton not yet spun or woven, to British mills.

Power mattered too. Much of India was ruled by the British East India Company, and British cotton cloth entered India at a tax of only 2½ percent. Earlier, Britain had taxed Indian cloth heavily: around 1813, Indian calicoes paid taxes of about 70 to 85 percent of their value to be sold in Britain. Historians still argue about how much each cause mattered: cheaper machine-made goods, the trade rules of the people in power, and, in India, troubles that had begun before the factories did, such as the breakup of the Mughal Empire and years of drought.

Shipbuilding followed the same path. Bombay's teak ships were among the best wooden ships of their day. But by the middle of the 1800s navies were turning to ships of iron, driven by steam, and they came mostly from British shipyards and engine works. Bombay's age of teak warships came to an end.

So, how did the ways and places goods were made change between 1750 and 1900? The way goods were made changed from hand work to steam-powered factories, and the places changed with it: factory methods spread from Britain to other parts of Europe, the United States, Russia, and Japan, carried by people, machines, and money, and the share of the world's goods made in Europe and the United States rose. What continued was production in India, China, and the Middle East, much of it by hand, which kept making goods, but a shrinking share of them.

Factories needed coal, and steam engines turned that coal into power on a scale nobody had seen before. How did the machines themselves change the world? That is Topic 5.5.

AP Thinking, Continuity and Change

Three myths to drop. "Asia stopped making things": it kept making them, but its share fell. "Industry spread everywhere": it spread to a few regions, mostly ones with coal or rivers strong enough to turn water wheels. "India fell behind only because British machines were better": the machines mattered, but so did the trade rules made by the people in power, and historians still argue how much.

BeReady: 10-Second Takeaway

In 1750 most of the world's goods were made by hand, and most of them in Asia: Paul Bairoch estimated that China made about a third of the world's manufactured goods and India about a quarter, while Britain made under 2 percent. Then factory methods spread from Britain, first carried by skilled people such as Samuel Slater, who built spinning machines from memory in Rhode Island, and John Cockerill, who opened works at Seraing in Belgium in 1817, and later by machines and money. By 1900 they had reached other parts of Europe, the United States, Russia, and Japan, and Europe and the United States made more than four-fifths of the world's manufactured goods. India's and China's shares fell to a few percent. But the old makers kept making: Indian hand weaving shrank but survived, with about a million people living from it in one province alone in 1908, and Bombay's shipbuilders built teak warships for the Royal Navy. Cheaper machine-made goods and the trade rules of the people in power, among other causes, shrank their share. The factory traveled. The share moved. The old makers kept making.
