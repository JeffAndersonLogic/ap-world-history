The Question

A Revolution in How Things Were Made

Topic 5.2 ended with a turn. The revolutions in America, France, Haiti, and Spanish America changed who had the right to rule. But even before Paris argued about rights, a different revolution had begun in British workshops. It changed how things were made.

In 1750 almost everything people owned was made by hand. Most thread and cloth were made in people's homes. Under the putting-out system, a merchant brought raw wool or cotton to families in the countryside. They spun it into thread and wove it into cloth on their own spinning wheels and looms, and the merchant came back to collect the work and pay for each piece. Power came from human muscles, horses, wind, and water wheels on streams. Much of the world's cotton cloth came from India.

Over the next hundred years, Britain built a new way of making things. Why did it begin there? The answer has two parts: what nature gave Britain, and what people built on top of it. Then there is the new workplace those causes produced: the factory.

AP Thinking, Causation

Two kinds of causes. The College Board lists seven factors behind the Industrial Revolution. Two come from nature: waterways, and where coal, iron, and timber were found. Five were built by people: towns, better farming, laws that protected property, resources from abroad, and saved-up money. Canals sit in between: nature's water, dug into new places by people. The College Board lists all seven under its question about environmental factors; sorting them into two groups is this course's way to think about them. As you read, sort each factor.

2

Nature, Part 1

Coal, Iron, and Not Enough Wood

Britain sat on huge beds of coal. Much of it lay close to the surface, and much of it lay close to rivers or the sea. Iron ore was often found nearby.

What Britain did not have was much timber. By the 1600s most of its old forests had been cut down, so wood was expensive. Iron had always been made by heating iron ore with charcoal, a fuel made from wood, and much of the iron Britain used was bought from Sweden, and later from Russia, where forests were still large. So Britons turned to coal. By 1700 coal gave England about half of all its energy, much of it burned in fireplaces for heating and cooking.

In 1709, at Coalbrookdale beside the River Severn, Abraham Darby made iron using coke, a fuel made by baking coal. Iron could now be made with British coal instead of scarce wood. At first it was used mostly for cast-iron pots, and coke iron spread slowly, mainly after the 1750s, as charcoal and imported iron grew dearer. A shortage of wood helped push Britain toward coal, which it had in plenty.

AP Thinking, Causation

A shortage can be a cause. Timber is on the College Board's list, and Britain's timber mattered because there was so little of it. When a resource runs short, people look for a substitute. Britain's substitute, coal, was lying under its fields.

3

Nature, Part 2

Water: Moving Heavy Things and Turning Wheels

Coal is heavy. Before railways, the cheapest way to move something heavy was to float it. Britain had a long coastline and rivers that boats could travel. Coal from the mines near Newcastle went by ship down the coast to London.

Fast streams mattered too. A stream could turn a water wheel, and a water wheel could turn machines.

Then people improved on nature with canals, waterways dug by hand. The Duke of Bridgewater owned coal mines at Worsley, a few miles from Manchester. In 1761 his canal opened from Worsley to Stretford, on the edge of Manchester, and the price of coal in Manchester soon fell by about half. By 1765 the canal reached the town itself. Other canal builders followed, and within a few decades canals linked coalfields, industrial towns, and ports.

AP Thinking, Causation

Say what the factor did. "Britain had rivers" is a fact, not an explanation. An explanation says what the factor did and what that led to. Always add the "so."

4

People, Part 1

More Food, More People, Bigger Towns

Nature is not the whole story. China also had coal, and it did not have Britain's industrial revolution. The rest of the story is what people built.

First, farms. British farmers were growing more food on the same land. Instead of leaving a field empty every few years to rest, they planted turnips and clover. Turnips fed cattle and sheep through the winter, and clover made the soil richer. More animals meant more manure for the fields, and that meant more grain. Landowners also fenced off the old shared village fields into private farms, often with permission from Parliament. This enclosure let them farm their land the new way.

Better farming meant more food from each field and each farmworker. More food fed more people. Britain's population grew from about 8 million in 1761 to about 10.5 million in 1801, and a smaller share of them farmed. By 1801 only about a third of English working men worked on the land.

Many of the rest lived in towns. Urbanization, the growth of towns and cities, put many workers close together and gave them neighbors to sell to. Manchester, a town of about 23,000 people in 1773, counted 70,409 in the census of 1801.

AP Thinking, Causation

An effect can become a cause. Better farming helped cause more people, and more people, crowded into towns, became a cause of industry. The College Board lists urbanization as a cause of the Industrial Revolution. You will meet it again in Topic 5.9, as an effect.

5

People, Part 2

Laws, Cotton from Abroad, and Money

Building a mill was a big risk. Three more things made the risk worth taking.

Laws protected private property. In Britain, Parliament, where landowners and merchants sat, passed the laws that enclosed fields and allowed canals to be dug. Since 1624, English law had also let an inventor take out a patent: the sole right to use a new invention for up to fourteen years. In 1769 Richard Arkwright patented a new spinning machine. A person who invested could expect the law to protect what he built and earned.

Britain reached resources from abroad. Cotton does not grow in Britain's climate. By the 1780s, most of the cotton spun in Manchester was grown by enslaved people on plantations in the Caribbean and South America. After the 1790s, more and more of it came from plantations in the United States. The factory's raw material came from far away, and most of it was grown by people who were not free.

Money had been saved up to invest. The College Board calls this the accumulation of capital: money used to build something that makes more money. Merchants, bankers, and landowners had grown rich from trade, including the Atlantic trade in enslaved people and the sugar they grew, and some were ready to risk their savings on mills and machines. Arkwright's partners, Samuel Need and Jedediah Strutt, were stocking makers who had grown rich in the hosiery trade. The partners spent thousands of pounds on machines and mills before the business made a profit, at a time when a carpenter earned no more than about £36 a year.

AP Thinking, Causation

Say only what you can support. Historians still argue about how much of Britain's capital came from the slave trade and slavery, so a strong answer says "some" or "part," not "all." What historians agree on is where most of the cotton came from: plantations worked by enslaved people.

6

Working Together

How the Factors Combined

None of these factors made an industrial revolution by itself. Watch five of them meet in one canal.

The Bridgewater Canal needed coal under the ground at Worsley (nature). It needed water to float the coal on (nature, improved by people). It needed acts of Parliament, passed in 1759 and 1760, to allow it to be built (law). It needed the Duke's money, and he borrowed more to pay his workers (capital). And it needed a growing town to buy the coal (urbanization). Cheap coal then made Manchester an even better place to build mills, and the new mills made the town grow faster still.

That is how the factors worked. Each one made the others pay off.

AP Thinking, Developments and Processes

Explain a process step by step. To explain how something developed, name where it starts, then each step, joined by words like "which meant" and "so." For example: a new bus line opened near the school, which meant students who lived farther away could get there easily, so more students signed up for the after-school club.

7

The Factory

One Place, One Job Each

In 1771, Arkwright and his partners built a cotton-spinning mill at Cromford, in Derbyshire. A water wheel turned the machines. It was fed by a brook and by a tunnel that drained the nearby lead mines. Because that water came from underground, it was said never to freeze.

The mill did not send cotton out to spinners' homes. It brought the workers to the machines. Everyone came to one building, at the hours the mill set, under the eye of an overseer. The village was too small to supply enough workers, so Arkwright built houses for them. Most of his workers were women and children, some as young as seven. This was the factory system: production gathered in a single place, around one source of power. UNESCO, which protects the valley as a World Heritage Site, says it "saw the birth of the factory system."

Inside a cotton mill, the work was split into steps. Raw cotton was cleaned, then carded (combed so its fibers lay straight), drawn out, twisted into a loose rope, and spun into thread. Cromford began with machines for spinning, and in 1775 Arkwright patented machines for the earlier steps too. From then on each step after cleaning had its own machine, tended by workers who did that job and no other. This is specialization of labor.

Adam Smith, a Scottish thinker, showed why it mattered in 1776, using a pin workshop. Ten men shared about eighteen steps, each doing only one, two, or three of them, and together they "could make among them upwards of forty-eight thousand pins in a day." A worker who had not learned the trade, working alone, he wrote, "could scarce ... make one pin in a day, and certainly could not make twenty." Because each job was small and simple, it could be learned quickly, so a mill could hire workers with no training, many of them children.

So, how did environmental factors contribute to industrialization? Britain's coal, iron, and waterways made fuel, metal, power, and transport cheap in the same places, and its shortage of wood pushed it toward coal. Better farming, growing towns, laws that protected property, cotton from abroad, and saved-up money made it worth building something new on top of them. Together they made factories pay. And the factory changed work itself: it gathered production in one place and split it into small, specialized jobs.

The new way of making things did not stay in Britain. That is Topic 5.4.

AP Thinking, Causation

Three myths to drop. "Britain had coal, so it industrialized": coal was one cause of seven, and China had coal too. "Cromford was the first factory": earlier mills had some of its features. "Steam started it": Cromford ran on water. Steam power is Topic 5.5.

BeReady: 10-Second Takeaway

Britain's industrial revolution began with what nature gave it: coal and iron close together, close to rivers and the sea, and a shortage of wood that pushed Britons to burn coal and, from 1709, to make iron with coke. Water made heavy coal cheap to move, and canals such as the Bridgewater Canal, opened in 1761, cut the price of coal in Manchester by about half. But nature alone did not do it. People built the rest: better farming that fed a growing population, towns such as Manchester that gathered workers and buyers, laws that protected property and inventions, cotton from abroad grown by enslaved people, and saved-up capital ready to risk on mills. Together these made factories pay. The factory, like Arkwright's water-powered mill at Cromford in 1771, gathered production in one place and split the work into small, specialized jobs. Nature gave the fuel and the water. People built the rest. The factory put the work under one roof.
