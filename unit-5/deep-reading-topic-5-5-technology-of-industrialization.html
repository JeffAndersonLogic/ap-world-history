More Power, Longer Reach

In 1750 almost all the energy people used came from muscles, wood, wind and water. By 1900 about half of it came from coal. This chapter follows how machines unlocked that buried energy, what new things it let people make, and how railroads, steamships and the telegraph carried goods, people and news into the interiors of continents.

How to Use This

The College Board asks you to explain how technology shaped economic production between 1750 and 1900. That is a causation question, and the answer is a chain: each technology made the next one possible. Section 01 sets the starting point; sections 02 and 03 are the power; section 04 is the new materials; section 05 is the reach, and its two effects, trade and migration. Each section ends with a tool, a limit and a comparison you can use in an answer.

  2. 02 The steam engine: from a mine pump to the power of factories.
  3. 03 More coal, then oil: how much more energy, and a second engine.
  4. 04 The second industrial revolution: steel, chemicals, electricity and precision machinery.
  5. 05 Longer reach: railroads, steamships and the telegraph, and more trade and migration.

01

Energy Before 1750

About 1750  ·  The starting point

In 1750 almost all the energy people used came from things that grow, and from wind and water. Each had a limit.

Things that grow, blow and flow

In 1750 a farmer plowed with a horse or an ox, a family cooked and kept warm with wood, a ship crossed the ocean by sail, and a mill turned beside a river. Food gave energy to the muscles of people and animals. Wood gave fire. Wind and water turned some machines. All of these came, in the end, from the sun and the land: crops and forests had to grow, every year, to be used.

Each had a limit you could see. A horse gets tired and must be fed from land that could grow food for people. A forest takes decades to grow back. A sailing ship waits for the wind. A water wheel works only beside a fast river, and turns slowly when the river runs low. Topic 5.3's first factory, Arkwright's mill at Cromford, ran on a water wheel.

How we know: the world's energy, measured

Historians of energy, such as Vaclav Smil, have estimated how much energy the world used and where it came from. Their figures show that in 1850 wood, crop waste and other things that grow gave more than 90 percent of the world's energy, and that they were still the largest source in 1900, when coal gave nearly half (Our World in Data).

Energy buried underground

There was another store of energy, underground. Coal and oil are each a fossil fuel: the remains of plants and tiny sea creatures, buried for millions of years, with energy stored inside them. Coal did not need land to grow this year. Britain already burned a great deal of it for heat (Topic 5.3). The trick was to turn its heat into motion.

Best evidence of exclusion or limit
Water wheels and windmills were good machines; the limit was the energy itself, not the skill of the people who used them.
Comparison hook
Against 1900 (section 03): in 1750 energy came mostly from things that grow; by about 1900 coal gave about half of the world's energy.

Terms to use precisely

Fossil fuel
A fuel such as coal or oil, formed from plants and sea creatures buried for millions of years.
Water wheel
A wheel turned by flowing water, used to drive machines in a mill.
Biomass
Fuel from things that grow, such as wood, straw and dung.
02

The Steam Engine

1712 to the 1780s  ·  From a pump to a power

The steam engine began as a pump for flooded coal mines. Watt made it use far less coal and turn wheels, so factories could run on coal instead of rivers.

A pump for mines

As British miners dug deeper for coal, water flooded the mines. In 1712 Thomas Newcomen set up a steam engine near Dudley Castle, in the English Midlands, to pump it out. A fire boiled water into steam. The steam filled a cylinder under a piston; a spray of cold water turned the steam back into water, the air pressed the piston down, and a great wooden beam rocked to work the pump. It worked, but it burned a great deal of coal, because the cylinder was cooled and heated again on every stroke.

Watt's improvement

James Watt, an instrument maker at the University of Glasgow, was repairing a model Newcomen engine when he saw the waste. In 1765 he found the fix: cool the steam in a separate chamber, the separate condenser, so the cylinder could stay hot. He patented the idea in 1769. Watt's engines used less than a third of the coal of a Newcomen engine.

Then came the change that mattered most for factories. Watt's business partner, Matthew Boulton, wanted engines that could drive mill machinery. In the 1780s Boulton and Watt built engines that turned a wheel. A wheel can drive spinning machines, looms and hammers. A mill no longer had to sit beside a fast river: it could run on coal, wherever coal could be brought, and cotton mills crowded into towns such as Manchester.

Common mistake to avoid

Watt did not invent the steam engine. Newcomen's engines had pumped mines for half a century before Watt's patent. Watt made the steam engine use far less coal, and made it turn wheels. And steam did not start the Industrial Revolution: Cromford ran on water, and many mills used water power for decades after Watt.

Use this in your answer
Strongest tool, with its mechanism
Explain a process step by step. The problem, what the machine did, what it made possible, what followed.
Best evidence of exclusion or limit
A patent is not the same as a change in production: Watt's engines spread over decades, and water wheels stayed common.
Comparison hook
Against section 01: the water wheel tied a mill to a river; the rotary steam engine tied it to coal, which could be carried.

Terms to use precisely

Steam engine
A machine that uses steam from boiling water, heated by burning fuel, to make motion.
Separate condenser
Watt's chamber for cooling steam outside the cylinder, so the cylinder stays hot and less coal is burned.
Rotary motion
Turning in a circle, like a wheel, so an engine can drive machines.
03

More Coal, Then Oil

1854 to 1900  ·  The fossil fuels revolution

Machines let people use far more energy than ever before: coal gave about half the world's energy by 1900. Oil and the internal combustion engine came next, but their big effects came later.

How much more energy

Steam engines needed coal, and coal mines needed steam engines to pump them dry, so each made the other grow. Britain mined about 65 million tons of coal in 1854 and about 225 million tons in 1900. By about 1900, coal supplied about half of all the energy used in the whole world; things that grow, such as wood, gave about the other half. The College Board calls this the fossil fuels revolution: it "greatly increased the energy available to human societies."

A person who lived through it said so. In 1865 the British economist William Stanley Jevons wrote that "with coal almost any feat is possible or easy; without it we are thrown back into the laborious poverty of early times." He was worried that Britain's cheap coal would not last forever. (His words are this lesson's Primary Source.)

Oil, and an engine that burns fuel inside itself

In 1859 Edwin Drake drilled a well at Titusville, Pennsylvania, and struck oil. For the rest of the 1800s, most oil was refined into kerosene, a fuel for lamps.

A steam engine burns its fuel under a boiler, outside the engine. An internal combustion engine burns its fuel inside the engine itself, in the cylinder. In 1876 Nikolaus Otto built a four-stroke gas engine in Germany, and in 1886 Carl Benz patented a car driven by a small engine that burned a fuel made from oil.

How we know: counting cars

Government tables of the number of cars in the United States begin with the year 1900, when there were about 8,000 in the whole country. (No state yet required cars to be registered: New York was the first, in 1901.) The internal combustion engine's big changes, to cars, trucks, tractors and airplanes, came after 1900.

Use this in your answer
Strongest tool, with its mechanism
Say how much. Use one number to show that the energy available grew greatly.
Best evidence of exclusion or limit
Coal caught up with wood and other things that grow only around 1900, and passed them after; say "about half" by 1900, not that coal had replaced wood.
Comparison hook
Against the steam engine (section 02): the internal combustion engine was developed before 1900, but most of its effects came after.

Terms to use precisely

Fossil fuels revolution
The great increase in energy that came when machines let people use coal and oil.
Kerosene
A fuel made from oil, burned in lamps.
Internal combustion engine
An engine that burns its fuel inside the cylinder, like a car engine.
04

The Second Industrial Revolution

1850 to 1900  ·  Four new methods

After 1850, with so much energy to use, new methods made steel cheap, made chemicals in factories, sold electric power, and cut machine parts precisely enough to swap.

Steel

Steel is iron with a small, carefully measured amount of carbon. It is stronger and tougher than ordinary iron, but before the 1850s it was slow and costly to make in large amounts. In 1856 Henry Bessemer showed that blowing air through melted iron burned out the carbon and other impurities, and the burning kept the metal hot. The United States made about 69,000 tons of steel in 1870 and about 10 million tons in 1900; Britain went from about 215,000 tons to about 4.9 million. Steel became rails, bridges, ships and machines.

Chemicals

In 1856 an eighteen-year-old London chemistry student, William Perkin, made a purple dye, mauve, from chemicals that came from coal tar, a sticky leftover of coal. It was the first synthetic dye to sell well. German chemical companies such as BASF, founded in 1865, built laboratories whose job was to invent new dyes, and in 1897 BASF began selling indigo blue made in a factory instead of from a plant. By 1914 Germany made most of the world's dyes.

Electricity

In 1882 Thomas Edison's Pearl Street Station in New York began selling electric power, lighting about 400 lamps on its first day. Electric motors could let each machine run on its own motor, instead of on belts from one big steam engine. But the change was slow: in 1899, electric motors gave less than 5 percent of the power in American factories, and it took about two more decades for them to give half. Factories had to be rebuilt to use them well.

Precision machinery

Machine tools, such as lathes and milling machines, could now cut metal parts to exact sizes, so that one part fit any copy of the same machine. These are interchangeable parts. Standard screw threads, set by Joseph Whitworth in Britain in 1841 and by William Sellers in the United States in 1864, helped machines with interchangeable parts, "from typewriters to locomotives," spread.

Common mistake to avoid

Do not say "by 1900 factories ran on electricity." Electric power was new and growing, but in 1899 it gave under 5 percent of American factory power. Steam, burning coal, still drove most machines.

Use this in your answer
Strongest tool, with its mechanism
Name the method and what it made. Steel made rails and ships; dyes came from coal tar; motors could drive single machines; precise tools made parts that fit.
Best evidence of exclusion or limit
A new method is not the same as a change everywhere: electricity, especially, spread slowly.
Comparison hook
Against section 03: the second industrial revolution ran on the energy of the first; coal ran the furnaces that made steel and gave the tar that made dyes.

Terms to use precisely

Second industrial revolution
The new methods in steel, chemicals, electricity and precision machinery after about 1850.
Steel
Iron with a small, measured amount of carbon, stronger and tougher than ordinary iron.
Interchangeable parts
Machine parts made so exactly that any one fits any copy of the same machine.
05

Longer Reach

1830 to 1904  ·  Railroads, steamships and the telegraph

Railroads, steamships and the telegraph reached the interiors of continents and joined distant markets, which led to more trade and more migration.

Railroads

Before railroads, heavy goods moved cheaply only by water, so the middle of a continent, far from rivers and the sea, was hard to reach. The Liverpool and Manchester Railway, opened in England in 1830, was the world's first railway between two cities, with trains pulled by steam locomotives. Railways spread fast: Russia had 27 kilometers of railway in 1840 and more than 53,000 in 1900; Germany went from 469 kilometers in 1840 to more than 51,000 in 1900.

India's first passenger train ran from Bombay to Thane in 1853. The first railroad across the United States was finished at Promontory Summit, Utah, in 1869. Russia began the Trans-Siberian Railway in 1891, and by 1904 trains could cross to the Pacific Ocean. A railroad could reach farms, mines and towns where no river or canal went.

Steamships

Under sail, emigrants from Norway took about 53 days on average to reach America. By steamship, the Atlantic crossing from England took about 12 days in the 1860s, and the fastest ships of the 1870s crossed in about 7 or 8 days. The Suez Canal, opened in Egypt in 1869, cut the voyage from Europe to Asia; it was of little use to sailing ships, because the winds of the Red Sea were hard to sail against, so it mostly served steamships. Steamboats also pushed up rivers into the interior of continents: in 1854 the steamer Pleiad went far up the Niger and Benue rivers in West Africa, and none of its crew died, because they took quinine, a medicine against malaria. What European empires did with this reach is Unit 6.

The telegraph

A telegraph sends messages as pulses of electricity along a wire. In 1844 Samuel Morse sent "What hath God wrought?" from Washington to Baltimore. A cable under the Atlantic worked only a few weeks in 1858; another worked lastingly from 1866, and one reached Bombay in 1870. A message that once traveled as fast as a ship could now travel in minutes, so merchants could know prices in distant markets the same day.

More trade, more migration

Cheaper, faster transport and fast news joined markets. In 1870 wheat cost 57.6 percent more in Liverpool, England, than in Chicago. By 1913 the gap was only 15.6 percent. Wheat grown in the middle of North America, carried by train to the coast and by steamship across the ocean, fed workers in Britain.

Trains and ships carried people, too. Migration means people moving to live in a new place. About 55 million Europeans moved to the Americas and Australia between 1850 and 1914. Between 1846 and 1940, more than 29 million Indians and more than 19 million Chinese moved to Southeast Asia and the lands around the Indian Ocean, and tens of millions of Chinese and Russians moved into Manchuria, Siberia and Central Asia. Steamships and railways made these journeys faster and cheaper. Why people moved, and what they found, is Unit 6.

How we know: prices and passengers

Economic historians compare the price of the same good in two markets: when the gap shrinks, the markets have joined. The wheat figures are from Kevin O'Rourke and Jeffrey Williamson. The migration counts come from passenger lists and port records, gathered by Timothy Hatton and Jeffrey Williamson for Europe and by Adam McKeown for Asia.

Strongest tool, with its mechanism
Trace the cause to the effect. Say what the technology made possible, then name the trade or the migration it led to, with a number.
Best evidence of exclusion or limit
Technology made journeys cheaper and faster; it does not explain on its own why people chose to move, which is Unit 6's question.
Comparison hook
Against section 01: in 1750 heavy goods moved cheaply only by water, and news only as fast as a horse or a ship.

Terms to use precisely

Interior
The middle of a continent, far from the sea.
Telegraph
A way of sending messages as pulses of electricity along a wire.
Migration
People moving to live in a new place.
