A New Ladder

1

Before

A Ladder of Land and Birth

Topic 5.8 ended with a question: industry had changed who worked, where people lived and how families got by, so what kind of society did it make? The answer is not "a brand-new one." In this reading, keep asking two questions: what changed, and what kept going?

Every society has a social hierarchy, a ladder of rank that says who is on top and who is below. In 1750, most people in Britain, as almost everywhere, lived in the countryside. At the top of the ladder were landowning families, the aristocracy and the gentry, whose wealth and rank came from land they inherited. Below them were farmers, craftsmen and laborers. Most families worked together, on the farm or at home: women spun thread, and children helped as soon as they could. In Topic 5.3 you saw that work begin to move into the factory.

Industry did not throw this ladder away. It rebuilt it: it added new rungs, moved people up and down, and changed what life was like on each rung.

AP Thinking, Continuity and Change

Start with what was already true. You cannot say what changed until you know what came before. The College Board's question is how industry changed existing social hierarchies: the ladder was there first.

2

Who Was on Top

New Classes, Old Families

Industry made two groups bigger and more important than ever before. The first was the middle class: factory owners, merchants and bankers, and the people who ran things for them, from engineers, doctors and lawyers to managers and clerks. Their place came from money they made and the education they paid for, not from land or titles. They gained power, too. In Britain the Reform Act of 1832 gave the vote to most middle-class men in the towns.

The second was the industrial working class: people who owned no land and no tools, and lived on the wages they earned in mills, mines, docks and workshops, at the hours the employer set. These were the workers who organized in Topic 5.8.

Now the continuity. The old landed families did not disappear. They kept their land, their titles and much of their power. In 1900 Britain's prime minister, Lord Salisbury, was a marquess, the head of a noble family with large estates of land. Some rich middle-class families bought country estates and married into landed families, though historians debate how many. The ladder had new rungs, but it was still a ladder.

3

Women and Children, Part 1

Working-Class Families Earn

Women and children had always worked. What changed was where they worked, and how the answer split by class.

In working-class families, one wage was rarely enough to pay for rent and food. So women, and often children, took wage-earning jobs to add to the family's income. In a survey of British textile factories in 1833, women and girls were a majority of the workers in most kinds of mill. Many more worked in domestic service, as maids and cooks in other people's houses, the largest single job for women in Britain. Others sewed clothes or made goods at home, paid by the piece. Women were usually paid less than men, often because they were given different, lower-paid jobs; historians still debate how much was also unequal pay for the same work.

The same pattern spread with industry. In Japan, which industrialized after 1868 (Topic 5.6), women made up about 62 percent of the workers in private factories in 1900, most of them young women in textile mills.

AP Thinking, Continuity and Change

Change and continuity in one sentence. Working-class women kept working, as they always had (continuity), but now for wages, outside the home, at an employer's hours (change).

4

Women and Children, Part 2

The Middle-Class Home

In middle-class families, the husband's earnings were enough. So the wife was increasingly expected to stay out of paid work and to run the home and raise the children. Writers called the home a woman's proper "sphere"; historians call the idea separate spheres: men in the world of work and politics, women in the home.

In 1861 Isabella Beeton published a best-selling guide for middle-class wives, The Book of Household Management. Its first chapter opens:

"As with the commander of an army, or the leader of any enterprise, so is it with the mistress of a house."

Running a middle-class house was real work, and the "domestics" the mistress gave orders to were servants, most of them working-class women. One class's home ran on the other class's wages.

What kept going? In both classes, women were paid less and had fewer rights than men. In England and Wales, until laws of 1870 and 1882, a married woman's property passed under her husband's control, and until 1870 so did the money she earned. The College Board's word is "increasingly": the middle-class home was an ideal that grew over the century, and many women's lives never fit it.

AP Thinking, Continuity and Change

Look for the reason. A change that splits by class needs a cause. Read Sections 3 and 4 again: what did one kind of family need that the other did not? Put that reason in your women-and-children row.

5

Where People Lived, Part 1

Cities That Could Not Keep Up

The fastest change of all was where people lived. Urbanization is the growth of cities, as people move from the countryside to towns. Manchester, the great cotton town, had about 23,000 people in a count of 1773, about 70,000 at the first national census in 1801, and more than 300,000 by 1851, counting the larger borough. That year's census found, for the first time, more than half the people of England and Wales living in towns.

No city was ready for growth that fast. The College Board names six challenges, and the new industrial towns had all of them. Pollution: coal smoke hung over the towns, and waste ran into the rivers. Poverty: many wages barely paid the rent. Housing shortages: whole families lived in one room or a damp cellar. Insufficient infrastructure: the new streets had no sewers, no clean water and no rubbish collection. Public health crises: diseases spread through dirty water and crowded rooms. Cholera reached Britain in 1831, and in 1848 and 1849 it killed about 53,000 people in England and Wales. Increased crime: more people were charged with crimes, a number that rose until the early 1840s, though historians warn that it also rose because towns were setting up police forces to catch them.

AP Thinking, Continuity and Change

Old problems, new speed. Poverty and disease were not new. What was new was the speed and size of the cities, which made old problems much worse. That is the College Board's word: "rapid" urbanization.

6

Where People Lived, Part 2

What It Did to Life, and Not Only in Britain

A family's standard of living is how well it lives: its food, its home, its health, and how long its members live. In the 1840s a baby born in Manchester could expect to live only about 25 to 28 years, against about 40 to 42 for England and Wales as a whole. (Those averages are pulled down by the many babies and young children who died; adults who survived childhood lived much longer.) Comfortable middle-class families moved to cleaner streets and, later, to suburbs at the edge of town, away from the smoke.

The College Board ties these challenges to "global capitalism," because the same thing happened wherever industry and trade drew people in. Manhattan, the heart of New York City, grew from about 515,000 people in 1850 to about 1.4 million in 1890, many of them immigrants. In 1890 a reporter, Jacob Riis, wrote that "three-fourths of its people live in the tenements." A tenement was a crowded apartment building for poor renters. Riis had photographed one room, "not thirteen feet either way," where twelve men and women slept; most of the men were lodgers, paying "five cents a spot."

Later in the century, cities began to catch up, building sewers and clean water supplies and setting up police forces (Topic 5.8 has London's new sewers after 1858). Death rates fell, and wages rose faster after about 1860. But in 1900 many city workers were still poor.

AP Thinking, Contextualization

Put one thing inside a bigger story. To contextualize, take one specific thing and ask what larger development, happening in many places at once, explains why it happened. Then say how the two connect.

7

Put It Together

A New Ladder, Still a Ladder

Put the four rows together. Industry added a middle class that rose by money and education and a working class that lived on wages, beside landed families who kept much of their wealth and power. It moved work out of the home, sending working-class women and children into wage jobs while middle-class women were increasingly kept at home. And it packed people into cities that grew faster than they could house, clean or protect them, so standards of living rose for some, while in the fast-growing towns many people's health was far worse than the nation's.

Industry changed almost everything in a hundred and fifty years. Or did it? In Topic 5.10 you will weigh the whole unit: how much did industrialization really change the world from 1750 to 1900?

AP Thinking, Continuity and Change

Three myths to drop. "Industry created social classes": there was a ladder of rank before industry, and landed families stayed near the top. "In the 1800s women stopped working": working-class women and children earned wages; only middle-class women were increasingly kept at home, and even they ran households. "Workers in industrial cities died at 25": the averages are pulled down by the many babies who died.

BeReady: 10-Second Takeaway

Before industry, society was a ladder of rank based on land and birth. Industry rebuilt the ladder. A middle class of owners, merchants and professionals rose by money and education and, in Britain, won the vote in 1832, and an industrial working class lived on wages; but landed families kept much of their land and power (Lord Salisbury, a marquess, was prime minister in 1900). Work moved out of the home: working-class women and often children took wage jobs to add to the family's income, in mills, in domestic service and at home, while middle-class women, whose families did not need their wages, were increasingly kept to the home and the children (the ideal of separate spheres). Cities grew faster than they could cope (Manchester from about 23,000 people in 1773 to more than 300,000, counting the larger borough, by 1851; Manhattan to about 1.4 million by 1890), bringing pollution, poverty, crime, cholera, crowded housing and too few sewers and water pipes, so standards of living rose for some, while in the fast-growing towns many people's health was far worse than the nation's. New classes. Split homes. Crowded cities. A new ladder, still a ladder.
