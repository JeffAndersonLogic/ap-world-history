How Much Changed?

Between 1750 and 1900 industry changed how the world made and moved things. But a change has a size, a reach and a limit. This chapter measures all three, sorts out the other great change of the age, the revolution in ideas and governments, and shows how to connect the evidence into an argument.

How to Use This

The College Board asks you to explain the extent to which industrialization brought change from 1750 to 1900. "Extent" means how much, so this is a measurement: how big the change was, how far it reached, and what kept going beside it. The evidence is the whole unit's, and every number here is the one its own topic teaches. The skill is argumentation: explaining how pieces of evidence relate.

  1. 01 Making: coal, factories, cheaper goods, and who gained.
  2. 02 Moving: railroads, steamships and the telegraph, and why they reached further.
  3. 03 How far it reached: a witness in 1898, the old makers, the farmers, and the gains.
  4. 04 The other revolution: ideas and nations, sorted by date and cause.
  5. 05 Make the argument: three ways evidence connects, and what a thesis about extent needs.
  6. Then the closing section, which gathers the evidence for an answer.
01

Making: Factories and Coal

1750 to 1900  ·  Britain, Europe, the United States

Industry changed how goods were made and what they cost, and moved the making of the world's goods from Asia to Europe and the United States. The gains in living standards were real, slow at first, and unequal.

The starting line

To measure a change you need a starting line. In 1750 almost everything people bought was made by hand, at home or in small workshops, and most of the world's manufactured goods were made in Asia. The economic historian Paul Bairoch estimated that China made about a third of them and India about a quarter, while all of Europe together made about a quarter and Britain under 2 percent. Almost all the energy people used came from things that grow: food for the muscles of people and animals, wood for fire, and wind and water for ships and mills.

How we know: estimates, not counts

Nobody in 1750 counted every piece of cloth in the world. Bairoch's shares are a historian's estimates, built from what records survive, and historians who use them say so. What they show well is the size and direction of the change, not exact amounts. Say "estimated" when you use them.

A new source of energy

The change began with energy. Steam engines turned the heat of burning coal into motion, and from the 1780s James Watt and Matthew Boulton built engines that turned a wheel, so an engine could drive machines. A mill no longer had to sit beside a fast river; it could run wherever coal could be brought. Coal mining and steam engines grew together, and by about 1900 coal supplied about half of all the energy used in the world. That is the size of the change: not just new machines, but a new kind of energy, stored underground for millions of years.

Cheaper goods, and whose

Industrial capitalism, factories and businesses run by owners and investors for profit, kept improving how things were made, so consumer goods became more available, more affordable and more varied, the three words the College Board uses. By 1815 even the coarsest cotton yarn cost about a third of what it had in the early 1780s, after rising prices are taken into account. Soap came in wrapped bars with a brand name, like Sunlight, sold from 1884. And the making moved: Britain's share of the world's manufacturing rose from under 2 percent in 1750 to about a fifth by 1860, and by 1900 Europe and the United States together made more than four-fifths of the world's manufactured goods.

Did people live better? The College Board's answer is careful: standards of living rose "for some." One careful estimate finds British working families' standard of living rose by less than 15 percent from the 1780s to the 1850s, and most historians agree it grew much faster from about 1860 to 1900. In the fast-growing towns, many people's health was far worse than the nation's (Topic 5.9).

Common mistake to avoid

"Industry made everyone richer." The College Board's own words are "for some." Owners and the middle class gained first; working families gained slowly, then faster after about 1860; and many city workers were still poor in 1900. The opposite mistake, "workers gained nothing," goes too far the other way.

02

Moving: Rails, Steam, and Wire

1830 to 1913  ·  the Americas, Russia, India, the oceans

Railroads, steamships and the telegraph reached the interiors of continents around the world, so trade and migration grew. The change in moving reached further than the change in making.

Into the interior

Before the railroad, heavy goods moved cheaply only by water, and the interior of a continent, far from the sea or a big river, was hard to reach. Railways changed that, and not only in Britain. Russia had 27 kilometers of railway in 1840 and more than 53,000 in 1900. India's first passenger train ran in 1853. The first railroad across the United States was finished in 1869.

Ships and news changed too. Under sail, emigrants from Norway took about 53 days on average to reach America; by steamship, the Atlantic crossing from England took about 12 days in the 1860s. From 1844 a telegraph message could travel along a wire in minutes. From 1866 cables under the Atlantic carried messages without a lasting break, and in 1870 a cable reached Bombay, in India.

More trade, more migration

When moving goods got cheaper and faster, markets far apart began to behave like one market. In 1870 wheat cost 57.6 percent more in Liverpool, England, than in Chicago; by 1913, just after this unit's period, it cost only 15.6 percent more. Wheat grown in the middle of North America, carried by train to the coast and by steamship across the ocean, fed workers in Britain.

People moved too. Migration means moving to live in a new place. About 55 million Europeans moved to the Americas and Australia between 1850 and 1914, and between 1846 and 1940 more than 29 million Indians and more than 19 million Chinese moved to Southeast Asia and the lands around the Indian Ocean. Why they moved, and what they found, is Unit 6.

How we know: a price gap

Historians cannot measure "connection" directly, but they can measure prices. When the same grain costs nearly the same in two cities an ocean apart, it means moving it has become cheap and fast. A gap that shrinks from 57.6 percent to 15.6 percent is a number that shows a change over time.

03

How Far It Reached

1840s to 1908  ·  India, China, Egypt, Japan, Britain

The change was deep where it reached, but it did not reach everyone. The old makers kept making, most people still farmed, and the gains went to some more than others.

A witness weighs his century

In 1898 Alfred Russel Wallace, a British naturalist who had worked out the theory of evolution by natural selection at the same time as Charles Darwin, wrote a book weighing his century, The Wonderful Century. His preface claims that "not only is our century superior to any that have gone before it, but that it may be best compared with the whole preceding historical period." Then he turns: "But this is only one side of the shield." More than half his book is about the century's "Failures."

How we know: reading a witness

Wallace lived through most of the century, which makes him a valuable witness, but a witness has a point of view. He wanted land taken out of private hands, and his "Failures" also include attacks on vaccination and a defense of phrenology, ideas scientists rejected. Use him as a claim to test against other evidence, not as the answer.

The old makers, and the farmers

India, China and the Middle East did not stop working. Their share of the world's goods shrank, and China's fell from about a third in 1750 to about 6 percent in 1900, partly because the rest of the world's production grew much faster. But in 1908, in just one province of northern India, about a million people still lived from weaving cloth on handlooms.

And most people still farmed. In India, the census of 1901 found about two-thirds of the people living by farming. Even in the industrial United States, about two in five workers still farmed in 1900. Until about 1900, wood and other things that grow were still the world's largest source of energy.

Gains for some

Only a few states built industry, and not all of it lasted: Muhammad Ali's Egypt built factories for its army, and most had closed by the mid-1840s, while Meiji Japan built railways and mills that lasted (Topic 5.6). Inside industrial countries, the ladder of rank was rebuilt, not ended: landed families kept much of their power, and in 1900 Britain's prime minister, Lord Salisbury, was a marquess. In the 1840s a baby born in Manchester could expect to live only about 25 to 28 years, against about 40 to 42 for England and Wales as a whole, averages pulled down by the many babies who died (Topic 5.9).

Wallace went further. "One of the most prominent features of our century," he wrote, "has been the enormous and continuous growth of wealth, without any corresponding increase in the well-being of the whole people." Test it. The gains were unequal, as he says. But historians find that working families' living standards did rise, slowly to the 1850s and faster after about 1860. Wallace is partly right and partly too far.

Common mistake to avoid

"A shrinking share means the old makers stopped." A share is a part of a whole. China's and India's makers kept working; the whole grew much faster around them. The opposite mistake, "nothing changed for Asia's makers," forgets that India came to buy British cloth and sell raw cotton (Topic 5.4).

04

The Other Revolution: Ideas and Nations

1687 to 1900  ·  Europe and the Americas

The revolution in ideas and governments was the age's other great change. It began before the factories spread, from ideas and grievances, and it met industry later.

By what right?

The Enlightenment was a movement of thinkers who used reason and evidence, first to explain nature and then to question old authorities. They reexamined the role of religion in public life: Voltaire's Treatise on Tolerance (1763) argued that no government should force a faith on anyone. And they wrote new political ideas. In 1689 John Locke argued that people are born with natural rights to "lives, liberties and estates," and that government is a social contract that rests on the people's consent (Topic 5.1).

The ideas came first

The College Board says Enlightenment thought "often preceded" revolutions. The dates show it. The American Revolution began in 1775, the French in 1789, the Haitian in 1791, and the wars for independence in Spanish America in 1810. Factory methods, meanwhile, were only beginning to leave Britain: Samuel Slater's spinning mill in Rhode Island was begun in 1793, John Cockerill's works in Belgium in 1817, and the big growth in Russia and Japan came mostly after 1870 (Topic 5.4). So the political revolution was not industry's. Its causes, Topic 5.2 showed, were a grievance, the words to name it, and a chance to act.

Common mistake to avoid

"Industry caused the revolutions." Check the dates: France's revolution began in 1789, before factories had spread beyond Britain. The opposite mistake, "the two revolutions had nothing to do with each other," forgets where they met: Meiji Japan and the Chartists, below.

New nations, and where the two revolutions met

The revolutions built new countries, the United States, Haiti and the new republics of Spanish America. Each was a nation-state, a state that claims to belong to one people, its nation. They also left a new loyalty, nationalism, which joined most of Italy into one kingdom in 1861 and the German states into an empire in 1871, and pulled Greeks and other Balkan peoples away from the Ottoman Empire (Topic 5.2).

Later, the two revolutions met. Nationalism and fear of stronger powers pushed Meiji Japan to build railways and mills to make the nation strong (Topic 5.6). In industrial Britain, the Chartists used the language of rights to demand the vote for working men (Topic 5.8). And the old order held on in politics too: in 1900 kings and emperors still ruled most of Europe, and women could vote in national elections almost nowhere; New Zealand, in 1893, was the first self-governing country where all adult women could (Topic 5.1).

05

Make the Argument

The whole unit

A list of evidence is not an argument. An argument explains how the pieces relate, and an argument about extent takes a side, names a change and a continuity, and says for whom and why.

Three ways evidence connects

Led to. Coal and steam engines led to railroads and steamships, which led to cheap North American wheat feeding workers in Britain. Each piece is a cause of the next.

The same pattern. Railways reached the interior of Russia and the interior of India: the same change, in two different empires.

A contrast. Lancashire's power looms made cloth more cheaply than India's hand weavers could, yet a million people in one Indian province still lived from the handloom in 1908. Set a change beside a continuity, and you can say how much changed.

What a thesis about extent needs

Your thesis is the claim that answers the question, and you must be able to defend it with evidence. A thesis about extent takes a side: to a great extent, to a limited extent, or great in some ways and limited in others. It names a change and a continuity. And it says for whom or where the change was greatest, and why. "Industrialization changed the world a lot" does none of this: it fits any century in history.

Common mistake to avoid

"Everything changed" and "nothing really changed" are both easy to write and both wrong. The first forgets the farmers, the handloom weavers and the kings; the second forgets the railroads, the telegraph and the price of cloth. A good answer is a measurement in between, with evidence.

Building an Answer That Scores

Each card gathers the evidence for one part of the answer. The College Board asks for the extent to which industrialization brought change, so each card gives how big the change was and its limit, and the last asks you to connect them.

Continuity and Change

Making

Changed: coal about half the world's energy by about 1900; cotton yarn about a third of its 1780s price by 1815; Europe and the United States more than four-fifths of the world's manufactured goods by 1900. Limit: living standards rose "for some," slowly to the 1850s, faster after about 1860.

Continuity and Change

Moving

Changed: Russia's railways from 27 km (1840) to more than 53,000 (1900); India 1853; the United States coast to coast 1869; the Atlantic in about 12 days by steam; telegraph cables 1866 and 1870; the wheat gap from 57.6 to 15.6 percent; tens of millions of migrants.

Continuity and Change

How far it reached

Kept going: a million handloom weavers in one Indian province in 1908; about two-thirds of India's people farming in 1901, and about two in five American workers in 1900; Egypt's factories closed by the mid-1840s; landed families near the top. Test Wallace (1898) against it.

Continuity and Change

The other revolution

Changed, from ideas and grievances, not machines: Enlightenment ideas (Locke, 1689) before the revolutions of 1775, 1789, 1791 and 1810; new nation-states; nationalism (Italy 1861, Germany 1871). Kept going: kings and emperors in most of Europe in 1900; women's votes almost nowhere.

Argumentation

Connect the evidence

Pick two pieces of evidence and name how they relate: one led to the other, they show the same pattern, or they make a contrast. Then take a side on how much changed, for whom, and why.
