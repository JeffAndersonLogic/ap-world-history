First & 10 Reading

Industry on Purpose

In Britain and the United States, private owners built the first factories. A few governments did not wait. Wanting armies to match stronger powers, they built industry themselves. This reading follows two of them, Egypt under Muhammad Ali and Japan in the Meiji era: why each government acted, what it built, and why one strategy lasted while the other mostly ended.

The Question

Why Would a Government Build Factories?

Topic 5.4 followed the factory as it spread from Britain to other parts of Europe, the United States, Russia, and Japan. Topic 5.5 showed how steam, steel, railroads, and the telegraph let industry make far more and reach far farther. In Britain and the United States, most of the first factories were built by private owners and investors, people such as Richard Arkwright or the Boston merchants who paid for the mills at Lowell.

A small number of governments did something different. They did not wait for private owners. They paid for factories themselves, owned them, and ran them. This is state-sponsored industry: industry that a government plans, pays for, and protects.

Why would a government do that? To be strong. By the early 1800s, the countries with factories, steam power, and new weapons were becoming the strongest in the world. A ruler watching them could see that a country without industry could be beaten in war, or forced to sign whatever it was told to sign, and that a ruler with a modern army could hold on to power, or win more. Two of the clearest cases of a government that decided to build industry itself are Egypt under Muhammad Ali and Japan in the Meiji era.

AP Thinking, Causation

Build a chain for each case. The question for today asks for causes and effects. For each country, find four links: the threat (the stronger power or rival the government faced), the decision (to build industry with state money), the strategy (how it paid for it and what it built), and the result. Start with the threat.

2

Egypt, Part 1

A Governor Who Wanted Power of His Own

In 1798 a French army under Napoleon invaded Egypt and easily defeated its rulers, the Mamluks, a class of soldier-lords. The French left in 1801. Among the Ottoman troops sent to take Egypt back was Muhammad Ali, an officer from an Albanian family. In 1805 the Ottomans named him governor of Egypt, and by 1811 he had destroyed his rivals and ruled alone.

Egypt was a province of the Ottoman Empire, and Muhammad Ali was the sultan's governor. But he wanted Egypt strong enough that he, and his family after him, could hold it, even against the sultan. Napoleon's easy victory had shown what a modern European army could do, and Muhammad Ali wanted an army like it. Strength meant an army, and an army needed guns, uniforms, and ships.

So his government took control of Egypt's farming. It told peasants what to grow, bought their crops at low prices, and sold them abroad at higher ones. When only one buyer or seller is allowed, that is a monopoly. A new crop, long-staple cotton (cotton with long fibers, which makes strong, fine thread), first grown for sale in 1820, earned a great deal of money.

With that money, from the late 1810s, the government built factories: cotton mills, and works for weapons, sugar, and other goods, with machines bought in Europe and European experts to run them. By the early 1830s about 30 cotton mills employed about 30,000 workers, many of them making cloth and uniforms for the army. The workers did not choose these jobs. They were drafted, the way Egyptian peasants had long been made to dig canals. From 1822 peasants were drafted into the army, too. Forcing people to serve is called conscription, and by the early 1830s the army had about 130,000 soldiers. In the 1820s he also sent Egyptian students to Europe to learn its sciences. To protect the new mills, Muhammad Ali kept cheap British cloth out of Egypt.

AP Thinking, Causation

Follow the money. A strategy has to be paid for. In Egypt the chain ran: the state buys crops cheaply, sells cotton abroad, and spends the money on factories and an army. Who paid in the end? The peasants, with their crops and their labor.

3

Egypt, Part 2

It Worked, Then It Stopped

For a while the strategy worked. In 1831 Muhammad Ali's army, led by his son Ibrahim, invaded Syria and beat the sultan's own armies more than once.

Then it stopped. Britain, Austria, Russia, and Prussia did not want a strong Egypt breaking the Ottoman Empire apart. In 1840 they sided with the sultan, and British warships and Ottoman troops drove Ibrahim out of Syria. In 1841 the sultan made the governorship of Egypt hereditary in Muhammad Ali's family, passing to its eldest male. But Egypt had to give up its conquests except Sudan, and its army was cut to 18,000 men in peacetime. Egypt also had to accept a trade treaty that Britain had signed with the Ottoman sultan in 1838. It ended Muhammad Ali's monopolies and opened Egypt to cheap foreign goods.

The factories had problems of their own, too. Egypt had almost no coal, so many machines were turned by animals instead of steam engines. The drafted workers resisted, and the managers had little experience. By the mid-1840s, about a decade after their peak in the early 1830s, most of the new factories had failed. Historians still argue about which cause mattered most, and about how much the factories achieved while they lasted.

Egypt went on growing more and more cotton, almost all of it sold abroad, much of it to the mills of Britain. What that led to is Unit 6.

AP Thinking, Causation

More than one cause. Some causes of the result came from outside Egypt (the powers of 1840 and the 1838 treaty) and some from inside (almost no coal, forced workers, new managers). A strong answer names at least one of each, and does not pretend one cause explains everything.

Japan, Part 1

Ships That Changed a Government

In 1853 an American naval officer, Commodore Matthew Perry, sailed warships into Edo Bay, near today's Tokyo, and demanded that Japan open its ports to trade. Japan had no navy that could stop him. Treaties in 1854 and 1858 opened Japanese ports to Americans and Europeans, let foreigners accused of crimes in Japan be tried in their own countries' courts, and took away Japan's control over its own trade. A treaty forced on a weaker country, giving the stronger side rights it does not give back, is an unequal treaty. Some samurai blamed the shogun, the military ruler who governed Japan in the emperor's name.

In 1868 a group of young samurai overthrew the shogun and governed in the name of the fifteen-year-old emperor, Meiji. This is the Meiji Restoration. In April 1868 the new government made a promise, the Charter Oath: "Knowledge shall be sought throughout the world so as to strengthen the foundations of imperial rule." The government's slogan was "Rich country, strong army."

The Meiji leaders changed Japan itself first. In 1871 they abolished the lands of the old lords and made one central state. The samurai lost their special rights, and men of every class were drafted into a national army. A national school system began in 1872. A new land tax, paid in money instead of rice, gave the government money to spend. From 1871 to 1873 a group of leaders, the Iwakura Mission, toured the United States and Europe to study how they worked.

AP Thinking, Causation

Outside pressure, inside reform. The College Board's Key Concept is itself a chain: U.S. and European influence led to reform inside Japan, and the reform supported industry. Name the outside cause (Perry, the unequal treaties) and one reform it led to.

5

Japan, Part 2

Build It, Sell It, Grow Strong

Then the state built industry. The government built Japan's first railway, from Tokyo to Yokohama, which opened in 1872 with British engineers. In the same year it opened the Tomioka Silk Mill, with French machines and a French engineer, Paul Brunat, to teach Japanese workers, mostly young women, to reel silk by machine. It built telegraph lines, shipyards, mines, and factories for weapons and for goods such as cement, glass, and cloth. Farmers paid for much of this through the land tax. Some samurai rebelled against losing their place, and in 1877 the new army of drafted commoners crushed the last great revolt.

Running so many businesses cost more than the government could afford. In 1880 it decided to sell most of them to private investors. Business families such as Mitsui and Mitsubishi bought them and grew into huge business groups called zaibatsu. Private companies such as the Osaka Spinning Company (Topic 5.4) built more. The state started key industries; private companies carried them on and built much more.

Japan grew strong. To win back its own courts, it rewrote its laws on French and German models, and in July 1894 Britain agreed to end the foreign courts in Japan from 1899, so that foreigners there would be tried by Japanese law; other countries followed. Japan won back full control of its taxes on foreign goods only in 1911. Weeks later, in 1894 and 1895, Japan fought China over Korea and won. In the Treaty of Shimonoseki, China gave up its claim over Korea, gave the island of Taiwan to Japan, and agreed to pay 200 million taels of silver (a tael is a Chinese measure of silver). Japan, forced to open its ports forty years before, was now a regional power, a state strong enough to win wars and gain land in its own part of the world, with a colony of its own. What empires did with that power is Unit 6.

AP Thinking, Causation

The last link. The Key Concept ends with Japan's "growing regional power." Do not stop your chain at the factories: say what the industry and reforms led to, with one piece of evidence from 1894 or 1895.

6

The Pattern

Two Chains, Side by Side

Put the two cases side by side. Both governments wanted an army and industry to match stronger powers: Muhammad Ali, to match armies like Napoleon's and to hold Egypt against the sultan; Japan, to answer the Western warships. In both, the state, not private owners, paid for the first factories. Both bought foreign machines, hired foreign experts, and sent people abroad to learn. In both, farmers paid much of the bill. And both wanted the same result: a state strong enough to defend itself, with an army to match. When the same links show up in different cases, that is a pattern.

The difference is what happened next. Japan's industry lasted and grew; Egypt's mostly closed. Historians point to several reasons. Japan was never conquered or colonized, though it was forced into unequal treaties, while Egypt was a province of the Ottoman Empire that European powers forced to cut its army and open its markets. Japan reformed its whole society, while Egypt's factories were one ruler's project, run with drafted workers. Japan had coal of its own, and Egypt had almost none. And Japan's government handed its industries to private companies that kept them running. Japan also had to accept low taxes on foreign goods under its unequal treaties, about 5 percent from 1866, through the years it was building its industry, so outside pressure alone does not explain the difference.

So, what caused these economic strategies, and what were their effects? The wish for armies to match stronger powers made a small number of governments build industry themselves. Muhammad Ali's Egypt built cotton mills and weapons works to supply an army; it won wars for a while, but European pressure, missing coal, and forced labor closed most of the factories. In Japan, Perry's warships and the unequal treaties led to the Meiji reforms; the state built railways and mills and then sold them to private companies, and the industry it started helped make Japan strong enough to defeat China and take Taiwan, while its legal reforms began ending the unequal treaties.

All this industry made goods, and goods had to be paid for, traded, and financed. Who owned the factories, and how did money move around the industrial world? That is Topic 5.7.

AP Thinking, Making Connections

Three myths to drop. "Japan just copied the West": Japan's leaders chose what to borrow and from whom, seeking knowledge from many countries, which the Charter Oath said would "strengthen the foundations of imperial rule." "Muhammad Ali was building an Egyptian nation": he was an Ottoman governor from an Albanian family whose court spoke Turkish, and he built for his own and his family's power. "One cause explains Egypt's result": historians weigh European pressure, missing coal, drafted workers, and new managers, and still disagree about how much each mattered.

BeReady: 10-Second Takeaway

In Britain and the United States, private owners built the first factories, but a small number of governments built industry themselves, wanting armies to match stronger powers. In Egypt, Muhammad Ali, an Ottoman governor who wanted to hold power for his family, took control of farming and cotton and used the money to build about 30 cotton mills and weapons works for an army of about 130,000 drafted peasants. The army won wars, but in 1840 and 1841 European powers forced Egypt out of Syria and cut its army to 18,000, the 1838 trade treaty ended its monopolies, and with almost no coal and forced workers most factories had failed by the mid-1840s. In Japan, Perry's warships in 1853 and the unequal treaties led to the Meiji Restoration of 1868. The new government reformed Japan, built railways, the Tomioka Silk Mill, shipyards, and mines, and from 1880 sold them to private companies such as Mitsui and Mitsubishi. After Japan rewrote its laws, Britain agreed in 1894 to end its foreign courts in Japan, and Japan won a war with China in 1894 and 1895 and took Taiwan: it had become a regional power. Two states built industry. Japan's grew. Egypt's mostly closed.
