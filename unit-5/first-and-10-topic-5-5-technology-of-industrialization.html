The Question

Where Did Energy Come From in 1750?

Topic 5.3 ended at a mill driven by water. Richard Arkwright's mill at Cromford ran on a water wheel, and coal, in 1700, was mostly something people burned to keep warm. Topic 5.4 followed the factory as it spread. This reading asks a different question: how did the machines themselves change what the world could make?

In 1750 almost all the energy used in the world came from things that grow. Food gave energy to the muscles of people and animals. Wood gave fire for heat and cooking. Wind moved ships and turned some mills, and rivers turned water wheels. Every one of these had a limit. A water wheel works only where a stream flows strongly. A forest takes decades to grow back. A horse gets tired. Until about 1900, wood and other things that grow were still the world's largest source of energy.

But there was energy buried underground. Coal and oil are each a fossil fuel: the remains of plants and tiny sea creatures, buried for millions of years, with energy stored inside them. Britain was already the exception: it burned so much coal for heat that, by 1700, coal gave England about half of its energy (Topic 5.3). The trick was to turn that heat into motion.

AP Thinking, Causation

Build a chain. A causation question asks what made something happen. This topic is a chain of causes: a machine made something possible, and that made the next thing possible. Start your chain here, with the problem: before 1750, energy was limited to what grows, blows, and flows.

2

Power, Part 1

A Pump, Then an Engine That Turns Wheels

The first useful steam engines were pumps. In 1712 Thomas Newcomen set up a steam engine near Dudley Castle, in the English Midlands, to pump water out of a coal mine. Burning coal boiled water into steam, and the steam helped move a heavy beam up and down. It worked, but it burned a great deal of coal, because its cylinder was cooled and heated again on every stroke.

James Watt, an instrument maker at the University of Glasgow, found the fix in 1765: cool the steam in a separate chamber, so the cylinder stays hot. He patented the idea in 1769. Watt's engines used less than a third of the coal of a Newcomen engine.

Then came the change that mattered most for factories. In the 1780s Watt and his business partner, Matthew Boulton, built engines that turned a wheel, so an engine could drive machines. A mill no longer had to sit beside a fast river. It could run on coal, wherever coal could be brought, and cotton mills crowded into towns such as Manchester.

Explain a process step by step. To explain how a technology changed production, say four things: what the problem was, what the new machine did, what that made possible, and what followed. Try it on Watt's engine before you read on.

3

Power, Part 2

More Coal, Then Oil

Steam engines needed coal, and coal mines needed steam engines to pump them dry, so each made the other grow. Britain mined about 65 million tons of coal in 1854 and about 225 million tons in 1900. By about 1900, coal supplied about half of all the energy used in the whole world, nearly as much as wood and other things that grow. Historians call this the fossil fuels revolution: machines let people use energy that had been stored for millions of years, far more than muscles, wood, wind, and water could give.

Oil came next. In 1859 Edwin Drake drilled a well at Titusville, Pennsylvania, and struck oil. For the rest of the 1800s, most oil was turned into kerosene, a fuel for lamps.

A new kind of engine would later make oil far more important. An internal combustion engine burns its fuel inside the engine itself, instead of boiling water in a separate boiler. In 1876 Nikolaus Otto built a four-stroke gas engine in Germany, and in 1886 Carl Benz patented a car driven by a small engine that burned a fuel made from oil. But in 1900 the United States had only about 8,000 cars. The internal combustion engine's big changes, to cars, trucks, tractors, and airplanes, came after 1900.

AP Thinking, Causation

Effects can come late. A cause does not always work right away. Watt's engine took decades to spread, and the internal combustion engine changed the world mostly after 1900. When you explain a cause, say when its effects came.

4

New Materials

The Second Industrial Revolution

After 1850, with so much energy to use, people found new ways to make things. Historians call this the second industrial revolution. It brought new methods in four areas.

Steel. Steel is iron with a small, carefully measured amount of carbon. It is stronger and tougher than ordinary iron, but it was slow and costly to make. In 1856 Henry Bessemer showed that blowing air through melted iron burned out the impurities and made steel quickly. The United States made about 69,000 tons of steel in 1870 and about 10 million tons in 1900. Britain went from about 215,000 tons to about 4.9 million. Steel became rails, bridges, ships, and machines.

Chemicals. In 1856 an eighteen-year-old London chemistry student, William Perkin, made a purple dye, mauve, from chemicals that came from coal tar, a sticky leftover of coal. German chemical companies such as BASF, founded in 1865, built laboratories to invent new dyes, and in 1897 BASF began selling indigo blue made in a factory instead of from a plant. By 1914 Germany made most of the world's dyes.

Electricity. In 1882 Thomas Edison's Pearl Street Station in New York began selling electric power to light about 400 lamps. Electric motors would let each machine run on its own motor, instead of on a belt from one big steam engine. But this change was slow: in 1899, electric motors gave less than 5 percent of the power in American factories.

Precision machinery. Machine tools had begun to cut metal parts so exactly that one part fit any copy of the same machine, and after 1850 the method spread widely. These are interchangeable parts. Standard screw threads helped: Joseph Whitworth set one early, in Britain in 1841, and William Sellers set one in the United States in 1864. They helped machines with interchangeable parts, from typewriters to locomotives, spread.

AP Thinking, Causation

Name the link. How does the second industrial revolution connect to Section 3? Coal ran the furnaces that made steel, and coal tar gave the first synthetic dyes to sell well. As you read on, ask what the railroads in Section 5 were made of.

5

Longer Reach

Railroads, Steamships, and the Telegraph Reach the Interior

Energy and steel built the machines that moved things. Before them, heavy goods moved cheaply only by water. Places far from a river or the sea, the interiors of continents, were hard to reach.

Railroads. The Liverpool and Manchester Railway, opened in England in 1830, was the world's first railway between two cities, with trains pulled by steam locomotives. Railways then spread fast. Russia had 27 kilometers of railway in 1840 and more than 53,000 in 1900. India's first passenger train ran from Bombay to Thane in 1853. In 1869 the first railroad across the United States was finished at Promontory Summit, Utah. Russia began the Trans-Siberian Railway in 1891, and by 1904 trains could cross to the Pacific Ocean. A railroad could reach farms, mines, and towns in the middle of a continent, where no river or canal went.

Steamships. Under sail, emigrants from Norway took about 53 days on average for the whole trip to America. By steamship, the Atlantic crossing from England took about 12 days in the 1860s, and the fastest ships of the 1870s crossed in about 7 or 8 days. The Suez Canal, opened in Egypt in 1869, was of little use to sailing ships, because the winds of the Red Sea were hard to sail against, so it mostly served steamships. Steamboats also pushed up rivers into the interior of continents. In 1854 the steamer Pleiad went far up the Niger and Benue rivers in West Africa, and none of its crew died, because they took quinine, a medicine against malaria. What European empires did with this reach is Unit 6.

The telegraph. A telegraph sends messages as pulses of electricity along a wire. In 1844 Samuel Morse sent the message "What hath God wrought?" from Washington to Baltimore. Before, a message traveled only as fast as a horse or a ship. Now it could travel along a wire in minutes. From 1866, cables under the Atlantic carried messages without a lasting break, and one reached Bombay, in India, in 1870.

AP Thinking, Developments and Processes

Three ways to reach. Railroads carried goods and people over land. Steamships carried them over oceans and up rivers. The telegraph carried news. For each one, ask: what could people now do that they could not do before?

6

The Effects

More Trade, More Migration

Cheaper, faster transport and fast news joined distant markets. In 1870 wheat cost 57.6 percent more in Liverpool, England, than in Chicago. By 1913 the gap was only 15.6 percent. Wheat grown in the middle of North America, carried by train to the coast and by steamship across the ocean, fed workers in Britain.

Trains and ships carried people, too. Migration means people moving to live in a new place. About 55 million Europeans moved to the Americas and Australia between 1850 and 1914. Between 1846 and 1940, more than 29 million Indians and more than 19 million Chinese moved to Southeast Asia and the lands around the Indian Ocean, and tens of millions of Chinese and Russians moved into Manchuria, Siberia, and Central Asia. Steamships and railways made these journeys faster and cheaper. Why people moved, and what they found, is Unit 6.

So, how did technology shape economic production between 1750 and 1900? Machines such as the steam engine, and later the internal combustion engine, let people use the energy stored in coal and oil, which greatly increased the energy available. That energy, and new methods for making steel, chemicals, electricity, and precise machines after 1850, let factories make far more goods, and new kinds of goods. Railroads, steamships, and the telegraph, built of that steel and driven by that coal, carried goods, people, and news into the interiors of continents, which led to more trade and more migration.

A few governments did not wait for these machines to arrive on their own. They decided to build industry themselves. That is Topic 5.6.

AP Thinking, Causation

Three myths to drop. "Watt invented the steam engine": Newcomen's engines were pumping mines from 1712, more than fifty years before Watt's patent; Watt made the engine use far less coal and turn wheels. "One invention changed everything": each technology needed the others; railroads needed steam, coal, and then steel. "By 1900 the world ran on electricity and cars": electric motors gave under 5 percent of American factory power in 1899, and the United States had about 8,000 cars in 1900. Some of the biggest effects came later.

BeReady: 10-Second Takeaway

In 1750 almost all the energy used in the world came from muscles, wood, wind, and water. Then machines unlocked the energy stored in fossil fuels. Newcomen's steam engine of 1712 pumped water out of a coal mine; Watt's engines used far less coal, and from the 1780s they turned wheels, so factories could run on coal instead of rivers. Britain's coal output rose to about 225 million tons by 1900, when coal supplied about half the world's energy. Oil came next, at first as lamp fuel, and the internal combustion engine's big changes came after 1900. After 1850 a second industrial revolution brought new methods: Bessemer steel, dyes made from coal tar, electric power, and precise machines with interchangeable parts. Railroads, steamships, and the telegraph carried goods, people, and news into the interiors of continents, so trade grew and tens of millions of people migrated. More power. New materials. Longer reach.
