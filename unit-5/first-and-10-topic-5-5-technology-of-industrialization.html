AP World History · Unit 5: Revolutions · Topic 5.5

The Energy Machine

Industrial technology changed production because it unlocked far more energy, created new materials and machines, and made distance cheaper to cross. Coal and steam, oil and internal combustion, steel, chemicals, electricity, railroads, steamships, and telegraphs formed an interdependent system rather than a list of isolated inventions.

01

Energy

Coal and Oil Multiply the Energy Available to Society

For most of human history, production depended on muscle, wood, wind, and water. The steam engine changed that relationship by converting the stored energy in coal into continuous mechanical power.

Factories could operate away from fast-flowing rivers, mines could pump water from deeper shafts, and locomotives and steamships could move loads no animal team could match. The point was not simply that steam was “faster.” It multiplied the amount of usable energy available for work.

Later in the 19th century, the internal combustion engine began turning oil into another concentrated, portable source of mechanical power. Coal remained dominant, but the energy base of industrial society was expanding.

AP Thinking, Causation

Coal/oil -> concentrated energy -> mechanical power -> greater production and transport capacity. Explain that chain instead of memorizing engine names.

02

Production

The Second Industrial Revolution Changes What Factories Can Make

The later 19th century brought new methods in steel, chemicals, electricity, and precision machinery. The Bessemer process and related methods made structural steel cheaper, supporting larger machines, bridges, ships, and rail networks.

Chemical industries produced dyes, fertilizers, explosives, medicines, and industrial inputs. Electric lighting and motors extended working time and allowed machinery to be powered more flexibly inside factories.

Precision machinery made standardized parts and complex equipment more reliable. Together these technologies increased productive capacity and broadened the range of goods industrial economies could make at scale.

AP Thinking, Mechanism

For each innovation, identify the production problem it solved: cheaper material, new source of power, standardized parts, or a new chemical product.

03

Transport

Railroads and Steamships Make Distance Cheaper

Railroads sharply lowered overland transportation costs. Farms, mines, and factories in interior regions could reach cities and ports, while manufactured goods could move back into those regions.

Steamships made ocean travel faster and more regular because schedules depended less on wind. Combined with canals and modern ports, they increased the volume and predictability of long-distance trade and passenger movement.

The economic effect was larger than transportation itself: lower costs widened markets, encouraged investment in interior resources, and made migration across continents and oceans easier.

AP Thinking, Effects

Infrastructure changes economic geography. Ask which place becomes profitable or reachable only after transport costs fall.

04

Communication

The Telegraph Lets Information Outrun Physical Travel

The telegraph allowed prices, orders, political instructions, and news to travel faster than ships or trains. Undersea cables eventually connected major financial and imperial centres across oceans.

Businesses could coordinate shipments and production more quickly; governments could issue instructions across distant territories; merchants could react to supply and price changes before goods physically arrived.

Railroads, steamships, and telegraphs were complementary. Physical transport moved people and goods; telegraphy moved the information needed to coordinate those movements.

BeReady: 10-Second Takeaway

More energy + new production technologies + cheaper transport + faster information = larger industrial markets, more trade, and more migration.

BeReady: 10-Second Takeaway

Topic 5.5 is a technology-to-production story. Steam engines made coal into large-scale mechanical power; internal combustion opened a growing role for oil. The second industrial revolution expanded production through steel, chemicals, electricity, and precision machinery. Railroads and steamships reduced the cost of moving people and goods, while the telegraph reduced the time needed to move information. Together these technologies increased production, opened interior regions, and expanded trade and migration.
