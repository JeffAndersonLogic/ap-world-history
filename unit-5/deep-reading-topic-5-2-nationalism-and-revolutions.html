How Revolutions Made Nations

By 1789 the new ideas about rights were everywhere educated people read, but ideas alone never overthrew a king. This chapter follows four revolutions, in America, France, Haiti and Spanish America, asks what caused each one and what it created, and then follows the loyalty they left behind, to the nation, through the rest of the 1800s.

01

The Recipe, and America

1763 to 1787  ·  Grievance, words, chance

Many people read Enlightenment writers and went on obeying their kings. A revolution came when three things met: a grievance many people felt, the words to name it, and a chance to act. America had all three first.

Why ideas were not enough

Topic 5.1 showed that the new ideas about natural rights and the consent of the people had spread across Europe and the Americas by 1789. But reading a book is not the same as risking your life. Across this topic, three ingredients turn up again and again. The first is a grievance: a wrong that many people feel, such as an unfair tax, a privilege for a few, slavery, or a law that shuts people out of power. The second is the words: a document that names the grievance in the language of rights, something people can read aloud and rally around. The third is a chance: a moment when the old ruler is broke, far away, at war, or gone.

Common mistake to avoid

Do not write that the Enlightenment caused the revolutions. The College Board says Enlightenment ideas "influenced resistance" and that "discontent with monarchist and imperial rule" pushed people toward new kinds of government. The ideas were one cause. Name the others.

The grievance and the words

Britain won the Seven Years' War in 1763, but it came out of the war deep in debt. Parliament decided its thirteen American colonies should help pay for the soldiers who guarded them, and it taxed them with laws such as the Sugar Act of 1764 and the Stamp Act of 1765, which taxed newspapers, legal papers and playing cards. The colonists had no members in Parliament, so they answered with a slogan: "no taxation without representation." Protest reached ordinary homes: English potters even sold colonists teapots painted "No Stamp Act."

On 4 July 1776 the Continental Congress adopted the Declaration of Independence. It argued that "all men are created equal," that governments get "their just powers from the consent of the governed," and that when a government destroys people's rights, "it is the Right of the People to alter or to abolish it." That is the social contract from Topic 5.1, turned into a reason to break away.

The chance, and what it created

Britain was an ocean away, and in 1778 France, Britain's old rival, joined the war on the American side with money, soldiers and a navy. By 1783 Britain had recognized the independence of the United States. In 1787 the new country wrote a constitution for a republic: a country with no king, whose leaders are elected.

The College Board calls the American Revolution and its republic "a model and inspiration for a number of the revolutions that followed." It was also limited. The republic kept slavery, and most states let only men who owned property vote.

Use this in your answer
Strongest tool, with its mechanism
Name all three ingredients. British taxes were the grievance, the Declaration of Independence gave it words, and distance and French help gave the colonists their chance.
Best evidence of exclusion or limit
The revolution's "all men are created equal" did not end slavery, and the new states limited voting to some men.
Comparison hook
Against France (section 02): the American revolutionaries broke away from a ruler across an ocean; the French remade their own kingdom.

Terms to use precisely

Grievance
A wrong that many people feel, such as an unfair tax or a law that shuts them out.
Stamp Act
A 1765 British tax on printed papers in the American colonies, repealed in 1766.
Consent of the governed
The idea that a government's rightful power comes from the people who live under it.
Republic
A country with no king, whose leaders are elected.
02

France: The Bill Comes Due

1788 to 1804  ·  A kingdom remade

France's help was crucial to the American victory, and paying for it, on top of older war debts, pushed the French king to the edge of bankruptcy. One revolution's effect became the next one's chance.

The chance came from America

France had spent heavily on the American war, on top of the debts of earlier wars, and by 1788 King Louis XVI's government was nearly bankrupt. To raise new taxes, the king called the Estates-General, an assembly of the three "estates" of France: the clergy, the nobles, and everyone else. It had not met since 1614.

The grievance

The first two estates had privileges. The clergy and the nobles paid little in taxes, and the Third Estate, most of the people of France, from rich lawyers to poor peasants, paid most of them. After a bad harvest in 1788, the price of bread, the main food of the poor, soared. When the Estates-General met in May 1789, the Third Estate refused to be outvoted by the other two, and in June it declared itself the National Assembly. On 14 July a Paris crowd stormed the Bastille, a royal fortress and prison, to seize its gunpowder.

The words

In August 1789 the Assembly ended the nobles' special privileges and adopted the Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen. Its first article reads, "Men are born and remain free and equal in rights." Its third reads, "The principle of all sovereignty resides essentially in the nation." Sovereignty is the highest power in a state, and the declaration said it belonged to the nation, not to the king.

How we know: compare the documents

Put two revolutionary documents side by side and read them closely. Say each one's argument in your own words, then find one way the arguments are alike and one way they differ, and explain the difference by the grievance each document answered. That is the move the College Board's skill 3.C asks for.

What it created

France abolished its monarchy in 1792 and became a republic, and in January 1793 it executed the king. At war with much of Europe and afraid of enemies at home, the government began the Terror (1793 to 1794), during which it killed thousands of people it called enemies of the revolution. In 1799 a general, Napoleon Bonaparte, took power, and in 1804 he crowned himself emperor. His armies carried the revolution's laws, such as the end of noble privilege, across Europe, and they also made many Europeans hate French rule.

Strongest tool, with its mechanism
Show the link. France's spending on the American war left it nearly bankrupt, which forced the king to call the Estates-General, where the French Revolution began.
Best evidence of exclusion or limit
The revolution that began against a king ended with an emperor, and the Terror killed thousands in the name of the people.
Comparison hook
Against Haiti (section 03): the same declaration that France wrote for itself was claimed by people France held in slavery.

Terms to use precisely

Estates-General
France's old assembly of clergy, nobles and commoners, called in 1788 to meet in May 1789, for the first time since 1614.
Third Estate
Everyone in France who was not clergy or noble: most of the people.
Sovereignty
The highest power in a state.
The Terror
The years 1793 to 1794, when France's revolutionary government executed thousands of people it called enemies.
03

Haiti: The Words Claimed

1791 to 1825  ·  Saint-Domingue becomes Haiti

France's declaration said men are born free and equal. In Saint-Domingue, the people France held in slavery claimed those words for themselves, rose up first, and won a country of their own.

The grievance

Saint-Domingue, on the western part of the Caribbean island of Hispaniola, was France's most valuable colony. Its sugar and coffee were grown by enslaved Africans and their children, worked and punished brutally on plantations. Around 1789 the colony held more than 400,000 enslaved people and fewer than 80,000 free people, white colonists and free people of color (free people of African or mixed descent) together: more than five enslaved people for every free person.

The words, and the chance

When the 1789 declaration reached the colony, free people of color asked the Assembly in Paris for the equal rights it promised. The Assembly left the question to the colonists, and the white planters refused. In 1790 a free man of color, Vincent Ogé, led a small revolt for those rights and was executed. Paris granted free people of color equal rights only in April 1792, after the revolution in the colony had begun.

With France in revolution and the colony's white planters divided, enslaved people in the northern plain rose up in August 1791. They burned plantations across the northern plain. Toussaint Louverture, who had been born enslaved and had won his freedom before the revolution, became the revolution's most famous leader. In 1793, fighting on Spain's side against France, he announced: "I want Liberty and Equality to reign in Saint Domingue."

Common mistake to avoid

Do not write that France gave Haiti its freedom. Enslaved people rose up in 1791. French officials in the colony began freeing enslaved people in 1793 to keep their side's soldiers, and in February 1794 the government in Paris abolished slavery in all French colonies, after three years of fighting. Toussaint changed sides to France a few months later. Haitians then won their independence by defeating Napoleon's army.

What it created

In 1802 Napoleon brought slavery back in other French colonies, such as Guadeloupe, and sent an army to retake Saint-Domingue. Toussaint was arrested and died in a French prison in the mountains in 1803. His officer Jean-Jacques Dessalines fought on, and yellow fever and the war destroyed the French army. On 1 January 1804 Dessalines declared independence and gave the country its old Taíno name, Haiti.

Haiti was the second independent country in the Americas, after the United States, and the first founded by people who had freed themselves from slavery. Its effects reached other countries. In April 1803, with his army in Saint-Domingue dying of yellow fever and a new war with Britain coming, Napoleon sold Louisiana to the United States. Countries that allowed slavery feared Haiti's example: the United States did not recognize Haiti until 1862, and in 1825 France demanded 150 million francs, later cut to 90 million, as the price of recognizing Haiti's independence. And in 1816 Haiti helped the next revolution (section 04).

Best evidence of exclusion or limit
Haiti paid for its independence: in money to France, and in years of isolation by countries that allowed slavery.
Comparison hook
Against the United States (section 01): both broke from a European empire, but only Haiti's revolution ended slavery.

Terms to use precisely

Saint-Domingue
France's Caribbean sugar and coffee colony, which became Haiti in 1804.
Free people of color
Free people of African or mixed descent in the colonies, who had property but not equal rights.
Toussaint Louverture
A formerly enslaved man who became the most famous leader of the Haitian Revolution.
Jean-Jacques Dessalines
Toussaint's officer, who declared Haiti's independence on 1 January 1804.
04

Spanish America: An Empire With No King

1808 to 1826  ·  Bolívar and the new states

Creoles in Spain's American empire had grievances for decades. Their chance came when Napoleon, the French Revolution's general, took Spain's king, and the last revolution's creation, Haiti, helped them win.

The grievance

In Spain's American empire, creoles, people of Spanish descent born in the Americas, owned land, mines and businesses, but the highest offices in the church, the army and the government went to peninsulares, men born in Spain. Spain also controlled what the colonies could grow, make and trade. Simón Bolívar, a creole from a rich Caracas family, wrote that Spain "has kept us in a sort of permanent infancy with regard to public affairs": treated like children, never allowed to govern.

The chance

In 1808 Napoleon invaded Spain and forced its king from the throne, putting his own brother in his place. Spanish America suddenly had no rightful king to obey. In 1810 creole leaders set up governments of their own, in Caracas and in Buenos Aires, at first in the name of the captive king, and many soon moved to full independence. In Mexico a priest, Miguel Hidalgo, called on Native and mestizo villagers to rise up.

The words, and help from Haiti

The wars went badly at first. In 1815, beaten and in exile on the British island of Jamaica, Bolívar wrote a long letter to a British reader, now called the Letter from Jamaica. "The hatred that the Peninsula has inspired in us," he wrote, "is greater than the ocean between us," and the tie to Spain "has been severed." He also doubted that governments "wholly representative," chosen entirely by the people's votes, suited Spanish America yet.

Then Haiti helped. In 1816 Haiti's president, Alexandre Pétion, gave Bolívar guns, supplies and men for a new expedition, and asked him in return to end slavery in the lands he freed.

What it created

By 1826 Spain had lost every colony in the Americas except Cuba and Puerto Rico. Mexico, Gran Colombia, Peru, Chile, the provinces of the Río de la Plata and others became independent countries, and Brazil broke away from Portugal in 1822 with an emperor of its own. Creole elites took the top places. Most of the new countries kept a sharp social hierarchy, and slavery lasted in several of them for decades: Colombia ended it in 1851, Venezuela and Peru in 1854.

Best evidence of exclusion or limit

Comparison hook

People of Spanish descent born in the Americas.
Peninsulares

05

New Ideas, and the Nation

1789 to 1878  ·  Liberalism, democracy, nationalism

Some of the revolutions' longest-lasting effects were ideas: new answers to how a country should be run, and a new loyalty, to the nation, that joined some states together and pulled peoples away from empires.

New ways to govern

Behind all four revolutions was anger at kings and empires, and the revolutions left behind new answers to an old question: how should a country be run? One was the republic, with a written constitution, a set of basic laws that even the government must follow. Another was liberalism: the belief that a constitution and an elected parliament should limit the government and protect each person's rights, such as free speech, a free press and property. Many liberals in the 1800s still wanted only men with property to vote. A third was democracy, rule by the people, which pushed for the vote to reach all men.

These ideas did not stay on paper. They set off new revolutions across Europe in 1830 and 1848, when crowds demanded constitutions, parliaments and wider voting. Most of the uprisings of 1848 were crushed within two years, but the demands did not go away.

From "the people" to the nation

The revolutions said that a state belongs to its people. That raised a new question: which people? More and more, people answered with the nation: everyone who shared a language, a religion, customs and a homeland. Nationalism put loyalty to the nation above loyalty to a king or an empire.

Governments learned to use that loyalty. In 1793, at war with much of Europe, revolutionary France ordered that "all Frenchmen are in permanent requisition for the service of the armies": young men would fight, married men would make weapons, women would make tents and work in hospitals, and old people would go to public squares to preach "the unity of the Republic." But nationalism could also be turned against a government, by people who felt they were a nation without a country of their own.

Nationalism joins states

Italy was split into several states, some ruled by Austria. Italian nationalists, among them Count Cavour, the chief minister of the kingdom of Piedmont-Sardinia, and the soldier Giuseppe Garibaldi, whose volunteers took Sicily and Naples in 1860, joined most of Italy into one kingdom in 1861, and added Rome in 1870. Joining states into one is called unification.

Germany was split into more than thirty states. In 1848 German liberals met in Frankfurt to unite Germany with an elected parliament and a constitution, and failed. Then Otto von Bismarck, the chief minister of Prussia, told Prussia's lawmakers in 1862 that the great questions of the time are not decided "by speeches and majority resolutions," which had been "the big mistake of 1848 and 1849," but "by iron and blood." After three wars, against Denmark, Austria and France, the German Empire was proclaimed on 18 January 1871 in the Hall of Mirrors at Versailles, the palace Louis XIV built.

Nationalism breaks an empire

The Ottoman Empire ruled many peoples in the Balkans, in southeastern Europe, Muslim and Christian. Serbs rebelled and won the right to rule themselves. Greeks rose up in 1821, and in 1830 Britain, France and Russia declared Greece an independent state; it became a kingdom under a Bavarian prince in 1832. Other Balkan peoples followed, and in 1878 Serbia, Montenegro and Romania were recognized as independent.

The Ottoman government answered with a loyalty of its own, Ottomanism. In 1856 it promised equal treatment to all its subjects, Muslim or not, and in 1869 a law made every subject an Ottoman citizen. The idea was one loyalty for everyone, to the empire, instead of to a nation. It did not stop the Balkan nations from leaving. The idea reached beyond Europe, too: in the Spanish-ruled Philippines, Filipino writers in the Propaganda Movement of the 1880s and 1890s, José Rizal among them, demanded equal rights with Spaniards, and Spain executed Rizal in 1896, during a revolution against its rule.

Common mistake to avoid

Do not write that nationalism only built countries. The same loyalty joined Italy and Germany together and pulled Greece and Serbia away from the Ottoman Empire. A cause can push in opposite directions.

Use this in your answer
Strongest tool, with its mechanism
Follow an effect forward. The revolutions created the idea that a state belongs to its nation; nationalists then united Italy (1861) and Germany (1871) and pulled Greece (1830) away from the Ottoman Empire.
Best evidence of exclusion or limit
A nation is imagined: people come to believe they belong to it. Many Italians in 1861 did not speak the same Italian, and Ottomanism's promise of equality was often not kept in practice.
Comparison hook
Against revolutionary France in 1793: France called on loyalty to the nation; the Ottoman government tried to build loyalty to the empire instead, to hold back nationalism.

Terms to use precisely

Liberalism
In the 1800s, the belief that a constitution and an elected parliament should limit the government and protect individual rights.
Democracy
Rule by the people; in the 1800s, the push to give all men the vote.
Nationalism
Loyalty to a nation, people who share a language, religion, customs and homeland, above a king or an empire.
Ottomanism
The Ottoman government's attempt to give all its peoples one loyalty, to the empire.

Building an Answer That Scores

Each card gathers the evidence for one part of the answer. The College Board asks for causes and effects, so the first two cards are causes and the last two are effects.

Causation

A grievance, the words and a chance

British taxes such as the Stamp Act (1765), French privilege and bread prices (1788 to 1789), slavery in Saint-Domingue, and creoles kept out of high office were the grievances. The Declaration of Independence (1776), the Declaration of the Rights of Man (1789) and Bolívar's Letter from Jamaica (1815) were the words. French help (1778), a bankrupt king (1788), a France in revolution (1791) and Napoleon's invasion of Spain (1808) were the chances.

Causation

Each revolution helped cause the next

France's spending on the American war left it nearly bankrupt by 1788; free people of color in Saint-Domingue claimed France's 1789 declaration; Napoleon's invasion of Spain in 1808 left Spanish America with no king; Haiti armed Bolívar in 1816. The College Board calls the American Revolution "a model and inspiration" for the revolutions that followed.

Effects

New states and new ideas about government

The United States (1783, a republic under the 1787 constitution), Haiti (1804) and the independent countries of Spanish America (by 1826) were new states. Republics, written constitutions, liberalism and democracy were new ideas, which set off new revolutions in Europe in 1830 and 1848. Many things stayed: slavery in the United States and much of Latin America, and creole elites at the top.

Effects

The nation, an effect that became a cause

Evidence to sort: the levée en masse (1793); the Frankfurt Parliament (1848 to 1849); Bismarck's "iron and blood" (1862); the Kingdom of Italy (1861) and Rome (1870); the German Empire (1871); the London protocol on Greece (1830); the Treaty of Berlin (1878); the Ottoman edict of 1856 and the citizenship law of 1869. Decide which show nationalism joining states, which show it pulling peoples from an empire, and which show a government using or answering it.
