How to Use This

This chapter has two halves and they do different work. Sections 01 to 06 are the regional baseline, each on the same four parts, ending with the Foundations theme that best explains that region and an argument for why. Sections 07 to 09 are the analytical half: the five thinking skills, then why the course starts where it does, then an SAQ paragraph built one sentence at a time.

  1. The snapshot: where the region actually stood around 1200, dated honestly, including what was already declining.
  2. What holds it up: the institutions, geography or trade that explain the snapshot rather than just describe it.
  3. The pressure on it: the strains already visible at c.1200, which is what makes this a moment rather than a photograph.
  4. Which theme explains it: geography, belief systems, states and power, or trade networks, with the case for choosing that one. This part answers your first reading question directly.
  5. Section 07 defines the five thinking skills, points to where each one already appeared in Foundations 1 to 4, and names the way each is usually done badly.
  6. Section 08 asks why the course starts at c.1200 at all, which is the periodization question and a useful thing to have an opinion about.
  7. Section 09 writes an SAQ paragraph in front of you, sentence by sentence, and then shows the weak version of the same paragraph so you can see the difference.
  8. The closing takes your three reading questions in turn, including the Africa and Americas comparison, which is the one most easily got wrong.
01

Song China

Southern Song, capital at Lin’an  ·  East Asia

Song China at c.1200 is the most commercially sophisticated economy on earth and one of the most militarily vulnerable states in this chapter. Holding both facts at once is the whole skill, because a student who remembers only the prosperity cannot explain anything that happens next.

The snapshot

The dynasty has been ruling from the south since 1127, when Jurchen forces took the northern capital and carried off the emperor. What remains is the Southern Song, governing from Lin'an, modern Hangzhou, a city of perhaps a million or more people and among the largest in the world. The Jurchen Jin hold the north, which means the overland routes to Central Asia run through somebody else's territory.

The economy is extraordinary by any contemporary measure. Paper money circulates, having begun with merchant notes in Sichuan before the state took over issue in the eleventh century. Printing is widespread, including movable type from the eleventh century, though woodblock remained the standard method. Iron output, coal use, canal shipping and urban markets are all at levels no other economy of the period approaches. Zhu Xi, who dies in 1200, has just completed the Neo-Confucian synthesis that will define the examination curriculum for centuries.

And on the northern steppe, Temujin is finishing the unification of the Mongol tribes. He will take the title Genghis Khan in 1206. Nothing in the Song snapshot suggests what that is about to mean, which is itself a lesson about reading a moment in time.

What holds it up

Three mechanisms, and each one is traceable to an earlier Foundations day. Agriculture first: the Yangtze region is the most productive wet-rice zone in the world, and the fast-ripening Champa rice promoted by Song officials from the early eleventh century allowed double-cropping, which raised the food ceiling and freed labour for everything else. That is Foundations 1 and Foundations 4 combining in one field.

Revenue second. Losing the north cost the Song their overland trade access, and the state responded by orienting seaward. Maritime customs offices at ports such as Quanzhou taxed the Indian Ocean trade, and that revenue funded a state that could not out-fight its northern neighbours and therefore had to out-pay them, including through negotiated tribute payments to keep the peace.

Personnel third. The examination system, expanded enormously under the Song and made cheaper to prepare for by printing, staffed the bureaucracy with men selected on a Confucian canon rather than born into office. It gave the dynasty a governing class loyal to the throne rather than to regional families, and it gave every official the same vocabulary for duty.

The pressure on it

The Song could not solve their military problem, and the reason is partly geographic. The pastures that produced cavalry horses lay in the north, which the Jin now held, so the wealthiest state in the world could not field the arm of war that mattered most on its own frontier. It compensated with fortifications, crossbows, a substantial navy and gunpowder weapons, and with money: negotiated payments to northern neighbours bought years of peace at a price the Song economy could absorb more easily than a war.

That arrangement had worked for over a century by c.1200 and it is easy to read as weakness. It was closer to a policy, and a rational one for a state that could out-earn any opponent and out-fight none of them. What it could not survive was an opponent uninterested in being paid, which is what was assembling on the steppe while Zhu Xi finished his commentaries.

Which theme explains it

Trade networks, with geography underneath. The case for it is that the distinctive features of Song China at c.1200 are all commercial: paper money, credit, urban scale, and a state financed substantially by taxing exchange rather than only by taxing land. Those follow from a maritime reorientation forced by the loss of the north and made possible by the Indian Ocean system Foundations 4 describes.

The case for choosing geography instead is also strong, and either choice can score if you argue it: the Yangtze Delta's rice made the surplus, and the loss of the north was itself a geographic and military fact. What will not score is naming a theme and moving on. Name it, then explain the connection in the next sentence, which is the difference between contextualization and introduction.

Common mistake to avoid

Do not write that Song China had "the civil service exam" as though it were new, and do not credit it to Confucius or the Han. The examination system developed under the Sui and Tang; the Song expanded it dramatically and made it the ordinary route into office. Similarly, do not write that the Song "invented paper money" without qualification: the notes began as private merchant instruments in Sichuan and the state took over issue afterwards. Both corrections cost one clause and both are the kind of precision an examiner notices.

02

Dar al-Islam

Iberia to Central Asia  ·  politically fragmented, intellectually connected

Dar al-Islam at c.1200 is the best evidence in this chapter that political unity and cultural coherence are different things. There is no single ruler and there has not been for centuries, and it is nevertheless one connected world of law, language and scholarship.

The snapshot

The Abbasid caliph in Baghdad retains religious prestige and very little else; real power in the central lands has belonged to Turkish dynasties since the eleventh century, and the Seljuk inheritance has fractured into competing successor states. Egypt and Syria are under the Ayyubids founded by Saladin, who died in 1193 after retaking Jerusalem from the crusader kingdoms. The Maghrib and what remains of Muslim Iberia are under the Almohads. Ghurid armies out of Afghanistan have defeated their opponents in northern India and are about to establish a sultanate at Delhi.

Intellectually the same decades are a peak. Ibn Rushd, known in Latin Europe as Averroes, dies in 1198 having written the commentaries on Aristotle that Latin scholars will spend the next century arguing about. Maimonides, a Jewish philosopher and physician writing in Arabic, dies in Cairo in 1204. That one sentence is worth pausing on: the most important Jewish philosopher of the period wrote in the language of Islamic scholarship, which tells you what kind of world this was.

What holds it up

The institution to name is the waqf, the permanent endowment. A person could dedicate the revenue of a property irrevocably to a school, hospital, fountain or mosque, and because the endowment was legally inalienable it funded that institution regardless of who currently ruled the city. The madrasa network ran on this, which is precisely why scholarship survived, and expanded, through two centuries of political collapse and reconquest. Institutions that do not depend on a treasury do not fall when a dynasty does.

Around it, three connective tissues. Shared law, so a contract or a marriage or an inheritance was adjudicated on recognizably common principles from Córdoba to Samarkand. Arabic as the language of scholarship and much of commerce, which meant a book written in one city could be read across the whole zone. And the hajj, which assembled people from every corner of that world annually and carried news, students, goods and manuscripts along with them.

Paper underpins all of it, which is the direct link back to Foundations 4. Cheap writing material made large libraries, working bureaucracies, and a book trade possible.

The pressure on it

Three pressures at c.1200, and none of them is the one students expect. Crusader states still hold parts of the Levantine coast, though Saladin has taken Jerusalem back. In Iberia the long Christian advance is grinding southward and the decisive defeat of the Almohads is only a decade away. And political authority everywhere in the central lands rests on Turkish military elites whose successions are contested, which is why the map changes so often.

The catastrophe that most people associate with this world has not happened yet. The Mongol sack of Baghdad, which ends the Abbasid caliphate outright, is more than fifty years in the future. Writing about c.1200 as though that shadow were already falling is a version of the mistake section 07 calls projecting the ending onto the beginning.

Which theme explains it

Belief systems, understood institutionally rather than devotionally. The claim to make is that Islam in this period functioned as infrastructure: it supplied the commercial law that made long-distance trade enforceable, the endowment mechanism that funded schooling and medicine outside the state, and the annual gathering that kept a fragmented world in contact. That is why the political map at c.1200 is a poor guide to how connected this world was.

Common mistake to avoid

Do not describe Dar al-Islam at c.1200 as being in decline because it is politically fragmented, and do not treat "the Islamic Golden Age" as something that ended when the Abbasids lost power. Ibn Rushd and Maimonides are writing in the 1190s, and the madrasa network is expanding. The accurate and more interesting claim is that intellectual life continued through fragmentation because its funding was endowment-based rather than political. That is a causal argument, and it is available to you only if you keep the two maps, political and institutional, separate.

03

Africa

Swahili coast, the Sahel, the Zimbabwe plateau, Ethiopia  ·  c.1200

Africa at c.1200 is best described as selectively and profoundly connected. Different regions plugged into different networks by different routes, and the wealth of the trading states came from controlling exchange rather than from producing what was exchanged.

The snapshot

On the east coast, the Swahili city-states, among them Kilwa, Mombasa and Mogadishu, are Indian Ocean ports: Muslim, Swahili-speaking, stone-built, exporting gold and ivory from the interior and importing Chinese porcelain, Persian glass and Indian cloth. Kilwa is rising toward the dominance of the southern trade that its later monuments record.

Inland from the Mozambique coast, on the Zimbabwe plateau, the great stone enclosures of Great Zimbabwe are being built by a state whose wealth rests on cattle, on gold and on the route down to the coast. In West Africa, Ghana's power is gone, Mali's has not yet arrived, and the Sahel is in the unsettled interval between them, with the Sosso dominant and Sundiata's founding of Mali still a generation ahead. Around Lake Chad, Kanem is a substantial state on the eastern trans-Saharan routes.

In the northeast, Christian Ethiopia under the Zagwe dynasty is cutting the rock-hewn churches at Lalibela, traditionally dated to about this moment. Egypt and the Maghrib belong to the story in section 02.

What holds it up

Two separate networks, and keeping them separate is what makes an answer precise. The Indian Ocean connects the east coast, on the monsoon schedule described in Foundations 4, which is why Swahili ports face outward and why their elites were Muslim: conversion carried commercial advantage in a network whose law and language were largely Islamic. The trans-Saharan routes connect the west and the centre by camel caravan, which is why the West African states taxed the desert's southern end.

In both cases the mechanism is control of exchange rather than production. Kilwa did not mine gold; it commanded the point where interior gold met the ocean. Ghana had not owned the goldfields either. That is one of the most transferable ideas in this course, and it explains as much about Malacca and Samarkand as it does about Africa.

How we know, and how much to trust it

Most written accounts of West Africa in this period come from outsiders writing in Arabic, often at a distance: al-Bakri described Ghana's capital in 1068 without going there. For the interior states, archaeology and oral tradition carry more of the weight, and both come with their own conventions. Great Zimbabwe is the sharpest case of what happens when evidence is read through prejudice: European commentators in the colonial period insisted the stonework must have been built by outsiders, a claim archaeology has thoroughly refuted and which survived as long as it did because of who was making it. Cite the site with confidence, and cite the controversy as an example of how the identity of the interpreter shapes the interpretation.

The pressure on it

The vulnerability common to all these states is that their wealth came from a route rather than from a resource they held. Ghana had already demonstrated what that means: once the political and commercial conditions of the western Sahara shifted, an empire built on levying an exchange lost its basis, and the century around 1200 is the unsettled interval before Mali reassembles the same business on a larger scale.

On the east coast the risk ran the other way. Swahili ports depended on interior producers they did not govern for the gold and ivory that made them worth visiting, and on a monsoon network whose demand was set thousands of kilometres away. Prosperity that depends on a route and on somebody else's demand is prosperity with two failure points, and naming them is what separates an explanation from a description.

Which theme explains it

Trade networks, decisively. The condition of every African polity in this snapshot follows from which network it touched and where it sat on it. The follow-up that makes an answer strong is to name the second-order effect: the trade brought Islam to the Sahel and the Swahili coast, which brought literacy, written law and a place in a wider commercial community, which then reinforced the trade. Belief systems and trade networks are not competing explanations here. One arrived on the other.

04

South and Southeast Asia

The Ghurid conquests, Angkor at its height, the straits  ·  c.1200

This is the region where all four Foundations themes are visible in one frame: a belief system supplying kingship, a trade chokepoint supplying revenue, a state formed by conquest from outside, and monsoon geography deciding what any of them could do.

The snapshot

In northern India, Ghurid forces from Afghanistan defeat the Rajput coalition in the early 1190s and their commanders establish a sultanate at Delhi in 1206. This is the beginning of sustained Muslim political authority in the subcontinent, ruling over a population that remains overwhelmingly non-Muslim, which will be a defining tension of Unit 1. In the south the Chola dynasty, whose naval reach had extended across the Bay of Bengal in the eleventh century, is past its peak.

In mainland Southeast Asia the Khmer empire is at its height. Angkor Wat, completed around 1150 as a Hindu temple identifying Suryavarman II with Vishnu, is joined under Jayavarman VII, ruling from about 1181, by the new capital at Angkor Thom and a state turn toward Mahayana Buddhism. In the islands and straits, Srivijaya's old dominance has weakened, and the contest for the passage between the Indian Ocean and the South China Sea continues without a single master.

What holds it up

For Angkor, water and rice. The Khmer state's capacity rested on managing a monsoon that delivers most of the year's rain in a few months, through reservoirs and channels that supported dense population and the labour to build on the scale the temples record. Whether those works were primarily agricultural, ritual or both is debated, but the connection between water control and state power is not.

For the straits states, position. Whoever held the passage taxed the traffic between the two halves of the Indian Ocean network, and the wealth funded courts and religious institutions out of all proportion to local production.

For the Delhi Sultanate, a military and administrative apparatus imported from the Persianate world and imposed on an existing agrarian order, with the practical problem all conquest states face: a small ruling group governing a large population that does not share its religion or language. Foundations 3 gives you the vocabulary for how such states manage that, and it is worth asking which of Persia's or Rome's solutions this one resembles.

The pressure on it

Angkor's water works needed continuous maintenance, and a state that mobilizes enormous labour for temples and reservoirs is committing itself to keeping that mobilization going. Historians debate how much later Angkor was undone by hydraulic failure, by climate swings between drought and flood, by war, or by a shift of gravity toward maritime trade on the coast. What is not in doubt is that the system had a running cost.

For the new sultanate at Delhi the pressure is the one every conquest state in Foundations 3 faced: a small ruling group, foreign in language and religion, governing a large agrarian population through local intermediaries it did not fully control. Persia bought quiet with autonomy and Rome bought loyalty with citizenship. Which of those routes a Muslim state in a mostly non-Muslim subcontinent could take is a genuinely open question, and Unit 1 is partly the answer to it.

Which theme explains it

Belief systems, if you take Angkor, and the reason is that Hindu and then Buddhist cosmology is doing political work here, not just religious work. A Khmer king identified with a deity gets an argument for his own authority and a reason for tens of thousands of people to build his temple. Foundations 2 explains the mechanism: Hindu kingship traveled to Southeast Asian courts as a legitimacy technology adopted by rulers, without the caste order that structured society in South Asia.

Trade networks, if you take the straits, for the reasons in section 03 and Foundations 4. Either choice is defensible. What is not defensible is choosing one theme and then writing evidence that supports the other, which is the most common way this question goes wrong.

05

The Americas

Cahokia past its peak, Chan Chan rising, no Aztec or Inca yet  ·  c.1200

The Americas at c.1200 are isolated from Afro-Eurasia and densely connected internally, and the entire value of this section is in keeping those two statements from collapsing into each other.

The snapshot

In the Mississippi valley, Cahokia was the largest settlement north of Mexico, with population estimates commonly in the range of ten to twenty thousand at its height around the eleventh and twelfth centuries, surrounded by enormous earthen mounds. By c.1200 it is declining, and it will be largely abandoned within roughly a century and a half. In the southwest, the great houses of Chaco Canyon have already been left behind by about 1150.

In Mesoamerica, the Toltec centre at Tula has collapsed around the middle of the twelfth century, and the region is in a period of competing city-states out of which the Aztec Triple Alliance will eventually form in 1428. In the Andes, the earlier Wari and Tiwanaku states are long gone, the Chimú kingdom is building its capital at Chan Chan on the north coast, and the Inca are a small polity around Cusco with imperial expansion still more than two centuries away.

Read that list again and notice its shape. Three of the four best-known American sites in this paragraph are past their peak, and the two empires everybody has heard of do not exist yet. This is the single most useful thing Foundations 5 can teach about periodization.

What holds it up, and what is missing

Long-distance exchange is documented and extensive. Marine shell from the Gulf coast, copper from the Great Lakes region and mica from the Appalachians all reach Mississippian sites, which means goods crossed most of a continent. Mesoamerican obsidian, cacao, feathers and jade moved through established trade circuits, and Andean societies moved goods between coast, highland and forest by design. This is not a world of isolated villages.

What is missing is specific and consequential, and naming it precisely is the mark of a good answer. There are no horses and no cattle, so no draft animals for heavy haulage and no cavalry; llamas and alpacas in the Andes carry loads but cannot pull a plough or a wagon. Wheels appear on Mesoamerican figurines and are not used for transport, which makes sense in a world with nothing to pull the cart. There is no Afro-Eurasian disease pool, because the herd animals whose pathogens crossed into humans there are absent, which means American populations have no exposure and no acquired resistance to the diseases that will arrive after 1492.

Common mistake to avoid

Isolation is not backwardness, and the two must not be run together. The Americas were isolated from Afro-Eurasia, and that isolation is a geographic fact about oceans, not a judgment about the societies. Within the hemisphere there were monumental cities, engineered agriculture including terracing, raised fields and irrigation, mathematics and astronomy, and trade over thousands of kilometres. Write "isolated from Afro-Eurasian networks" rather than "isolated," and the sentence becomes both accurate and safe. The consequences of that isolation, above all in 1492, were catastrophic, and they were consequences of separation rather than of any deficiency in what had been built.

The pressure on it

The pressures here are internal and environmental, which is exactly what makes this region a useful control case. Cahokia's decline has been attributed to some combination of climate shifts and flooding, resource exhaustion around a large concentrated population, and political fragmentation, and the argument is unsettled. The abandonment of Chaco Canyon is associated with prolonged drought. In Mesoamerica the fall of Tula left competition rather than a successor.

No outside power is pressing on any of it. Everything happening in this hemisphere at c.1200 is happening for reasons internal to it, which is precisely the condition that ends in 1492. Holding that thought is what stops the Americas from being narrated as a place waiting for history to arrive.

Which theme explains it

Geography, and it is the clearest theme match in the chapter. Two oceans separated the hemisphere from Afro-Eurasian exchange, and the Americas' own north to south axis runs across climate zones rather than along them, which slows the spread of crops and domesticated animals compared with the long east to west corridor of Eurasia. The absence of large domesticable animals is likewise a fact about which species happened to live where.

That argument is influential and it is also contested in its strong form, so the version to write is careful: geographic separation from Afro-Eurasia is the decisive condition, and it explains an absence of exchange rather than any absence of achievement.

Europe

Universities founded, fields reorganized, still peripheral  ·  c.1200

Latin Europe at c.1200 is a secondary region by almost every measure this chapter uses, and it is also in the middle of the agricultural and institutional changes that will matter later. Both halves have to be said, because saying only the first is inaccurate and saying only the second is the whole error this course exists to correct.

The snapshot

Political authority is fragmented among kingdoms, duchies, counties and towns, with the papacy at the height of its claims under Innocent III, elected in 1198. Cities are small by the standards of section 01: the largest in Latin Europe number in the tens of thousands where Lin'an numbers in the hundreds of thousands. The universities are appearing, at Bologna, Paris and Oxford, as guilds of masters and students rather than as buildings. The crusades have been running for a century, and in 1204 the Fourth Crusade will sack Constantinople, a Christian city, which is worth remembering whenever the crusades are described as a straightforward religious conflict.

The intellectual current runs inward. Aristotle is arriving in Latin translation, much of it through Iberia and Sicily, together with Ibn Rushd's commentaries and Arabic mathematics and medicine. Europe at c.1200 is a net importer of ideas, and the universities are, among other things, the institutions built to digest what is arriving.

What holds it up

Underneath the politics is an agricultural reorganization several centuries in the making: the heavy plough that could work the wet clay soils of the north, the horse collar and horseshoe that made horses usable for ploughing, and three-field rotation, which left a third rather than a half of the land fallow and added a spring crop. More food supported more people, and more people supported towns.

Alongside it, mechanical power on a scale worth noting. The Domesday survey of England in 1086 records several thousand water mills for a population of perhaps a couple of million, which is a striking density of installed machinery, and windmills spread in the following century. Mills ground grain, fulled cloth and drove hammers.

And commerce is reviving along two axes: the Italian maritime cities trading into the eastern Mediterranean and the Champagne fairs linking northern cloth production to southern goods, with the credit and partnership instruments that trade required.

The pressure on it

The most revealing pressure at c.1200 is internal to Christendom. In 1204 a crusade aimed at Egypt sacked Constantinople instead, installed a Latin regime there, and did lasting damage to the Byzantine state and to relations between the two churches. A movement that justified itself as a war for the faith had just destroyed the richest Christian city in the world, which is worth remembering whenever a source describes a conflict purely in terms of belief.

Underneath, population has been rising for two centuries and good land is running short, which pushes settlement onto marginal ground. That works while the weather cooperates. It stops working in the fourteenth century, when famine and then plague cut the population sharply, and a student who knows the c.1200 baseline can explain why that hit so hard instead of merely reporting that it did.

Which theme explains it

Geography, if you want the underlying condition: heavy northern soils that became productive only with a specific plough, rivers and coasts that made internal transport cheap, and a position on the receiving end of Mediterranean exchange. Belief systems, if you want the institutional story: the church supplied the schools, the law, the literate personnel and the network that held a fragmented region together, and the universities grew out of cathedral schools.

Common mistake to avoid

Two errors, opposite in direction. The first is to write about Europe at c.1200 as if its later dominance were already visible; it is not, and an answer that treats 1200 as the start of a European rise is projecting the ending onto the beginning. The second is to overcorrect into calling this a dark age of stagnation, which the ploughs, the mills, the fairs and the universities all contradict. The accurate sentence holds both: peripheral in wealth, population and scholarship relative to Song China and Dar al-Islam, and simultaneously in the middle of changes whose consequences arrive later. Getting that balance right is itself the periodization skill from section 07.

Use this in your answer
Strongest tool, with its mechanism
The heavy plough with three-field rotation. The mechanism is that a wheeled plough with a mouldboard could turn the heavy wet soils of northern Europe that a lighter plough merely scratched, and rotating three fields instead of two cut fallow land from a half to a third while adding a spring crop, so output per farmer rose and supported the towns, fairs and universities that follow.
Best evidence of exclusion or limit
Peripheral by almost every measure at c.1200, and importing its philosophy through Arabic translations. Also worth citing: the Fourth Crusade sacking Constantinople in 1204.
Comparison hook
Against Dar al-Islam on the direction of intellectual traffic in 1200, which runs from the Islamic world into Latin Europe and not the other way. Against Song China on urban scale, which is the fastest way to show that the 1200 ranking is not the 1900 ranking.

Terms to use precisely

Three-field rotation
A system leaving one field in three fallow while adding a spring crop, raising output over the older two-field method.
Heavy plough
A wheeled, mouldboard plough able to turn the wet clay soils of northern Europe.
University
A guild of masters and students, appearing at Bologna, Paris and Oxford, and the institution built to absorb newly arriving texts.
Champagne fairs
The cycle of markets linking northern European cloth to Mediterranean goods, with the credit instruments that came with them.
Fourth Crusade
The expedition that sacked Constantinople in 1204, a Christian army taking a Christian capital.

The Five Moves

Contextualization, causation, continuity and change, comparison, argumentation

The five AP historical thinking skills are not vocabulary to memorize. They are five things a historian does to a piece of evidence, and you have already done all five in Foundations 1 to 4 without them being named. Each part below defines the move, points to where you already made it, and names the way it is usually done badly.

Contextualization

The move: place the thing you are explaining inside the conditions that shaped it, and state the connection. Contextualization is a zoom out followed by a link back, and the link is the part that earns the point.

Where you already did it: Foundations 1. You cannot explain Song prosperity without the Yangtze Delta's wet-rice capacity, and section 01 above does exactly that move in two sentences.

The failure mode: writing an introduction and calling it context. "Song China was a dynasty in East Asia during a period of Confucian tradition" names a background and connects nothing. "Driven south in 1127, the Song court found itself governing the most productive rice region in the world, and taxed the maritime trade that its loss of the north had made essential" names conditions and explains what they produced.

Causation

The move: explain why something happened by tracing the mechanism from cause to effect. A cause you have named but not traced is an assertion.

Where you already did it: Foundations 3, in every "how did this tool of rule actually work" question, and Foundations 4, in the monsoon and the camel.

The failure mode: stopping at the feature. "The monsoon winds caused Indian Ocean trade" is a feature. "The wind reverses in June and again in November, so a round trip took most of a year and merchants overwintered in foreign ports, which produced permanent diaspora communities in every major harbour" is a mechanism. Test yourself with the word because: if the sentence after it repeats the cause instead of explaining how it operated, you have not finished.

Continuity and change over time

The move: identify what changed and what stayed the same across a period, which requires a baseline and an endpoint. Foundations exists to give you the baseline. Everything in sections 01 to 06 is what the world looked like before the story starts.

Where you already did it: Foundations 2, in syncretism, which is precisely a question of what continued and what changed when a tradition arrived somewhere new.

The failure mode: assuming change means improvement. At c.1200 Cahokia is shrinking, Chaco is abandoned, Ghana has fallen and the Abbasid caliph has not held real power in two centuries, while the richest economy is Chinese and the most advanced mathematics is being written in Arabic. Describe the direction of change from evidence, not from where you know the story ends.

Comparison

The move: choose one analytical category, put two cases inside it, and explain a similarity or difference with evidence on both sides. The category is what turns two descriptions into a comparison.

Where you already did it: Foundations 3, in the four state pairings, and Foundations 2, in comparing how two belief systems defined membership.

The failure mode: the parallel list. Writing a paragraph about Kilwa and then a paragraph about Cahokia is not comparison, even if both paragraphs are accurate. Name the category first, in the sentence itself: "Both Kilwa and Cahokia sat at the centre of long-distance exchange, but the networks they anchored differed in reach," and the rest of the paragraph has somewhere to go.

Argumentation

The move: make a defensible claim, support it with specific evidence, and explain the reasoning that links them. Defensible means someone could reasonably disagree, which is what separates a claim from a summary.

Where you already did it: every checkpoint in Foundations, and every "evaluate this statement" question in the readings.

The failure mode: answering the topic instead of the question. If the prompt asks you to evaluate whether belief systems mattered more than states, a paragraph describing belief systems has not taken a position. The first sentence should be one you could argue against.

Use this in your answer
Strongest tool, with its mechanism
The five moves as verbs rather than labels. Contextualization links a condition to an outcome; causation traces a mechanism; continuity and change measures against a baseline; comparison puts two cases inside one category; argumentation states a position and defends it. A response that performs three of them well beats one that names all five.
Best evidence of exclusion or limit
Each move has a signature failure: an introduction posing as context, a feature posing as a cause, progress posing as change, a parallel list posing as a comparison, and a summary posing as an argument.
Comparison hook
Set section 07 beside section 09. This one defines the moves; that one shows all of them working inside a single paragraph, which is what an SAQ actually asks for.

Terms to use precisely

Contextualization
Placing a development inside the conditions that shaped it and stating the connection.
Causation
Explaining why something happened by tracing the mechanism from cause to effect.
Continuity and change over time
Identifying what changed and what persisted, measured against a baseline.
Comparison
Explaining similarity or difference between two cases inside one named analytical category.
Argumentation
A defensible claim, specific evidence, and explicit reasoning connecting the two.

Why the Course Starts Here

c.1200 as a choice  ·  the sixth move

A period boundary is an argument someone made, not an event that happened. Nobody alive in 1200 noticed a new era beginning, and understanding why this course starts where it does is worth more than any single fact in the six snapshots above.

What a period boundary actually is

Periodization is the practice of cutting continuous time into named stretches. It is unavoidable, because a history that refuses to divide anything cannot be organized or taught, and it is always an argument, because the moment you choose says what you think the story is about. A course that began in 1492 would be making a claim about European expansion. A course that began in 1750 would be making one about industry. This one begins around 1200, and that is a claim too.

The c. in c.1200 is doing real work and is not decoration. It stands for circa, meaning approximately, and it is there because the boundary is a convenience rather than a date on which anything occurred. Angkor Wat was finished around 1150, the Delhi Sultanate was founded in 1206, Zhu Xi died in 1200 and Genghis Khan took his title in 1206. The line is drawn through a cluster, not at a moment.

Common mistake to avoid

Never write as though something happened in 1200 to start the period. There was no event. Students who treat the boundary as an occurrence end up writing sentences like "in 1200 the world became interconnected," which is false in both directions: Afro-Eurasia had been connected for well over a thousand years, and the Americas were not connected to it in 1200 or for three centuries afterwards. The boundary marks where this course starts looking, and nothing more.

The case for c.1200

Read the six snapshots as an argument and the choice makes sense. Starting here says that the history of the modern world begins with an Afro-Eurasia already densely connected by trade, law and religion, with its wealthiest and most sophisticated societies in China and the Islamic world, and with Latin Europe as a secondary region. Every later question about European dominance then has to be answered rather than assumed, because the starting picture does not contain it.

The choice also sits just before a genuine transformation. Within a few decades of 1200 the Mongol conquests will link Eurasia under a small number of connected regimes, raising the volume and speed of movement across routes that already existed. Beginning slightly before that gives you a baseline against which the change can be measured, which is precisely what continuity and change over time requires and precisely what this chapter is for.

The case against it, and what it hides

Every boundary conceals the continuities that run straight through it, and a strong student can name the ones this boundary hides. The Indian Ocean network was already routine when a Greek merchant wrote the Periplus more than a thousand years earlier. The Silk Roads were roughly thirteen centuries old. Every belief system in Foundations 2 predates the line, some by two millennia. The states in Foundations 3 are older still, and their answers to the problems of rule were being copied long after the empires themselves were gone.

The boundary also fits some regions far better than others. It is a reasonable hinge for Afro-Eurasia. It means very little in the Americas, where the significant discontinuities fall at different moments entirely, and where the change that does reorganize everything arrives in 1492 from outside. Whenever a periodization fits one region and is imposed on another, ask whose history set the frame. That question is not a complaint about the course. It is one of the most reliable ways to get an interesting sentence into an argument.

How to use this in an answer

Watch for the frame in the prompt. "Before c.1200," "in the period 1200 to 1450" and "over the course of the period" are all instructions about the window you are being asked to look through, and evidence from outside it will not count for you no matter how good it is. Check the window before you choose your examples, which is a mechanical habit worth building now.

Then, when a question invites it, say something about the frame itself. Historical significance is a judgment rather than a property: an event matters because of what it made possible or prevented, and for whom. A sentence such as "the c.1200 boundary captures a moment when Afro-Eurasian exchange was dense but not yet politically consolidated, which is what makes the Mongol century legible as an intensification rather than an origin" is doing exactly what the top of the rubric asks for, and it costs one sentence.

Use this in your answer
Strongest tool, with its mechanism
Periodization as an argument. The mechanism is that choosing a start date determines which developments count as background and which count as change, so a course beginning at c.1200 makes Afro-Eurasian interconnection the given condition and European dominance the thing requiring explanation, where a course beginning in 1492 would have reversed both.
Best evidence of exclusion or limit
Every boundary hides continuity. The Indian Ocean network, the Silk Roads and all seven traditions in Foundations 2 run straight through this one, and the line fits Afro-Eurasia far better than it fits the Americas.
Comparison hook
Against the dating conventions in Foundations 3, which used dynastic dates such as 206 BCE and 476 CE. Those are equally chosen: a dynasty falling is an event, but treating it as the end of an era is a judgment about what mattered, and the satrapy system outliving the Achaemenids is the standing counter-example.

Terms to use precisely

Periodization
Dividing continuous time into named stretches; always an argument about what the story is about.
Circa
Approximately, abbreviated c.; a signal that the date is an estimate or a convenience rather than a record.
Historical significance
A judgment that something mattered because of what it enabled or prevented, and for whom, rather than a property it simply has.
Baseline
The established starting condition against which later continuity and change is measured.

Building the Paragraph

Claim, evidence, reasoning, context  ·  one paragraph, four jobs

An SAQ paragraph is short, and every sentence in it has a job. This section builds one in front of you from the material in this chapter, then shows the weak version of the same paragraph so the difference is visible rather than described.

The four jobs

Sentence one, the claim. Answer the question directly and take a position someone could dispute. Not "there were many important developments in Song China," which nobody would argue with and which answers nothing.

Sentences two and three, the evidence. Named institutions, people, places, dates or practices. "Trade was important" is not evidence. "Maritime customs revenue collected at Quanzhou" is.

Sentence four, the reasoning. Explain why the evidence supports the claim, using because, which meant, or as a result. This is the sentence most responses leave out, and it is usually the one carrying the point.

Somewhere, one contextualization move. Name the condition that made the whole thing possible and link it. It can be folded into the claim sentence or added at the end.

A worked example

Prompt: explain how one Foundations theme shaped the world at c.1200.

The paragraph. "Trade networks shaped the world at c.1200 more decisively than any single state did, because they determined which societies had access to wealth, ideas and technologies that no local resource could supply. The Southern Song, cut off from overland routes after the loss of the north in 1127, funded a substantial part of their state through maritime customs revenue collected at ports such as Quanzhou, while on the other side of the same ocean the Swahili port of Kilwa grew wealthy by controlling the outlet for gold and ivory from the African interior, and Chinese celadon in Kilwa's archaeology proves the two ends were joined. Neither place produced what made it rich: the Song taxed the passage of goods and Kilwa commanded an exchange point, which means that position within a network, rather than local production, was what converted geography into power. That was possible because the monsoon reversal, blowing from the southwest between June and September and from the northeast between November and March, made the Indian Ocean a predictable annual circuit rather than an obstacle."

What each sentence did. One: a claim you could argue against, since a reader might reasonably say states mattered more. Two: two specific cases with named places and a date, plus a piece of physical evidence. Three: the reasoning, explaining what the two cases have in common and why that supports the claim. Four: the contextualization, naming the underlying condition and connecting it. Four sentences, four jobs.

The weak version of the same paragraph

"Trade networks were very important in the world at c.1200. There was a lot of trade in the Indian Ocean, and China traded a lot of goods like silk and porcelain. Africa also traded gold. Trade networks connected many different places and helped societies grow and develop, which shows how important trade was."

Everything in that paragraph is true, and it would score close to nothing. The claim is not disputable. The evidence names no place, no date and no institution. The final sentence restates the claim instead of reasoning from evidence to it. There is no contextualization. The gap between the two paragraphs is not knowledge, since both writers know that Indian Ocean trade existed. It is specificity and the willingness to take a position.

One habit worth keeping

When you write a number, a date or a quotation, ask where it came from before you commit it to the page. Several figures in this course are genuinely disputed, including the mortality of the Plague of Justinian and the population of Cahokia, and the deep readings hedge them for that reason. Writing "estimates commonly range from ten to twenty thousand" is not weaker than writing a single confident number. It is stronger, because it is the sentence that survives someone checking.

Use this in your answer
Strongest tool, with its mechanism
One job per sentence. Claim that could be disputed, evidence with names and dates, reasoning that explains the link, and one condition named and connected. A four-sentence paragraph that does those four things scores better than a twelve-sentence paragraph that describes.
Best evidence of exclusion or limit
The commonest failure is a final sentence that restates the claim rather than reasoning from the evidence to it. Check that sentence last, every time.
Comparison hook
Set the worked paragraph beside the weak one. Both writers know the same history, and only one of them wrote a claim, named a place and explained a mechanism.

Terms to use precisely

Claim
A defensible position that answers the prompt directly and that someone could reasonably dispute.
Evidence
Specific named institutions, people, places, dates or practices, rather than general description.
Reasoning
The sentence explaining why the evidence supports the claim, usually the one carrying the point.
SAQ
The short answer question: a compact paragraph in which every sentence has a job.

Building an Answer That Scores

Your three reading questions ask for a contextualization, a comparison and an argument. All three have evidence in this chapter, and the second one is the easiest in the course to get subtly wrong, so it gets the longest entry.

Question 1: Contextualization

Pick the region, then earn the theme

Every regional section ends by naming a theme and arguing for it, so use that part as a model rather than as an answer to copy. The scoring shape is two sentences: name the condition, then explain what it produced. For Song China, the loss of the north in 1127 turned a land empire toward the sea and made customs revenue essential. For Dar al-Islam, waqf endowments funded scholarship independently of any treasury, so intellectual life continued through political collapse. For the Americas, separation from Afro-Eurasia meant no draft animals and no shared disease pool. More than one theme is defensible for most regions; what is not defensible is naming one theme and then citing evidence that belongs to another.

Question 2: Comparison

Africa and the Americas, done carefully

A significant similarity: both supported long-distance exchange in high-value goods over thousands of kilometres, moving gold, ivory, shell, copper and obsidian, and in both cases the wealthiest places were those that controlled an exchange point rather than a resource. A significant difference: Africa's networks connected it directly to Afro-Eurasia, through the Indian Ocean on the east coast and the trans-Saharan routes in the west, while the American networks were internal to the hemisphere and joined to nothing outside it. What that reveals: connection is a matter of degree and direction rather than a yes or no, and what a network gives a society is access to what it cannot produce, which in Afro-Eurasia included crops, technologies, religions and, eventually, epidemic disease and some resistance to it. Write "isolated from Afro-Eurasia," never "isolated," and never let isolation stand in for backwardness: Cahokia, Chan Chan and the Mesoamerican city-states were built by societies doing sophisticated things with what their hemisphere contained.

Question 3: Argumentation

Was Afro-Eurasia an interconnected system?

For: Champa rice moved from Vietnam into Chinese fields and raised the population ceiling; paper moved from China through Samarkand and Baghdad into Europe and reorganized administration and scholarship at every stop; plague moved on grain ships and emptied cities; Islam moved with merchants into the Sahel and Southeast Asia and brought written law and literacy; Ibn Rushd was read in Latin within decades. Developments in one region regularly produced effects in others, which is exactly what a system means. Against, or at least as a qualification: connection was uneven and slow, most people never saw a foreign good, no political authority spanned the system, and the whole thing contracted whenever the states along a route failed. And the strongest limit on the claim sits in section 05: the Americas are outside it entirely, so "the world at c.1200 was an interconnected system" is true of Afro-Eurasia and false of the globe. A strong answer states the claim, names the mechanism that carried the connection, and then names its edge.

Category: what the baseline is for

Why these six snapshots are worth memorizing

Every continuity and change question for the rest of the year measures against this moment. When Unit 2 asks what the Mongols changed about Eurasian trade, the answer depends on knowing that the routes were already 1,300 years old and that what the Mongols supplied was political consolidation rather than the network itself. When Unit 4 asks what the Portuguese changed in the Indian Ocean, the answer depends on knowing that a dense, sophisticated commercial system was already running on the monsoon and that they arrived into it rather than opening it. Learn the baseline and half the analytical work of the next eight units is already done.
