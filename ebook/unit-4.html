Topic 4.1

Sailing Away to Get Home

The Indian Ocean had long-established shipping and a wind that reversed on schedule. The Atlantic offered neither, and the technology in this chapter is the answer to that harder problem: how to leave the sight of land for weeks, find a place you have never been, and get back against the wind that brought you.

01

The Problem an Ocean Sets

c. 1420 to 1500  ·  Why the Atlantic is harder

Every technology in this chapter is a solution, so start with what needed solving. The Atlantic is not a bigger Indian Ocean; it is a different kind of problem, and the difference explains why the answers look the way they do.

What the Indian Ocean gave sailors, and the Atlantic did not

The Topic 2.3 chapter sets out the Indian Ocean system. The monsoon reverses twice a year with great reliability, so a captain knew months in advance when he could sail east and when he could come home. Voyages ran between known ports along coasts, with landmarks, pilots and centuries of accumulated route knowledge written into pilot books. Dhows and junks were superbly suited to it, and the junk in particular, with watertight bulkheads and a stern-post rudder, was arguably the most capable vessel afloat.

Now take that captain to the Atlantic. There is no seasonal reversal to carry him home. Beyond the coastal shelf there are almost no landmarks, only a few scattered islands, and past the Canaries there was, for European sailors, no accumulated route knowledge because nobody had come back to write any. The prevailing winds off West Africa blow steadily from the northeast, which is to say they push a ship south and hold it there. And the distances are such that a voyage means weeks out of sight of land with whatever food and water you loaded at the start.

So four problems, and it is worth writing them as questions because each section below answers one: What kind of ship can work against the wind and still carry enough cargo and provisions to matter? How do you know where you are with no coast in view? How do you get home against a wind that blows the wrong way? And how do you know any of this in advance, when nobody has done it before?

Why this is not a story about European cleverness

A caution worth taking early, because it shapes how the whole unit reads. Europeans did not work out the Atlantic first because they were better sailors or better engineers; on the evidence of Topic 2.3 they were neither. Part of the reason is that they were the ones with the problem.

China had the ships, the navigation and the capital to sail as far as East Africa, and the Topic 2.3 chapter explains why the Ming court stopped sending its great treasure fleets after 1433 (Chinese merchants kept trading across Southeast Asia). Indian Ocean merchants already had a working trade system reaching from Africa to China; sailing west into empty ocean solved no problem they had. Europe sat at the far end of that system, and why its rulers wanted to reach Asia by sea is the subject of Topic 4.2.

The honest version of this topic, and the one that will serve you in every later unit, is that borrowed tools, new ships and one genuine discovery about the wind let European sailors cross the Atlantic and come back. Keep that shape and the whole unit stops sounding like a story about who deserved to win.

02

The Ships

c. 1440 to 1650  ·  Caravel, carrack, fluyt

Three ships, three jobs. Confusing them is a common error in this topic, and keeping them straight is the fastest way to sound like you know what you are talking about.

The caravel: the explorer

The caravel was a small Portuguese vessel, on the order of fifty to seventy tons, with a shallow draft and, in its original form, lateen sails, the triangular rig used on the Mediterranean since Roman times and common across the seas of the Islamic world.

Each of those features answers a specific question. The lateen rig lets a ship sail much closer to the wind than a square sail does, which lets it work against the wind along an unknown coast. The shallow draft lets it enter river mouths and coastal shallows, which is where the exploring actually happened. And the small size means a small crew, which means less food and water for a given range and less capital risked on a voyage that might not come back. A caravel is a scouting ship: fast, handy, cheap enough to lose, and not much use for carrying cargo.

Later Portuguese ships combined the rigs, square sails forward for driving power on a long downwind passage and a lateen aft for handling, which is the practical answer to a voyage that has both problems in it.

The carrack: the workhorse

A carrack, the nau of Portuguese and Spanish usage, is a much larger vessel, high-sided, square-rigged on the main masts, with a deep hold. It carried the cargo, the provisions for a long crossing, and the guns.

That last point is one to keep for later. A high-sided ship carrying heavy cannon could fight in a way that most Indian Ocean merchant ships, lightly armed or unarmed, were not built to. What the Portuguese did with that in the Indian Ocean is the subject of the Topic 4.4 chapter.

Columbus's fleet in 1492 illustrates the division of labor neatly: the Santa Maria was a nao, a slower, roomier carrack-type ship, and the Nina and Pinta were caravels. One vessel to carry, two to look.

The fluyt: the profit machine

The Dutch fluyt, developed in the 1590s, belongs in this chapter for a reason students rarely see: it is the ship that shows the technology becoming an economic instrument rather than an exploratory one.

It was designed for one purpose, moving bulk cargo cheaply, and every feature follows. A long, box-like hull maximized capacity. Simple, standardized rigging let it be sailed by a much smaller crew than a comparable ship, which is the single largest cost in shipping. It carried little or no armament, on the calculation that speed and cheapness beat guns on routes patrolled by somebody else's navy. It was built in yards using standardized components and, by the standards of the day, mass-produced.

The result was cheaper freight, mostly in Europe's own bulk trades such as the Baltic: not a better warship, a cheaper truck. How the Dutch turned shipping into commercial power is the subject of the Topic 4.5 chapter.

Common mistake to avoid

Do not write that the caravel crossed the Atlantic and opened the world, full stop. The caravel was an exploring vessel, superb for probing the African coast, and too small to be the backbone of transoceanic commerce. The long-distance trade that followed ran mostly in carracks and galleons; the fluyt later made freight cheap, mostly in Europe's own waters. Naming the right ship for the right job, explorer, armed carrier, cheap freighter, is exactly the specificity the success criteria ask for, and it takes one extra clause.

03

Finding Your Way

c. 1450 to 1760  ·  Compass, astrolabe, latitude, longitude

Half of navigation was solved and half was not, and knowing which half is which explains the shape of every route in this unit.

Direction, and height above the horizon

The magnetic compass, Chinese in origin and in Mediterranean use by the late twelfth century as the Topic 2.5 chapter describes, gives a heading in cloud, at night, and out of sight of everything. It is the precondition for the rest, because a position estimate is worthless if you cannot steer a known course.

Latitude, how far north or south you are, is found by measuring the angle between the horizon and a fixed reference: the Pole Star in the northern hemisphere, or the sun at noon. The instruments are a family. The mariner's astrolabe, a simplified and heavier version of the Islamic astronomical instrument, is hung vertical and sighted along an alidade. The quadrant is a quarter-circle with a plumb line. The cross-staff and the later back-staff let a navigator take the sun's altitude with better accuracy from a moving deck.

The measurement alone is not enough. To turn the sun's noon altitude into a latitude you need to know the sun's declination for that date, which changes through the year, and that means tables. Iberian navigators had them, most famously in the tables of the astronomer Abraham Zacuto, composed in the 1470s and printed in Portugal in 1496. Printing, developed in Europe on paper that had arrived along the chain traced in the Topic 2.5 chapter, meant every pilot could carry the same table, instead of a copy whose errors depended on who had copied it.

The half that was not solved

Longitude, how far east or west you are, has no equivalent trick, because the Earth turns. To know your longitude you must know the time at a reference place and compare it with local noon, and no clock of the period could keep time on a rolling, damp, temperature-swinging ship. The problem was not solved until the marine chronometer of the 1760s, just after the period this unit covers.

So for the whole of Unit 4, a navigator knew his latitude well and his longitude badly, estimating east-west position by dead reckoning: course steered, speed estimated, time elapsed, corrected by guesswork about currents and leeway. Errors accumulated over weeks.

That single limitation shapes the routes. The standard practice, called running down the latitude, was to sail north or south until you reached the latitude of your destination, then turn and sail along it until you hit land. It is slower than a direct course and vastly more reliable, and it is why ships so often made landfall by running along a known latitude. It also explains why islands were so valuable and so often missed, and why a ship could be certain of its distance from the equator and wildly wrong about its distance from home.

How we know: the charts show what sailors trusted

Portolan charts, the Mediterranean sailing charts that predate this period, are covered in radiating rhumb lines, compass roses and coastal detail, and they carry almost nothing about the interior of any landmass and no latitude grid worth the name. That is a picture of what navigation actually was: bearings between known coastal points. Compare a chart from the sixteenth century, with a latitude scale, and you can see the change arrive. When a source tells you what a technology did, a chart made for working sailors tells you what they actually relied on.

04

Borrowed Knowledge, and the Wind

c. 1400 to 1500  ·  What was assembled, and the one insight

Almost nothing in the last two sections was invented in Europe. What was European was the assembly, and one genuine discovery about the Atlantic that no borrowed instrument could have supplied.

The inventory of what was borrowed

Take the package apart and its origins are the whole second success criterion. The magnetic compass is Chinese. The lateen sail was used on the Mediterranean since Roman times and was common across the seas of the Islamic world. The stern-post rudder appears in China centuries before Europe, though whether Europe borrowed it or reinvented it is debated. The astrolabe is a Greek instrument developed to a high art by Islamic astronomers, and the trigonometry and astronomical tables behind its use are largely Islamic and Indian work transmitted through Arabic. Paper, on which the tables were later printed, arrived along the chain traced in the Topic 2.5 chapter; the sea charts themselves were still drawn by hand, usually on vellum; printing itself developed in Europe. Gunpowder, which armed the carracks, is Chinese.

Classical learning came back through the same routes. Ptolemy's Geography, a different book from his astronomy, with its system of latitude and longitude coordinates for mapping, reached Italy from the Byzantine world around 1400 and was printed later in the century. It is worth adding that it also transmitted an error: Ptolemy's estimate of the Earth's circumference was too small, and a chain of underestimates of that kind is part of why Columbus believed Asia lay a manageable distance west.

State this plainly in an essay and it does real analytical work. European oceanic expansion was not a technological leap out of nowhere; Europe sat at the edge of the old trade network described in Unit 2 and used what that network already knew. What Europe supplied was the assembly, new ships, and the knowledge of the Atlantic's winds in the next section. Why Europeans wanted to point the package at the open Atlantic is the subject of Topic 4.2.

The volta do mar, which is the actual breakthrough

Here is the piece that is missing from most accounts and is the best single thing in this chapter. Portuguese ships going down the West African coast had the wind and current behind them, and that is precisely the problem: the same wind is in your face all the way home, and a lateen-rigged caravel beating up a thousand miles of coast against it is a voyage measured in months, if it succeeds.

The solution, worked out over decades of voyages in the fifteenth century, is counterintuitive enough that it deserves the emphasis: to get home, sail away from home. Instead of hugging the coast northward, a returning ship stood out into the open Atlantic on a northwesterly course, sailed for days out of sight of land into what looked like nowhere, and picked up the belt of westerly winds in the higher latitudes that carried it back to Iberia. Historians call the maneuver the volta do mar, the turn of the sea; Portuguese sailors of the time also spoke of the turn from Guinea or from Mina.

Notice what that requires. Confidence that a wind you cannot see exists where you are going. A ship that can stay at sea for weeks. The nerve to sail away from the only land you know. And it grew far more reliable once pilots could read their latitude at sea, with the quadrant from about 1460 and the astrolabe in the 1480s, because then they knew when they had gone far enough north to turn. It is a piece of knowledge about the ocean itself rather than about ships, and it is why the Portuguese, who had spent decades learning the Atlantic wind system in small steps, were the ones who could use it.

Columbus's 1492 route is the same insight turned west. He sailed south to the Canaries first to pick up the northeast trade winds for the crossing, and returned on a more northerly track to catch the westerlies. He used the pattern the Portuguese had learned near Africa, the trades out and the westerlies home, and bet that it held all the way across. When you are asked why oceanic voyaging became possible when it did, the wind system is a better answer than any instrument, and it is the one almost nobody gives.

How we know: the pattern of the voyages is the evidence

You do not have to take a chronicler's word for how the Atlantic wind system was learned. Most fifteenth-century return tracks were never written down, so historians reconstruct how the wind system was learned from scattered evidence: the order in which the Atlantic islands were found and settled, the farthest points each voyage reached, and the sailing directions written down later. Put together, they show each expedition pushing a little further south and the routes home relying more and more on the open ocean. That is a picture of knowledge built up voyage by voyage. Portugal also tried to control who saw its charts and route knowledge, though how strict that control was is debated.

Building an Answer That Scores

Each card is a full comparison: the claim, the specific evidence, and the reason. The first one answers the question this whole unit is built on, and it does it without saying anyone was cleverer than anyone else.

Technology and geography

The Atlantic package answers a harder problem, not a better engineer

Indian Ocean sailing had a monsoon that reversed on schedule, coastal routes with landmarks, and pilot books recording courses and hazards, so its ships were built to carry cargo on routes sailors already knew. The Atlantic offered no seasonal reversal, almost no landmarks past the shelf, and no route knowledge, so European voyaging needed a rig that could work to windward, instruments to fix latitude from the sky, and the volta do mar to find a wind home. The difference exists because of the oceans, not the sailors: China had more capable ships, and the Ming court stopped sending its treasure fleets for reasons of its own (Topic 2.3), and why Europeans kept trying is Topic 4.2.

Diffusion

Nearly every component came from somewhere else

The compass is Chinese, and China had the stern-post rudder centuries earlier; the lateen sail is Mediterranean, used since Roman times and common across the Islamic world's seas; the astrolabe is Greek in origin and Islamic in development, and the trigonometry and tables behind it came through Arabic; paper arrived along the chain traced in Topic 2.5; gunpowder armed the ships. Ptolemy's coordinate mapping came back through Byzantium. What Europe contributed was the assembly, new ships, and the volta do mar. That is the second success criterion answered in one sentence, and it also sets up the honest version of this unit: oceanic expansion was the periphery of the Afro-Eurasian network cashing in the network's own accumulated knowledge.

Constraint

Unsolved longitude explains the shape of the routes

Latitude could be measured accurately with an astrolabe or quadrant plus declination tables; longitude required knowing the time at a reference place, and no clock could keep time at sea until the chronometer of the 1760s, just after the period this unit covers. So navigators ran down the latitude, sailing north or south to a destination's parallel and then along it until landfall, and estimated east-west position by dead reckoning that accumulated error over weeks. The difference between the two halves of the problem is why ships so often ran along a known latitude to make landfall, why provisions ran out on longer-than-expected crossings, and why a captain could be sure of his distance from the equator and badly wrong about his distance from home.

The best single fact

To get home from Africa, they sailed away from Europe

The northeast trades carry a ship down the African coast and then blow in its face the whole way back. The Portuguese answer, learned expedition by expedition across the fifteenth century, was the volta do mar: stand far out into the open Atlantic on a northwesterly course, reach the westerlies of the higher latitudes, and ride them home. Columbus used the same pattern turned west, dropping south to the Canaries for the trades and returning on a northern track. When a prompt asks why transoceanic voyaging became possible when it did, name the wind system before you name a single instrument.

01

Why a State Paid

c. 1415 to 1500  ·  Margin, faith, and a contract

Rulers had three kinds of reasons, and each one only explains something once you say how it worked. Spices were not just valuable: middlemen added to the price at every step. Religion was not just faith: it came with a long war against Muslim states behind it. And glory came with a written contract that promised a share of the profits.

The margin

Pepper grew on the Malabar coast of India and cost little there. By the time it reached a European table it had crossed the Indian Ocean, been sold at a Red Sea or Gulf port, carried overland through Mamluk Egypt or Ottoman territory, taxed at each transfer, sold again to Venetian merchants who held privileged access to those markets, and shipped to European ports. Most of those margins went to Muslim states and merchants, and the last to Venice, and the retail price at the end was a large multiple of the price at the source. One historian's figures (M. N. Pearson): a kilo of pepper that cost 1 or 2 grams of silver where it grew cost 10 to 14 in Alexandria, 14 to 18 in Venice, and 20 to 30 elsewhere in Europe.

That is the economic motive stated properly, and notice what it implies. The prize was not "spices are valuable," it was cutting out the chain. A European power that reached the source by sea would buy at Calicut prices and sell at Lisbon prices, capturing every margin between. It would also, as a bonus its rulers cared about, stop enriching the Muslim states and the Venetian republic that currently collected them.

Historians since A. H. Lybyer in 1915 have made the necessary correction here: the Ottomans did not close the overland routes, spice prices did not jump after 1453, and no blockade forced Europe to sea. The trade continued. What drove the search was the cost and the number of hands it passed through, which is a motive of a completely different kind and a much better one to write.

The crusading inheritance

Portugal's oceanic program did not begin with a caravel. It began in 1415 with the capture of Ceuta, a Muslim port in North Africa, which is a crusading operation in the tradition of the Iberian reconquest. Spain's westward bet comes in a year that makes the same point without comment: 1492 is both the fall of Granada, the last Muslim kingdom in Iberia, and Columbus's first voyage, funded by the monarchs who had just completed that conquest.

Two specific religious motives, beyond a general desire to spread Christianity. First, outflanking the Islamic world: if a Christian power could reach the sources of the spice trade and the West African gold by sea, it would divert wealth away from the states it had spent centuries fighting. Second, the legend of Prester John, a powerful Christian monarch believed to rule somewhere beyond the Islamic lands, whose alliance would let Christendom attack from both sides. The legend was persistent enough that Portuguese expeditions were sent overland to look for him, and it is a real motive in the documents rather than a colorful detail.

The contract, which is the best evidence of all

Why did a crown back this rather than leave it to merchants? A trading voyage to a known port is a risk merchants can judge, as the Topic 2.1 chapter describes. A voyage to find out whether a route exists may bring back nothing at all, may take decades of repeated attempts, and pays off mainly as a route nobody else knows, which is exactly the kind of prize a ruler values and a merchant cannot sell.

So states stepped in, in two ways. Sometimes the crown paid most of the cost itself. Sometimes it licensed a merchant or a captain to explore at his own cost, in return for a share and a claim: Portugal leased the Guinea trade to the merchant Fernão Gomes in 1469 on condition that he explore a hundred leagues of new coast a year, and England's Henry VII let John Cabot sail "at their own proper costs and charges" for a fifth of the gains. Either way the crown paid in the currency it had: monopoly rights, titles, and a share of whatever came back. The agreement Columbus obtained from the Spanish monarchs before sailing, the capitulaciones, promised him the title of Admiral of the Ocean Sea, the governorship of lands he discovered, and a tenth of the wealth obtained from them, with the offices to be hereditary.

How we know: read the contract as a risk assessment

Columbus's terms look extravagant, and one way to read them is as evidence of how unlikely the crown thought success was. You do not promise a tenth of a hemisphere and a hereditary admiralty to someone you expect to succeed; you promise it when you doubt you will ever have to pay. The same logic explains the later fight: once the voyages did produce wealth, the Spanish crown spent decades in litigation with Columbus's heirs trying to claw those terms back. The generosity of a contract before the event, and the reluctance after it, tell you more about how the risk was perceived than any chronicler's account of royal enthusiasm.

02

The Portuguese Program

1415 to 1500  ·  Ceuta to Calicut

Not a voyage but a program: more than seventy years of incremental, state-funded expeditions, each pushing a little further and bringing back route knowledge that, at least under João II from the 1480s, was guarded as a state secret. It is the least dramatic and most instructive story in the unit.

Down the coast, decade by decade

After Ceuta in 1415, Portuguese expeditions worked south along the African coast under royal patronage, most associated with Prince Henry, whose household organized and financed voyages for decades. Progress was slow and psychological as much as technical: Cape Bojador, on the Saharan coast, is traditionally said to have been passed in 1434, after years in which crews feared the sea beyond it.

The pattern repeats: sail further, plant a marker, trade where possible, come home using the volta do mar described in the Topic 4.1 chapter, and record everything. By the 1440s Portuguese ships were trading on the Senegal coast, buying gold that had previously crossed the Sahara by camel, which is the change of route the Topic 2.4 chapter points to at its end, and buying enslaved people, which is where the Atlantic slave trade begins. In 1488 Bartolomeu Dias was blown around the southern tip of Africa and realized the coast now ran northeast: the ocean was connected.

Common mistake to avoid

Henry "the Navigator" did not navigate, and the school of navigation at Sagres, with its academy of cartographers and instrument makers, is largely a later invention. What the evidence supports is a prince who systematically financed voyages, collected the knowledge they brought back, and pursued the enterprise for a mixture of crusading, commercial and dynastic reasons. That is less romantic and more useful, because "a state that funded repeated expeditions and collected what they brought back" is a mechanism, and a school on a cliff is a picture.

Calicut, 1498, and what happened there

Vasco da Gama left Lisbon in 1497, rounded the Cape, worked up the East African coast through the Swahili ports described in the Topic 1.5 chapter, and at Malindi engaged a pilot who knew the Indian Ocean crossing. He reached Calicut in May 1498. The Topic 2.3 chapter describes Calicut: a wealthy, cosmopolitan port under a Hindu ruler, with resident Arab and Gujarati merchant communities and a reputation for protecting foreign traders.

The meeting went badly, and the reason is the most useful fact in this chapter. The trade goods da Gama had brought, coarse cloth, hats, basins, strings of beads, the goods that bought gold and captives on the West African coast, were of no interest whatever in a market that dealt in pepper, fine cotton, gems and porcelain. The gifts were reportedly laughed at, and the Portuguese were unable to trade on the terms they expected.

And yet the voyage was a triumph. Da Gama came home in 1499 having lost two of four ships and, to scurvy, a large share of his men, with a cargo of pepper and spices worth far more than the expedition had cost. Both halves are true simultaneously, and holding them together is the point: Europe had almost nothing Asia wanted, and the margin on what Asia had was so large that it did not yet matter. That single sentence explains the next two centuries, including why the Topic 4.5 chapter is largely about silver, which was the main thing Europeans could offer that Asian markets did want.

Portugal drew the obvious conclusion. If you cannot compete on goods, compete on force: the fleets that followed were armed, and the Topic 4.4 chapter is what they did with that. Cabral, sailing for India in 1500, swung west into the Atlantic, made landfall on the coast of Brazil and claimed it, which is how the Portuguese acquired a vast American territory they had not been looking for.

03

The Spanish Gamble, and the Size of the World

1492 to 1522  ·  Columbus to Elcano

Spain funded a voyage based on a calculation that was wrong in a way that would have killed everyone aboard, and got a hemisphere instead. Thirty years later another Spanish expedition measured the mistake by sailing around the planet.

The miscalculation

Educated Europeans in 1492 knew the Earth was a sphere; that had been standard since antiquity, and the Topic 4.1 chapter notes that Ptolemy's coordinate geography was back in circulation. What was disputed was its size, and Columbus took a chain of the smallest available estimates: an undersized circumference, an overestimate of how far east Asia extended, and a generous reading of the distance to Japan. The result was a westward passage to Asia of a few thousand miles, which a caravel could just about provision for.

The Portuguese crown's advisers, who had spent decades measuring coastlines, rejected his figures, which is why he went to Spain. They were right and he was wrong: the true distance from the Canaries to Asia is vastly greater than his estimate, and had the Americas not been in the way, his ships would almost certainly have run out of food and water long before reaching Asia.

He sailed in August 1492, dropped south to the Canaries to pick up the trade winds as the Topic 4.1 chapter describes, made landfall in the Caribbean in October, and returned on a northern track to catch the westerlies. He made three further voyages, established the first Spanish settlements in the Caribbean, and died in 1506 still maintaining he had reached the outskirts of Asia. The naming of the continents after Amerigo Vespucci, whose published accounts argued that this was a new landmass, is the historical judgment on that point.

The line, and the circumnavigation

Two Christian monarchies now had competing claims, and both looked first to the papacy. Papal bulls in 1493 drew a line favoring Spain; Portugal objected, and the two crowns negotiated the Treaty of Tordesillas in 1494, which moved the line farther west, to 370 leagues west of the Cape Verde islands. Everything discovered west of it went to Spain and everything east to Portugal. That is how Portugal came to hold Brazil, which falls east of the line, and Spain the rest of the Americas.

Then Ferdinand Magellan, a Portuguese captain sailing for Spain, proposed reaching the Spice Islands by going west and around the bottom of the new continents. He left in 1519 with five ships and around two hundred and seventy men, found the strait that carries his name at the tip of South America, and entered the Pacific.

And the Pacific was the discovery. The crossing took months and the ocean was vastly larger than anyone had believed; men died of scurvy and starvation on a passage nobody had provisioned for because nobody knew the scale. Magellan was killed in 1521 in the Philippines, in a fight with the forces of a local ruler who refused to submit. Juan Sebastian Elcano brought one ship, the Victoria, home in 1522 with eighteen of the original crew.

How we know: the expedition came home a day short

Antonio Pigafetta, who kept a journal through the voyage and survived it, recorded that when the Victoria reached the Cape Verde islands the crew's carefully maintained calendar was one day behind the local date. They had kept their reckoning faithfully; sailing westward around the globe had cost them a day. It is the first recorded encounter with the effect that would eventually require an international date line, and it is a lovely piece of evidence, because a discrepancy between two calendars is a measurement of the shape of the world made by men who had no idea they were making it.

The lesson to take from Magellan is not that the Earth is round, which nobody educated doubted, but that it is much bigger than Europe had calculated, and that the Pacific is far wider than anyone in Europe had guessed. That is the correction Columbus's error needed, and only one of the five ships completed the voyage; most of the men who sailed never came home.

04

The Latecomers Go North

1497 to 1650  ·  Passages, fish and plunder

England, France and the Dutch were shut out of the southern routes by a treaty they had no reason to respect and a Portuguese navy they could not yet fight, so they looked for a way around the top of the world. They never found one, and what they found instead paid better.

The search for a passage

The logic is straightforward. If Spain holds the west and Portugal the route around Africa, a latecomer needs a third way to Asia, and the map suggested two: a Northwest Passage over the top of North America, or a Northeast Passage over the top of Russia. Both would also, in theory, be shorter.

England licensed John Cabot to sail west, and in 1497 he reached the coast of North America, probably Newfoundland or Cape Breton. France sent Verrazzano along the North American coast in the 1520s and Cartier up the St Lawrence in the 1530s, looking for the opening. English expeditions under Frobisher and Davis probed the Arctic. Dutch expeditions under Barentsz tried the northeast route and wintered in the Arctic in the 1590s, and in 1609 Henry Hudson, hired by the Dutch East India Company to try that route again, turned west instead and sailed up the river that now carries his name. Every one of these failed at its stated objective, and the reason is worth stating carefully because the shorthand version is wrong. Both passages are real geography and both have since been sailed. What defeated these expeditions was Arctic ice, which in the climate of those centuries and with wooden sailing ships made the routes unusable as dependable commercial highways.

What they found instead

Two things above all, and they redirected the northern powers. Fish. The Grand Banks off Newfoundland held cod in quantities that European fishermen exploited within a few years of Cabot's voyage, and dried salt cod was a staple protein for a continent with religious fasting rules. Unglamorous and immediately profitable, and it put English, French, Basque and Portuguese fishermen across the North Atlantic decades before any colony.

Furs. French and later English traders on the St Lawrence and in the Hudson Bay watershed built an economy on beaver pelts obtained from Indigenous nations, which is a relationship of trade and alliance rather than of conquest, and which shaped North American colonization into something quite different from the Spanish pattern.

Some excluded states also licensed attacks on Spanish shipping; those rivalries are Topic 4.5.

Common mistake to avoid

Do not treat Tordesillas as though it governed the world. It was an agreement between two Catholic monarchies, built on papal grants, and it had no force for any state that refused to accept it: Catholic France, Protestant England and the Dutch Republic after the Reformation, and still less the Ottomans, the Mughals, the Ming, or any of the peoples whose lands it claimed to divide. A French king is said to have asked to see the clause in Adam's will that divided the world between Spain and Portugal. Write it as a settlement between two claimants that shaped where they expanded, and note who ignored it, and you have the treaty and its limits in two sentences.

In the end the English and the Dutch reached Asia by Portugal's own route around Africa, through trading companies founded around 1600; what those companies were is where Topic 4.5 picks up.

Motivation

The motive was the intermediaries, not a blockade

The Ottomans did not close the overland routes and no blockade forced Europe onto the ocean, which means the correct explanation is cost and the number of hands rather than an obstruction, and it is also the stronger one, because it explains why the search continued for seventy years rather than starting suddenly in 1453.

Portugal accumulated; Spain gambled

Portugal ran a program of state-backed expeditions lasting more than seventy years down the African coast, passing Bojador in 1434, rounding the Cape with Dias in 1488, reaching Calicut with da Gama in 1498, and guarding the accumulated charts and wind knowledge closely, above all under João II. Why the Disaster Fell Where It Did

c. 1492 to 1650  ·  Isolation, herds, and immunity

The catastrophe was not caused by anything about the people it fell on. The germs were new to the Americas because of which animals each hemisphere had domesticated and more than ten thousand years of separation, and they killed as many as they did because they arrived together with war, forced labor and hunger. Stating it that way is correct, and it survives scrutiny.

Where crowd diseases come from

Many of the great infectious killers of Afro-Eurasia crossed to humans from animals. Measles is closely related to a cattle disease, influenza circulates among birds, pigs and people, and others have animal reservoirs or close animal relatives. That kind of crossing needs sustained contact between large numbers of people and large numbers of animals, which is what farming villages with cattle, pigs, sheep, goats, horses and chickens provide.

Do not push that further than it goes. New studies of ancient DNA, including the DNA of the germs themselves, have made several of these histories more complicated over the last two decades, and smallpox and tuberculosis no longer trace to a herd animal in any simple way, so "cows gave us this one, pigs gave us that one" is a model to use with care. Animals are a large part of the ecological story rather than the single origin of every great killer.

The second condition is population density. A disease that confers lifelong immunity on survivors burns out in a small community, because it runs out of people who can catch it. Only in a large, connected population can it become endemic, circulating permanently and infecting each new generation of children.

Afro-Eurasia had both conditions for millennia, and the trade networks of Unit 2 stitched its populations together, which is why the Topic 2.6 chapter has a pandemic in it. The result is a population in which most adults had survived several of these diseases in childhood and carried immunity to them.

The Americas had neither condition in the same measure. The domesticated animals were the llama and alpaca in the Andes, the guinea pig, the turkey and the dog, none of them living in the crowded, continuous proximity to humans that European livestock did. So although the Americas had their own diseases, they had not generated the same family of crowd infections, and the populations of the two hemispheres had been effectively separated since the peopling of the Americas.

What "no immunity" actually means

A virgin soil epidemic is an outbreak in a population with few people carrying prior immunity to a pathogen. Immunity to smallpox is acquired rather than inherited: a European adult was resistant because he had survived the disease as a child, not because of anything in his ancestry. Where almost nobody has had it, the pathogen reaches every age group at once instead of only children, which is the part that turns an epidemic into something else.

That is the mechanism to write, and it is about proportions rather than absolutes: not everyone is infected and not everyone who is infected dies, but enough adults are sick at the same time that the society stops working. When every adult is sick simultaneously, nobody plants, nobody harvests, nobody hauls water, nobody nurses the sick, and nobody leads. People die of hunger, thirst and neglect who would have survived the infection itself. Then the next disease arrives, into a population already weakened and disorganized, and after it the next: smallpox, then measles, then influenza, then typhus, in waves across decades.

Some of the killers also needed carriers, and the ships brought those too. Black rats came ashore with the cargo. Malaria, which most scientists now think reached the Americas with Europeans and enslaved Africans, is spread by mosquitoes, and the mosquito that carries yellow fever most likely crossed from West Africa in ships' water casks. The College Board calls these disease vectors, and its word for how they crossed is "unintentional."

Add the conditions colonization created, forced labor, relocation, warfare, disrupted farming, and the deaths add up. Disease is the largest single factor. It was not a natural disaster that happened to strike at the same time as conquest: it traveled along the routes conquest opened, and it struck people whom conquest was already stripping of food, of their own labor and of the order of their lives.

Common mistake to avoid

Never write that Indigenous Americans were biologically weaker or that Europeans were biologically stronger. Immunity to smallpox is acquired by surviving childhood infection, and the difference between the hemispheres is a fact about animal and population ecology and about thousands of years of separation, not about the worth or the constitution of the people. One qualification keeps this accurate rather than merely reassuring: populations can differ in inherited immune variants shaped by long exposure to particular diseases, which is exactly what section 05 describes for malaria in West and Central Africa. That is a different mechanism from surviving smallpox as a child, and neither one ranks anybody. Two checks confirm it: European populations were devastated by the same mechanism by a novel pathogen in the fourteenth century, which the Topic 2.6 chapter describes, and Europeans arriving in West Africa died in appalling numbers from malaria and yellow fever, diseases the local population had substantial resistance to. Novelty is the variable, and it cut in every direction.

The Numbers, Honestly

Estimates  ·  What is known and what is reconstructed

This was one of the great demographic catastrophes in recorded history, and nobody counted it. Knowing how the figures are produced is what lets you use them without either overstating or minimizing.

What the estimates are, and where they come from

Estimates of the population of the Americas in 1492 commonly fall in the range of forty to sixty million, with serious scholarly proposals both lower and higher. Estimates of the decline over the following century to century and a half commonly run from about half to ninety percent or more in particular regions, with some Caribbean island populations almost entirely destroyed, central Mexico and the Andes enormously reduced, and the impact arriving later and unevenly in regions Europeans reached later.

These are reconstructions, and the methods are worth knowing because they are what makes the figures usable. Spanish colonial administrations kept tribute and tax records, which count taxpayers over time and can be worked backward. Missionaries and officials produced accounts and censuses of the populations they administered. Archaeologists estimate settlement size and density from house counts and site extents. And environmental records, pollen and charcoal in sediment cores, show cultivated land reverting to forest, which is a physical trace of people no longer farming.

Each method has a bias and they do not agree, which is why the range is wide and why any single confident number should be treated with suspicion.

How we know: the atmosphere may have recorded it

One line of evidence is worth knowing for its strangeness. Antarctic ice cores show a small dip in atmospheric carbon dioxide around 1610. One published hypothesis, sometimes called the Orbis spike, links it to the Americas: if tens of millions of farmers died, tens of millions of hectares of cultivated land reverted to forest and scrub, and the regrowth drew down enough carbon to register globally. Other researchers dispute the size of the effect and point to other causes, so this is a live argument rather than a settled fact. Cite it the way the researchers do, as a proposed signal consistent with depopulation on that scale, and you are demonstrating exactly the handling of contested evidence this course rewards.

How to write a contested number

Three habits, and they transfer to every disputed figure you will meet. Give a range, not a point. "Estimates of the pre-contact population range from about forty to sixty million and higher" is more accurate and reads as more expert than "there were fifty-four million people." Say what kind of number it is. A reconstruction from tribute records is a different sort of claim from a census. Attach it to something observable. "Sherburne Cook and Woodrow Borah's reconstruction, built from Spanish tribute assessments, puts central Mexico at about 6.3 million people in 1548 and about 1.1 million in 1605" is checkable in a way that a hemispheric percentage is not: it names its authors, its records and its years.

And do not let the uncertainty do work it should not. The range is wide, the mechanism is not in doubt, and the lowest credible estimates still describe a catastrophe with few parallels in human history. Precision about what is uncertain is not the same as doubt about what happened.

What Crossed Westward

c. 1493 to 1750  ·  Wheat, cattle, horses, sugar

The animals changed the land fastest, and they mattered in two opposite ways: as an ecological invasion that remade landscapes, and as a technology that Indigenous nations took and used on their own terms.

The ecological invasion

Europeans brought wheat, barley, grapes, olives and sugarcane, the crops of the diet they intended to keep eating, and they brought cattle, pigs, sheep, goats, horses and chickens. On his second voyage in 1493 Columbus carried livestock deliberately, and this is the moment the biological invasion begins in earnest.

Herd animals arriving in the Caribbean and Mexico, where no comparable grazing herds lived, with few large grazers to compete with, multiplied at extraordinary rates. Feral pigs and cattle spread across the Caribbean islands and then the mainland faster than the Europeans themselves, destroying Indigenous crops, which were often grown without fences because there had been no herds to fence out. That is the mechanism worth having: an agricultural system designed for a world without large herd animals has no defense against them, so livestock did not merely accompany colonization, they undermined the food supply of the people being colonized.

European weeds and grasses traveled in fodder, ballast and dung and displaced native plants on grazed ground. The compound effect, disease plus livestock plus new plants, is what historians call ecological imperialism: the colonizers arrived with a biological package that altered the environment in their favor before, and often faster than, their armies did.

Sugar, and the model that came with it

Sugarcane crossed on the second voyage too, and it is the plant with the most consequences in this chapter. The crucial point is that the plantation package, monoculture, a mill and boiling house that must process cane within hours of cutting, merchant capital, and coerced labor, was developed in the medieval Mediterranean and refined on the Atlantic islands: on Madeira, where sugar was worked by enslaved people, including Canary Islanders and Africans, before Columbus sailed, and then on São Tomé, settled by Portugal in the 1480s and 1490s.

So the Americas did not invent this system; they received it, at a scale the islands could never support. Brazil became the first great American sugar economy in the sixteenth century, and the Caribbean followed in the seventeenth. What the Americas supplied was land in quantities nobody in the Old World could imagine, and, after section 01, land with too few workers on it. Section 05 explains why that shortage alone does not explain African slavery.

What the enslaved carried

People crossed too, and not by choice. Enslaved Africans were carried across the Atlantic by force, and African foods crossed on the same ships, often as food for the voyage, among them okra and rice, which the College Board names. Okra was growing in Brazil by the 1650s, and enslaved people planted African foods in garden plots to feed themselves. African rice was grown by enslaved Africans in the Americas, and many of them knew rice farming from West Africa; how much that knowledge shaped rice growing in places such as South Carolina is still argued over by historians.

What Indigenous peoples did with what arrived

This section would be dishonest if it stopped at things happening to people. Horses escaped, were traded and were taken, and by the eighteenth century nations of the North American plains had built entirely new ways of life around them. Mounted hunters could take bison at a scale that transformed diet, wealth and mobility; the Comanche built a formidable power on horsemanship; and the equestrian Plains culture that outsiders would later treat as timeless and traditional is in fact a post-contact creation, made by Indigenous people out of an introduced animal, in under two centuries.

Sheep are a second case. Among the Diné, sheep herding and the weaving economy built on their wool became central to subsistence and to cultural life. Neither of these is a story of passive reception; both are adoption and transformation, which is what people do with new technology when they have any room to act at all.

Naming one of these in an essay does real work. It shows you can hold catastrophe and agency in the same account, which is the difference between describing Indigenous peoples as objects of history and as participants in it.

What Crossed Eastward

c. 1500 to 1750  ·  Maize, potatoes, cassava

American crops helped feed population growth across Afro-Eurasia, and the reason is not that they were tastier: they produce more calories per acre, and they grow where the old staples could not.

The mechanism, and then the cases

Two properties do the work. American staples yield heavily: a field of potatoes produces far more food energy than the same field of wheat or rye. And they tolerate land that existing staples reject, dry hillsides, thin acidic soils, short seasons, so they do not merely replace a crop, they extend cultivation onto ground that was not farmed at all. A society that can feed itself from land it previously ignored can support more people without conquering anyone.

Europe. The potato spread slowly and then decisively across northern Europe, where it produced more calories per acre than grain on cool, damp ground and could be left in the earth when armies passed through. Its contribution to European population growth from the eighteenth century onward is substantial. Its risk is equally instructive: a region that becomes dependent on one high-yielding crop is exposed to that crop's failure, and the Irish famine of the 1840s, beyond this period, is the case everyone should keep in view when writing about the benefits of the exchange.

China. Maize and the sweet potato reached China in the sixteenth century (officials in Fujian promoted the sweet potato in the famine year of 1594) and were taken up on exactly the terrain the mechanism predicts: dry hills and uplands that rice paddies could not use. The Qing population roughly doubled across the eighteenth century, and while the causes are several, secure grain supplies, internal peace and administrative capacity among them, the ability to farm marginal land is one of them.

Africa. Maize and cassava took hold along the West and West-Central African coasts, especially in Kongo and Angola, by the 1600s, and spread inland over the next two centuries and more; cassava suited farmers because it grows in poor soil, tolerates drought and can be left in the ground until needed. Both raised the caloric base of the regions that adopted them. And both were used to feed the people on slave ships, which is a connection to state plainly rather than to soften: the same crops that supported population growth in West and Central Africa were feeding the vessels carrying its people across the Atlantic.

Everywhere. Tomatoes, chilies, cacao, vanilla, peanuts, squash and tobacco crossed and reshaped cuisines and habits: chilies became fundamental to the cooking of India, Sichuan, Korea and Hungary within two to three centuries, which is worth remembering the next time a "traditional" cuisine is described as unchanging.

The asymmetry worth naming

Set the two directions beside each other and the asymmetry is stark. Eastward went crops that fed population growth across Europe, Asia and Africa. Westward went crops, livestock and diseases, and in many regions the diseases killed most of the people who received them.

The exchange was not a trade between partners; it was a transfer between hemispheres in which one side's population grew on the other side's plants while the other side's population collapsed from the first side's pathogens. When a prompt asks you to assess the consequences of the Columbian Exchange, that sentence is the assessment, and everything else is evidence for it.

How we know: the crops leave a paper trail and a genetic one

Crop diffusion in this period can be tracked several ways at once, which is why it is unusually well established. Herbals and agricultural manuals record new plants arriving, sometimes with confusion about where they came from, since maize was called Turkish wheat in parts of Europe and the potato was long treated with suspicion. Tax and market records show what was being grown and sold. Genetics adds the clincher: the varieties grown in Africa, Asia and Europe descend from American populations, and their relationships can be traced. Three independent kinds of evidence pointing the same way is a much stronger claim than any single source.

A Chain That Is Too Simple

c. 1500 to 1750  ·  Disease, plantations, and choices

"Disease killed the Indigenous population, so Europeans enslaved Africans" is the most common causal chain students write about this topic, and it is wrong in the way single causes usually are: it is not false that disease mattered, but it hides the decisions that made the outcome.

What the chain gets right, and what it leaves out

It gets one link right. Disease emptied land of workers, in the Caribbean almost entirely within a few decades, and the new plantations of sugar, and later tobacco, needed a great many workers. That is a real connection between this topic and the next.

It leaves out the rest. The plantation worked by enslaved Africans was not invented in the Americas: on Madeira, sugar was grown with enslaved workers, including Canary Islanders and Africans, before 1492, and a Portuguese trade in enslaved Africans along the West African coast already existed, as the Topic 4.2 chapter describes. Europeans chose to bring that system across the Atlantic and to expand it. Disease created a shortage of workers; it did not decide who would be enslaved. People decided that.

How the labor systems worked, from the Spanish grants of Indigenous labor to chattel slavery on the plantations, is the subject of Topic 4.4. For this topic, the honest sentence is the qualified one.

Common mistake to avoid

Do not write that Africans were enslaved because they were immune to disease, as though epidemiology explains slavery. It does not. Inherited resistance to malaria among many West and Central Africans may explain why enslaved Africans survived in plantation zones better than others did, which colonists noticed, but it explains nothing about why anyone was enslaved. That was a choice, made by people who profited from it, and racial ideas grew up alongside the practice and were used to justify it, which is part of Topic 4.7.

Building an Answer That Scores

Each card is a full comparison or chain: the claim, the specific evidence, and the reason. The first one answers the question students most often get wrong.

Causation

The catastrophe came from new germs and the conditions of conquest, not from the people

Many Afro-Eurasian crowd diseases have animal reservoirs or close animal relatives, and they became endemic in dense, connected populations, so European adults carried immunity acquired by surviving them in childhood. The Americas had domesticated the llama, alpaca, guinea pig, turkey and dog, none living in the crowded proximity that generates such diseases, and had been separated from Afro-Eurasia for thousands of years. The difference lies in the animal and population ecology, the isolation, and the war, forced labor and hunger that came with the germs, rather than in the people, which is confirmed twice over: Europe suffered exactly this kind of collapse from a novel pathogen in the fourteenth century, and Europeans died in appalling numbers of malaria and yellow fever in West Africa. Novelty was the variable, and it cut in every direction.

Consequences

One hemisphere’s population grew on the other’s plants

Eastward went potatoes, maize, cassava and sweet potatoes, crops that yield more calories per acre and grow on dry hills, thin soils and short seasons, extending cultivation onto land that had not been farmed and underwriting population growth in Europe and Qing China and feeding many people in West and Central Africa. Westward went wheat, sugar, cattle, horses and the diseases of section 01, and in many regions the population that received them fell by more than half, in some by ninety percent or more. The exchange was not a trade between partners but a transfer in which one side grew on the other's plants while the other collapsed from the first side's pathogens.

Chains

Disease made a shortage of workers; people made the plantation system

Smallpox, measles and other diseases emptied land of workers in the Americas, in the Caribbean almost entirely, and the sugar plantations of Brazil and the Caribbean needed many workers. But the plantation worked by enslaved Africans already existed on Madeira before 1492, and a Portuguese trade in enslaved Africans already ran along the West African coast. Europeans chose to bring that system across the Atlantic. Disease was a condition; the system was a decision, and Topic 4.4 explains how it worked.

Agency

Plains horse culture is younger than the printing press

Horses arrived with Spanish colonization, escaped, were traded and were taken, and within roughly two centuries Indigenous nations of the North American plains had built new economies, military systems and ways of life around them, with the Comanche among the most formidable powers on the continent. Diné sheep herding and weaving is a parallel case. Both are adoption and transformation rather than passive reception, and citing one is how you write an account of the Columbian Exchange that holds catastrophe and agency together instead of treating Indigenous peoples only as the objects of what was done to them.

The Empire of Chokepoints

c. 1480 to 1650  ·  Rivalry, forts, and the cartaz

Portugal was a small kingdom with no army that could conquer the large states of Asia. What it had was ships that carried guns, so it built an empire out of the only thing that advantage could hold: the narrow places every ship has to pass.

Why rivals built empires at all

The College Board puts the cause in three words: the maritime empires were "driven largely by political, religious, and economic rivalries." Each word does work. Political: Portugal and Spain divided their claims in the Treaty of Tordesillas in 1494; the Dutch Republic fought Spain for its independence from 1568; England and France fought each other for colonies through the 1600s and 1700s. Religious: papal bulls of the 1450s framed Portuguese expansion as war on Muslim powers and a Christian mission, and after the Reformation, Protestant England and the Dutch fought Catholic Spain. Economic: West African gold, Asian spices and American sugar, and the profits of carrying them.

Rivalry also explains why the empires kept changing hands. When one state found a route or a rich coast, the others came after it. The Dutch took Elmina from Portugal in 1637 and Malacca in 1641; the English and French built colonies of their own in North America and the Caribbean in the early 1600s.

What Portugal actually seized

Portugal's first great post was in Africa: the fortress of São Jorge da Mina, Elmina, built on the Gold Coast in 1482 to buy gold. In Asia, Afonso de Albuquerque took Goa in 1510, and by 1530 it was the capital of the Estado da Índia, Portugal's network of eastern posts. Malacca, taken in 1511, controls the main strait between the Indian Ocean and the South China Sea. Hormuz, taken in 1515, sits at the mouth of the Persian Gulf. Add fortified posts down the East African coast and along the Indian shore.

Every one of them is a port, and several, Malacca and Hormuz above all, sit at chokepoints. None of them is a province. The Portuguese held dozens of fortified points and almost no land behind them, which is exactly what a network built to tax movement, rather than to own land, looks like. A trading post made money for the king who backed it and the merchants who traded through it, the College Board's words, in two ways: by buying goods where they were produced, and by taxing other people's ships.

The tax was the cartaz, a pass that a merchant ship bought from the Portuguese, which committed it to calling at a Portuguese port, paying duties there and carrying none of the goods Portugal claimed for itself. A ship sailing without one could be seized by Portuguese patrols.

The merchants who kept trading

The cartaz changed who taxed the ocean. It did not change who traded on it. Gujarati merchants from western India kept shipping cotton cloth across the ocean, often buying a pass and sailing on, and ships from Gujarat's ports kept carrying cargo and pilgrims to the Red Sea. Swahili Arab traders kept the ports of the East African coast trading. Javanese merchants kept carrying rice to Malacca, whoever held it. And the Omanis pushed Portugal back: they drove the Portuguese from Muscat in 1650 and took Fort Jesus at Mombasa in 1698.

Much of the ocean's trade stayed intra-Asian trade: trade between one Asian port and another, in Asian goods. European merchants often made their money by joining it, carrying goods from one Asian port to the next, as the Portuguese did between China and Japan (section 02). That is why the College Board says the old networks "continued to flourish": the newcomers disrupted and taxed them, and the merchants who ran them kept them going.

Why ports and not provinces

This is the mechanism the section turns on. Portuguese naval artillery could out-shoot the fleets it met in the Indian Ocean and could batter a harbor, which is how the Portuguese won at Diu in 1509. But a ship's guns cannot occupy a river valley. Ashore, out of range of the guns, the Portuguese faced Gujarat, Vijayanagara, the Safavids, the Ottomans and Ming China (and, after 1526, the Mughals), states with armies, revenues and populations many times their own. Their empire took the shape of their advantage: decisive at sea and at the water's edge, small fifty miles inland.

Common mistake to avoid

Do not write that Portugal "took over Indian Ocean trade." It taxed a share of it from a string of forts, and it did so alongside much larger states that found the arrangement tolerable or not worth the cost of ending. When a regional power did decide to push back, it often could: Ottoman fleets contested the Red Sea, Aceh and Calicut fought the Portuguese for decades, and Oman took Muscat in 1650 and Mombasa in 1698.

Asia's Terms

1557 to 1641  ·  Macau, Nagasaki, Dejima

The strongest states in Asia let Europeans trade on Asian terms. Ming China allowed a small Portuguese settlement under Chinese officials, and Tokugawa Japan, fearing Christianity, cut European trade down to one Dutch post on an island in Nagasaki harbor. Europe's empires grew; in China and Japan they did not decide how far.

Macau under Ming officials

Ming China had restricted private overseas trade for much of the dynasty and dealt with foreigners on its own terms. From 1557, Chinese provincial officials let Portuguese merchants settle permanently at Macau, a small peninsula on the south coast. It was not a conquest and not, in the Ming view, a transfer of land: Chinese officials kept authority over the port, and the Portuguese paid them a yearly ground rent. Historians date the formal arrangement variously from the 1550s to 1573, but on the central point they agree: Macau existed because China allowed it, on China's conditions.

Why allow it at all? Because the trade was useful. Ming law officially barred Chinese merchants from trading directly with Japan, so the Portuguese carried Chinese silk to Japan and Japanese silver back to China, and Macau let Chinese officials keep that trade in one place they could watch.

Japan, 1635 to 1641

Portuguese ships first reached Japan in the 1540s, and Jesuit missionaries followed. By 1600 they had made many Christian converts, and the Tokugawa shoguns, who had just unified Japan after a century of war, came to see Christianity as a loyalty outside their control. The shogunate banned Christianity in 1614. The Spanish were expelled in 1624; the English had already left.

Between 1633 and 1639, the shogun's senior councilors sent five sets of orders to their two magistrates at Nagasaki, the main port where Portuguese and Chinese ships traded. The 1635 set has seventeen articles. The first reads: "Japanese ships are strictly forbidden to leave for foreign countries." The third: "If any Japanese returns from overseas after residing there, he must be put to death." Others order a search of every incoming ship for Christians, rewards for informers, and rules for buying and selling raw silk. In 1637 and 1638 a rebellion at Shimabara, near Nagasaki, drew in many Christian peasants, and in 1639 the councilors banned Portuguese ships outright, because, the order says, the Portuguese "have secretly transported those who are going to propagate that religion."

The Dutch stayed. They came to trade, not to spread Christianity, and from 1641 they lived and traded only on Dejima, a small artificial island in Nagasaki harbor, under Japanese supervision. Chinese merchants kept trading in Nagasaki, and Japan kept dealing with Korea and the Ryukyu kingdom through other ports. Historians later named the orders the "closed country" edicts, but the documents themselves carry no such title, and Japan was not closed: it chose who could come, and on what terms.

How we know

The orders survive in the shogunate's records and are translated in David J. Lu, Japan: A Documentary History (1997). Their audience is the clue to reading them. They were working instructions to two officials, not announcements to foreigners, so they show what the government meant to enforce, not what it wanted the world to hear.

Kongo and Asante

c. 1480 to 1750  ·  Kings, guns, and the trade

The College Board's sentence is that maritime trade "fostered the growth of states in Africa, including the Asante and the Kingdom of the Kongo." Both grew stronger by using the trade, and both sold captives into it. Holding both halves together is the whole section.

Kongo: a Christian king and the trade

Kongo was the dominant power in west-central Africa when Portuguese ships reached the mouth of the Congo River in the early 1480s. Its king was baptized in 1491, and his son Afonso I, king from about 1509 until the early 1540s, made Christianity the religion of the court. Afonso asked Portugal for priests and teachers, sent young Kongo nobles to study in Portugal, and fought his wars with Portuguese allies. Kongo's artists made Christianity their own, in brass crucifixes cast in their own style.

The trade strengthened the king in a specific way: imports came through him. When Afonso controlled who traded and what they sold, foreign goods were his to give to loyal nobles, and nobles who owed him their goods owed him their loyalty. Traders who sold directly to his nobles cut into that hold.

What Afonso's letter does and does not say

The same letter complains that traders "carry away our native-born countrymen and the children of our nobles and vassals as well as relatives of ours," and asks the king of Portugal to send "neither traders nor trading goods." The historian Linda Heywood adds what the letter leaves out. Kongo had slavery of its own, and most captives Kongo sold to the Portuguese in Afonso's reign were foreigners taken in Kongo's wars; Afonso ran a slave market in his capital. Kongo custom protected free-born Kongo people from sale abroad, and the people the letter says were carried off were free-born Kongo, seized by thieves and sold to Portuguese traders.

Keep both facts in view when you read the letter yourself in Module 08. Later, Heywood argues, Kongo's civil wars of the 1600s broke down the protection of free-born Kongo people, and far more of them were sold abroad.

Asante: gold, guns, and Feyiase

On the Gold Coast, where Elmina stood, several Akan states were joined under one ruler, the Asantehene, around 1700. Asante tradition credits Osei Tutu and the priest Okomfo Anokye with the union and with the Golden Stool that symbolized it; the sources disagree on Osei Tutu's dates, so this chapter gives none. In 1701 Asante defeated Denkyira, the region's leading power, at the battle of Feyiase, a date recorded in Dutch archives.

Asante's strength rested on gold, which it controlled, and on guns, which it bought from Dutch and English traders on the coast, paying in part with captives taken in its wars. Through the 1700s it expanded into the main power of the region. Like Kongo, it grew stronger through the trade and sold people into it.

Work in the Colonial Americas

c. 1500 to 1750  ·  Mit’a, encomienda, hacienda, indenture, slavery

The College Board's sentence is that colonial economies in the Americas "largely depended on agriculture, utilized existing labor systems, including the Incan mit’a, and introduced new labor systems." The colonies needed workers for farms and mines, and they got them by reusing an old duty and inventing new forms of coerced work.

The mit’a: an old duty, a new master

The Inca state had required each community to send people in turns to work for the state, building roads, farming state lands and serving in the army. That rotating duty was the mit’a. Between 1572 and 1575 the Spanish viceroy of Peru, Francisco de Toledo, reorganized it into a draft that required the communities of the highland districts to send one-seventh of their adult men each year to the silver mine at Potosí: more than 14,000 men by 1578.

It is the clearest continuity-with-change case in the unit. The name and the idea of a rotating duty to the state continued. The master, the work and the danger changed: the men now served a colonial state in deep mines and mercury refineries, and many did not come home. What the silver of Potosí did to the world economy is Topic 4.5.

Encomienda: paying conquerors with other people's work

The crown had not paid for the conquests. Expeditions were private ventures under royal license, and the men who survived expected reward. The encomienda was the reward: a grant to a Spaniard of the right to demand labor and tribute from a named Indigenous community, in exchange for a formal duty to protect them and have them taught Christianity. Legally it was not a grant of land and it did not make the people property; in practice, on communities collapsing from epidemic disease, the difference was thin.

Challenge came early and from inside the system. The friar Antonio de Montesinos preached against it in 1511; Bartolomé de las Casas spent decades campaigning; the New Laws of 1542 restricted it and provoked armed revolt in Peru. Indigenous communities fought it in Spanish courts too: in the 1550s the people of Tepetlaoztoc, in central Mexico, sent the crown a painted complaint, the Codex Kingsborough, recording what their encomenderos had taken and how they had been treated. Encomienda declined across the 1500s, as the population it drew on collapsed.

Haciendas, indentures, and chattel slavery

What grew instead was a set of systems that overlapped rather than replacing each other in neat stages. On the hacienda, a large estate, Indigenous and mixed-race workers farmed and herded for the owner, often for wages and sometimes tied to the estate by debt; historians argue about how many were bound that way. In the English colonies of the 1600s, most European newcomers to the Chesapeake came as indentured servants, working four to seven years under contract to pay for their voyage. And on the sugar and tobacco plantations, enslaved Africans were held in chattel slavery: owned as property, for life, with their children born enslaved.

Common mistake to avoid

Do not treat these as the same thing with different names. Ask the same four questions of each: who did the work, for how long, whether they could leave, and who took what they produced. An indentured servant's term had an end date; an enslaved person's had none, unless an owner chose to free them, and their children were born enslaved. That difference is why chattel slavery is a change and not just more of the same.

Slavery, Old and New

1450 to 1750  ·  Households, the Sahara, the plantations

The College Board pairs two sentences: enslavement in Africa "continued in its traditional forms," and the plantation economy "increased the demand for enslaved labor in the Americas, leading to significant demographic, social, and cultural changes." Slavery did not begin with the Atlantic trade. The plantations changed its size and its kind.

The older forms that continued

In many African societies, slavery was old and took forms unlike the plantations. Enslaved people, often captives from wars, were taken into households, where they worked, served or married, and over time they or their children could become part of the family. In Kongo, a king's letter of 1550 speaks of a rival's relatives "even if they are slaves," and Heywood concludes that domestic slaves in elite households were counted as part of the extended family. That was not freedom: enslaved people could still be sold. But it was not slavery passed down forever.

Captives were also carried out of Africa by older routes than the Atlantic: north across the Sahara to North Africa and the Mediterranean world, and east across the Red Sea and the Indian Ocean to Arabia, Persia and India. Those trades were centuries old before 1500 and went on through this whole period alongside the Atlantic trade.

What the plantations changed

Sugar, and later tobacco, needed huge numbers of workers, and the plantations of Brazil and the Caribbean bought enslaved Africans to do the work. The estimates in the Trans-Atlantic Slave Trade Database show the change in one comparison: about 278,000 Africans were forced onto ships bound for the Americas in the whole 1500s, and more than 2.5 million in the fifty years from 1701 to 1750.

The CED names three kinds of change. Demographic: on Barbados enslaved Africans outnumbered Europeans by the late 1600s, and across the British, French and Dutch Caribbean colonies Africans and their descendants became most of the people. Social: plantation slavery was for life and inherited, and colonial law tied it to race; Virginia's law of 1662 made the children of enslaved women enslaved. Cultural: African languages, beliefs and foods crossed with the people who carried them, and how they mixed with American and European cultures is Topic 4.5.

Common mistake to avoid

Do not write that slavery was invented after 1500, and do not write that African and plantation slavery were the same. The continuity is that slavery in its older forms went on in Africa; the change is the scale, the destination and the kind of slavery the plantations demanded: lifelong, inherited and tied to race.

Two Tools to Make an Empire Pay

c. 1500 to 1750  ·  Laws, charters, shares

European rulers did not just own their empires; they had to make them pay for ships, soldiers and forts every year. They used two tools: an idea about how a state grows rich, and a new kind of company built to carry it out.

The idea, and what follows from it

The idea starts from a belief: a kingdom's strength is the gold and silver it holds, and one kingdom's gain in trade is another's loss. If that is true, three policies follow. Sell more than you buy, because the difference arrives as gold and silver. Protect your own makers with taxes on foreign goods, because buying foreign goods sends money out. And keep the colonies' trade for yourself: colonies should send raw goods home and buy the home country's finished goods, and trade with no one else.

This set of ideas is now called mercantilism, but its critics named it. Adam Smith in 1776 called it the "mercantile system," and the word mercantilism came into use later still. The people who held these ideas did not call themselves mercantilists. One of them, Thomas Mun, a London merchant and a director of the English East India Company, put the first policy in a sentence: England "must ever observe this rule; to sell more to strangers yearly than wee consume of theirs in value." His book was printed in 1664, after his death.

Rulers wrote the idea into law. Spain's Casa de Contratación, founded in Seville in 1503, licensed and registered the trade with Spain's American colonies, and silver crossed the Atlantic in fleets guarded by warships. England's Navigation Act of 1651 said goods from Asia, Africa and the Americas could come into England only in English ships; it was aimed at Dutch shippers, who carried much of Europe's trade. Acts from 1660 listed colonial goods, sugar and tobacco among them, that could be shipped only to England. In France, the minister Jean-Baptiste Colbert used tariffs, royal support for workshops and royal trading companies from the 1660s.

A company that could go to war

A voyage to Asia cost a fortune and ships were often lost. A joint-stock company spread that risk: it sold shares to many investors, who split the profits and the losses. The English East India Company was chartered by Elizabeth I in 1600. In 1602 the States General of the Dutch Republic chartered the Verenigde Oostindische Compagnie, the VOC, and gave it, for twenty-one years, the only right of any Dutch merchant to trade east of the Cape of Good Hope. The VOC kept its capital in the company rather than paying it out after each voyage, and its shares were bought and sold in Amsterdam.

The charter is what made the company a tool of the state. It let the VOC build forts, keep soldiers, make treaties with Asian rulers and wage war. A monopoly meant that the profits of the Asia trade went to one national company rather than being competed away among Dutch merchants, which is exactly what a mercantilist government wanted, and the company's guns meant that rulers could fight rivals through it. The College Board's sentence names both uses: rulers and merchants used these companies "to finance exploration," and rulers used them "to compete against one another in global trade."

Common mistake to avoid

Do not treat mercantilism as an early version of free trade. It starts from the opposite belief. Mercantilists thought one country's gain was another's loss, so the state had to direct trade with laws, monopolies and force. Adam Smith wrote The Wealth of Nations in 1776 as an attack on that system.

Old Fights over Trade, New Tools

1509 to 1674  ·  Diu, Tondibi, Ambon

States had fought over trade routes for centuries. What changed in this period was the reach of the fights, now stretching across oceans, and the tools rivals fought them with.

The Indian Ocean: spices and who would carry them

When the Portuguese reached the Indian Ocean they tried to take the spice trade for themselves, by force and by selling passes to other ships (Topic 4.4). That threatened the Muslim states whose ports and merchants carried spices through the Red Sea to Egypt and on to the Mediterranean. In 1509 a fleet of Mamluk Egypt, the sultan of Gujarat and the ruler of Calicut fought the Portuguese off the port of Diu, in western India, and lost. After the Ottoman Empire conquered Egypt in 1517, Ottoman fleets fought the Portuguese in the Red Sea, the Persian Gulf and the western Indian Ocean through the 1500s, besieging Diu in 1538. The dispute was economic: which route, and whose ships, would carry the spices.

The Portuguese never closed the old route. By the middle of the 1500s spices were again reaching the Mediterranean through the Red Sea in large amounts, and Muslim merchants kept trading.

The Sahara: gold and salt

In Africa, the sultan of Morocco, Ahmad al-Mansur, looked south. The Songhai Empire controlled the trade in West African gold and the salt mines of the Sahara, and the two states had quarreled over the salt mine at Taghaza in the 1580s. Al-Mansur sent an army with firearms across the desert, and in 1591 it defeated the Songhai army at Tondibi (Topic 3.1 told the battle). Gold and the salt trade were a large part of the reason: al-Mansur wanted the gold.

He did not get as much as he hoped. Morocco held Timbuktu and Gao, but the old empire broke into smaller states, and gold kept flowing to other buyers, including European traders on the coast.

Europe's rivals: spices and shipping

The European companies fought each other too. The Dutch and English East India companies competed for the spice islands of Southeast Asia, and in 1623 VOC officials on the island of Ambon arrested and executed ten English company men, along with nine Japanese soldiers in VOC service and a Portuguese man, accusing them, on confessions taken under torture, of plotting to seize the Dutch fort; historians still argue over whether any plot existed. The English called it the Amboyna massacre, and it embittered relations for decades.

At home, England and the Dutch Republic fought three wars at sea, in 1652 to 1654, 1665 to 1667 and 1672 to 1674, largely over trade and shipping. The Navigation Act of 1651, which shut Dutch ships out of England's trade, was one cause of the first. Mercantilism and the rivalry were the same story: if one country's gain is another's loss, trade is a kind of war.

How we know

The company leaders said so themselves. In 1614 Jan Pieterszoon Coen, then the VOC's director-general of trade in Asia, wrote to the company's directors that trade in Asia "must be carried on and kept up under the protection and favor of your own weapons, and that the weapons must be paid for out of the profits the trade earns, so that trade cannot be kept up without war, nor war without trade" (translated from the Dutch of H. T. Colenbrander's edition of Coen's papers, 1919). Coen was asking for more ships and soldiers, so the sentence is an argument as well as a description, but it shows how the company's own leaders understood the business.

Silver Goes to China

1545 to 1750  ·  Potosí, the Single Whip, Manila

Spain found the silver, but China decided where it went. China's government taxed in silver while China mined little of its own, and that demand pulled American silver across both oceans.

Where it came from

In 1545 an Andean miner, Diego Gualpa, by his own later account, found silver at Potosí, in the Andes, and Spanish colonists claimed the mountain and began mining it. In 1546 a great silver strike came at Zacatecas in New Spain. From the 1570s, new ways of refining low-grade ore with mercury, and the mit'a draft of Andean workers (Topic 4.4), made Potosí produce far more. Historians estimate that Spanish America produced most of the world's silver between 1500 and 1800, roughly four-fifths of it by one standard estimate.

Why China set the price

Ming China had tried paper money and abandoned it, and through the 1500s its government folded many taxes and labor duties into a single payment in silver. Historians call this the Single Whip reform; the minister Zhang Juzheng pushed it across the empire around 1580. Tens of millions of Chinese households now needed silver every year to pay their taxes, and China mined little of its own.

So silver was worth more in China than in Europe. In the early 1500s, an ounce of gold bought about six ounces of silver in China and about eleven or twelve in Europe; by the 1590s, about five and a half to seven at Canton and twelve and a half to fourteen in Spain. A merchant who carried silver from Europe or the Americas to China made money on the silver itself, before he bought anything. That gap is the engine of the whole trade. In the College Board's words, the silver was used "to purchase Asian goods for the Atlantic markets and satisfy Chinese demand for silver."

Two roads to China

The western road crossed the Pacific. A Spanish expedition found a way back across the Pacific in 1565, and in 1571 the Spanish founded Manila in the Philippines. Galleons sailed from Acapulco in Mexico to Manila, usually one or two a year, loaded with silver. At Manila, Chinese merchants from Fujian sailed in with silk, porcelain and other goods and took the silver home. The galleons carried the silk back to Mexico, and some of it went on to Peru and Spain.

The eastern road crossed the Atlantic. Silver landed in Seville and moved through Europe's trade. Much of it then went to Asia around Africa, in Portuguese, Dutch and English ships, because Europeans had few goods that Asian buyers wanted. The European companies paid for Asian pepper, spices, cotton cloth, silk and porcelain largely in silver.

How we know

Spain taxed the silver its colonies mined and registered the fleets and cargoes, and Manila kept customs records, so historians can count much of the registered silver. They compare those records with Chinese and European evidence. What the records miss is smuggling, which everyone agrees was large, so the registered totals are a floor, not a ceiling.

New Systems, Old Markets, More Work

c. 1500 to 1750  ·  The Atlantic, Asia, the household

The new trade did two things at once. It built an Atlantic system that tied three continents together, and it ran into old regional markets that kept their own merchants and their own ways. Underneath both, ordinary people did more of the same old work.

The Atlantic system

The Atlantic trading system, in the College Board's words, "involved the movement of goods, wealth, and labor, including enslaved persons." Sugar and tobacco went to Europe. Guns, iron, alcohol and cloth went to Africa, and much of the cloth was Indian cotton. Cowrie shells from the Maldive Islands, shipped by Dutch and English traders, were used as money on the West African coast. Silver, and later Brazilian gold, flowed toward Europe. And enslaved Africans were forced across the ocean to the plantations (Topic 4.4 gives the numbers).

The neat triangle of textbook diagrams, Europe to Africa to the Americas and home, is a simplification. Brazil traded directly with Africa: ships from Rio de Janeiro carried sugar-cane brandy to Angola, ships from Bahia carried tobacco, and later gold, to the Bight of Benin, and both brought captives back on two-way routes. The Atlantic system also reached far outside the Atlantic: the cloth from India and the shells from the Maldives were part of it.

The markets that kept going

None of this replaced the trade that already crossed Africa and Asia. The College Board's sentence is that regional markets "continued to flourish in Afro-Eurasia by using established commercial practices and new transoceanic and regional shipping services developed by European merchants." The first half is continuity: merchants kept their partnerships, their credit and their family firms with agents in distant ports. Armenian merchants based at New Julfa, outside the Persian capital of Isfahan, traded from India to the Mediterranean and Europe through family networks; Gujarati and Chinese merchants kept trading too.

The second half is the change. European ships became one more carrier inside these markets. The VOC carried Japanese silver to its base on Taiwan to buy Chinese silk, Japanese silver and copper to India, and Indian cloth to Southeast Asia to buy spices, and earned much of its money from trade between Asian ports.

More of the same work

More trade meant more demand for food and goods, and that meant more work for the people who made them: peasant and artisan labor. The College Board says this labor "continued and intensified," which is continuity and change in two words.

In western Europe, merchants put out wool and flax to families in the countryside, who spun and wove at home and were paid by the piece; historians call this the putting-out system. In India, weavers in Gujarat, on the Coromandel coast and in Bengal made cotton cloth for buyers in Asia, Africa and Europe, and the Dutch and English companies' purchases grew greatly in the 1600s, often through advances of money to weavers before the cloth was made. In China, peasant households in the Jiangnan region raised silkworms and wove silk; some of it went to Manila to be traded for American silver. Much of the spinning, silkworm raising and weaving was done by women and girls.

Common mistake to avoid

Do not write that Europeans paid for Asian goods with better European products. For most of this period they had few goods Asian buyers wanted, and paid largely in American silver. In 1700 and 1721 English laws even restricted Indian cotton cloth to protect England's own weavers.

Families and Cultures

c. 1500 to 1750  ·  Africa and the Atlantic world

The silver, goods and people the empires moved did not only change prices. They changed who lived in African villages, and they made new cultures in the Americas that no single people made alone.

Who the trade took

About two of every three captives carried across the Atlantic were male. European buyers paid more for young men to work the plantations, and African sellers often kept women and children, whom African buyers valued. In some regions, especially parts of West Central Africa, that left communities with more women than men.

Historians argue that this changed family life where the trade hit hardest. Women did more of the farming, and more women and girls were enslaved within Africa itself, because local buyers wanted the people the Atlantic market wanted less. Some historians, Patrick Manning among them, argue that the shortage of men also made polygyny, men taking several wives, more common; others find the evidence for that thin. Some regions lost population, or grew more slowly than they would have, while states that sold captives grew richer. The College Board names these "demographic changes in Africa that resulted from the trade of enslaved persons."

How we know

The sex ratio comes from the shipping records gathered in the Trans-Atlantic Slave Trade Database, which count the men, women and children on many voyages. The family changes are harder to see: historians such as Patrick Manning and John Thornton build them from those numbers, from European observers' reports, and from later counts of the population, including Portuguese censuses of Angola from the late 1700s. That is why the effects are stated as arguments, and why they differ from region to region.

A mixed Atlantic world

In the Americas, Africans, Indigenous Americans and Europeans lived side by side, usually because some of them were forced to, and each shaped what came out. The College Board's words are "the mixing of African, American, and European cultures and peoples, with all parties contributing to this cultural synthesis."

Food: dishes across the Atlantic world combined African, American and European ingredients and ways of cooking, and enslaved West Africans brought rice-farming knowledge, though historians still argue over how much it shaped rice growing on coasts such as Carolina's. Language: new languages called creoles formed, such as Haitian Creole and Papiamentu. Most of their words come from a European language, and much of their grammar and sound shows the influence of African languages; linguists still debate exactly how they formed. Music and faith mixed too, as section 06 shows.

Common mistake to avoid

Do not write that enslaved Africans and Indigenous Americans "lost their culture" and took European culture instead. They kept and remade their own ways under conditions designed to stop them, and the new cultures came from all of them. Do not swing to the opposite mistake either: the mixing happened under force and slavery, not as a meeting of equals.

Spread, Clash, Blend

c. 1500 to 1750  ·  Christianity, Islam, and new beliefs

Religions traveled with the ships, and they did three different things when they arrived. Comparing those three is what the College Board asks for: how belief systems affected societies in similar and different ways.

Spread

Contact "expanded the reach" of existing religions, in the College Board's words. Christianity spread through Spanish and Portuguese America, to the Philippines after the Spanish arrived in 1565, and in Kongo, whose kings had become Christians (Topic 4.4). Jesuit missionaries reached Ming China; Matteo Ricci lived in Beijing from 1601. Islam kept spreading in Southeast Asia along older trade routes, carried by merchants and teachers rather than by European ships.

Clash

Contact also "contributed to religious conflicts." In the archdiocese of Lima, in Peru, priests led by Francisco de Ávila began campaigns in about 1609 and 1610 to "extirpate idolatry," to root out the old religion. They searched villages, destroyed Andean sacred objects and the remains of ancestors that families honored, and punished Andean religious specialists. In Japan, as Topic 4.4 showed, the shoguns banned Christianity and drove out the missionaries.

Blend

And contact produced syncretism, the blending of religions into new beliefs and practices. At Tepeyac, a hill near Mexico City, a shrine to the Virgin of Guadalupe drew Nahua and Spanish Christians by the 1550s. In the 1570s the Franciscan friar Bernardino de Sahagún wrote about the hill and about Tonantzin, "our mother," the name of a goddess the Nahua had honored there; the lesson's Skill Builder asks you to read his own words and judge his claim. Historians still argue over how much Guadalupe and Tonantzin were linked; what is certain is that Nahua Christians made Mary their own. The story of Mary appearing to Juan Diego in 1531 was first published in 1648.

In Kongo, Christian crucifixes were made by Kongo artists in Kongo style and given Kongo meanings. In Potosí, in the 1700s, a painter showed the Virgin Mary as the silver mountain itself, which art historians read as joining Christian painting to Andean beliefs about sacred mountains. And in the French colony of Saint-Domingue (today's Haiti), enslaved Africans in the 1700s built Vodou from West and Central African religions and Catholic saints.

Common mistake to avoid

Do not call syncretism "faking it." Some people did hide old beliefs behind Christian names, but many were sincere Christians who understood their new faith through ideas they already had. And check your dates: Cuban Santería took its familiar form in the 1800s, after this period, so it is not an example for 1450 to 1750.

The Push

1450 to 1750  ·  Centralization and expansion

States between 1450 and 1750 grew stronger in two ways at once. They pulled power inward, into the hands of their rulers, and they pushed outward over lands and peoples that had never been theirs. Each push took something from someone, and that is the context for every revolt in this topic.

Pulling power inward

Centralization means a ruler gathering power that others used to share. Topic 3.2 showed the tools: officials and soldiers who owed everything to the ruler, such as the Ottoman devshirme; tax systems that answered to the center; and a court, like Louis XIV's at Versailles, where great nobles spent their time under the king's eye instead of building power on their own lands.

Every one of those tools took something from someone. A king who wanted more taxes took money from towns, landowners and peasants. A king who wanted the final word took it from the judges, nobles and local assemblies who had shared it. A tsar who wanted his landholders to keep their workers took freedom of movement from the peasants. So the people who pushed back against centralization were usually the state's own subjects. These were internal challenges, from inside the state.

Pushing power outward

Expansion means a state reaching over new lands and peoples. Topics 4.2 to 4.5 showed the maritime empires doing it: forts on the coasts of Africa and Asia, colonies in the Americas, tribute and forced labor, missionaries, and the plantation economy built on enslaved Africans. Land empires expanded too: the Mughal emperors pushed south into the Deccan, and Russia pushed east and south.

Expansion took land, harvests, labor and faith from people who had never agreed to be ruled. So the people who pushed back against expansion were usually at the edges of a growing state: kingdoms it wanted to conquer, and peoples it had only recently claimed. These were external challenges, from outside the state that was pushing. (External here does not mean European states fighting each other over trade. That is Topic 4.5.)

Common mistake to avoid

Do not write that people resisted simply "because they were oppressed." That is true of almost any revolt and explains none of them. Name the push (what the state wanted), the group it landed on, and what the push took from that group. That is the context, and it turns a complaint into an explanation.

Inside the State

1648 to 1671  ·  France and Russia

The pushback against centralization came from the state's own people, and from every level: the judges of a royal court, the people of a capital city, great nobles, runaway peasants and frontier Cossacks.

France: the Fronde, 1648 to 1653

In 1648 France had been at war with Spain for years, and war was eating most of the crown's money. Louis XIV was nine years old. His mother, Anne of Austria, ruled for him with her chief minister, Cardinal Mazarin, and they kept raising money the way the late king and Cardinal Richelieu had: with new taxes and loans against future taxes.

The Parlement of Paris was the kingdom's highest court. Its judges had to register a royal law before it took effect, and they saw themselves as protectors of the people against a grasping crown. In 1648 they refused the new taxes, including taxes aimed at themselves, and wrote demands to limit the regent's power over the kingdom's money. In August the government arrested a respected judge, Pierre Broussel. Paris answered overnight with some twelve hundred barricades in the streets, and the queen had to let him go. In January 1649 the royal family fled Paris.

Then the great nobles rose, led by the Prince of Condé, in what is called the Fronde of the princes. But the rebels wanted different things and quarreled with each other, and some took help from Spain, France's enemy. By late 1652 the movement collapsed, and Louis XIV returned to Paris. Historians write that the Fronde marked his whole reign: as an adult he ruled in person and kept the great nobles where he could watch them.

Russia: the Law Code of 1649 and Razin

In Russia, the tsar's Law Code of 1649 finished turning peasants into serfs, people bound to their lord's land. Before it, a lord had only a limited number of years to reclaim a peasant who ran away; after it, a runaway could be returned no matter how long he had been gone. The tsar's landholding cavalry, who needed peasants to work their land, had demanded it.

Many peasants ran anyway, south to the Don River, where the Cossacks lived: communities of free frontier fighters who chose their own leaders and lived beyond the tsar's direct rule. In 1670 a Don Cossack named Stepan Razin, called Stenka, led an army up the Volga River. The towns of Saratov and Samara opened their gates, and Russian peasants and non-Russian peoples of the Volga rose by the tens of thousands. The rebels said they were loyal to the tsar and were fighting his great lords and officials.

The tsar's regular army defeated them at Simbirsk. Don Cossack leaders loyal to the tsar captured Razin and handed him over, and he was executed in Moscow in 1671. Serfdom stayed, and grew harsher over the next century.

How we know

Much of what Western Europeans knew about Razin came from travelers, merchants and newspaper reports, and travelers' tales spread stories that historians doubt, such as the Dutch sailor Jan Struys's account, printed in 1676, of Razin throwing a Persian princess into the Volga. Historians rely on Russian government records and the letters of the rebels themselves. The Dutch print of Razin's capture in this lesson was made for a book published in Leiden about thirty years later by an artist who never went to Russia.

At the Edges: Asia and Africa

1622 to 1707  ·  The Deccan and Ndongo

At the edges of expanding empires, the people who pushed back were often rulers themselves. A warrior kingdom wore down the Mughal Empire, and an African queen used war and diplomacy to hold off Portugal for forty years.

The Marathas and the Mughals

In the western Deccan, the high plateau of central India, Shivaji built a Maratha kingdom from hill forts, beginning in the 1640s. His soldiers fought with speed and surprise: they raided, struck supply lines and slipped back to their forts instead of meeting a large army in open battle. In 1674 he had himself crowned king at his fort of Raigad.

The Mughal emperor Aurangzeb wanted the whole Deccan. In 1681 he moved his court south. He conquered the two Deccan sultanates, Bijapur and Golconda, by 1687, and he captured and executed Shivaji's son and successor in 1689. But he could never finish the Marathas. His artillery could take a fort, and the Marathas would hold or retake others; his huge army could not pin down an enemy that would not stand and fight. He spent the rest of his life in the south and died there in 1707.

After his death, Maratha power spread across much of India while the Mughal emperors in Delhi grew weaker. The Mughal decline had many causes, but the long war in the Deccan was one of them: it cost the empire money, soldiers and decades of its emperor's attention.

Njinga and Portugal

In West Central Africa, the Portuguese colony at Luanda, founded in 1576, pushed inland into the kingdom of Ndongo. Its wars took captives, and Luanda became one of the busiest ports of the Atlantic slave trade.

In 1622 King Mbandi of Ndongo sent his sister Njinga to Luanda to negotiate peace with the Portuguese governor. She agreed to be baptized there, taking the Christian name Ana de Sousa. In 1624 she became Ndongo's ruler. When Portuguese armies drove her out, she conquered the neighboring kingdom of Matamba in the 1630s and made it her base. When the Dutch captured Luanda in 1641, she joined forces with them; when the Portuguese took it back in 1648, she kept fighting. In the 1650s she made peace with Portugal, and she died in 1663, still ruling Matamba.

She fought Portugal and bargained with it, as each moment called for. And she was no enemy of the slave trade itself: her wars took captives, and Matamba sold them. Like many rulers of her time, she resisted a state that was trying to rule her, not every injustice of her age.

At the Edges: The Americas

1675 to 1696  ·  New Mexico and New England

In the same decade, two Indigenous peoples rose against two expanding colonies. One drove the colonists out for twelve years; the other was crushed. Both show how expansion pressed on local communities, and both changed what came after.

Why the Pueblos rose

Spain planted its colony of New Mexico among the Pueblo peoples of the Rio Grande valley in 1598. The Pueblos lived in separate towns of stone and adobe and spoke several different languages. Under the encomienda, they had to hand over part of their harvest to Spanish settlers each year, and they were forced to work for Spaniards without pay.

The heaviest pressure fell on religion. Franciscan friars ran missions in the towns and tried to replace the Pueblo religion with Christianity. In 1661 they banned the sacred kachina dances and burned masks and prayer sticks. In 1675 the governor ordered kivas, the rooms where ceremonies were held, destroyed, and arrested forty-seven Pueblo religious leaders as sorcerers. Three were hanged, one killed himself, and the rest were whipped and jailed, until Pueblo men came to the capital in force and the governor let them go. This religious suppression came on top of drought and famine in the 1660s and 1670s, and raids by Apache neighbors that Spanish soldiers did not stop.

How many towns rose on one day

One of the whipped men was Popé (today often spelled Po'pay), a religious leader from Ohkay Owingeh. He and other leaders planned a revolt that would strike every Spanish settlement at once, so the Spanish could not gather to fight back. Runners carried knotted cords from town to town. A town that agreed untied one knot, and the knots left counted the days until the rising.

The Spanish caught two runners, so the towns struck early, on August 10, 1680. In the Pueblo Revolt they killed about 400 colonists, including 21 friars, surrounded Santa Fe and cut off its water. On August 21 the governor led the surviving colonists south to El Paso. The Pueblos burned churches, rebuilt kivas and returned to their own ceremonies, and for twelve years they ruled themselves.

In 1692 a new governor, Diego de Vargas, came back, and by 1696 Spain had put down two more risings. But Spanish rule came back on new terms. The encomienda was not brought back, and the friars pressed far less hard against Pueblo religion than before. The revolt was defeated in the end, and it still changed how Spain ruled New Mexico.

How we know

Almost every written account of the revolt was written by Spaniards. The fullest Pueblo voices come from Pueblo men captured in 1681 and questioned by Spanish officials, such as Pedro Naranjo of San Felipe Pueblo, whose answers were written down in Spanish. Read them knowing who asked the questions. Archaeologists have added another kind of evidence: the villages the Pueblos built and lived in after the revolt.

Metacom's War

In New England, English colonists had grown in number and were taking more and more land from the Wampanoag and their neighbors, and putting them under colonial courts. In 1675 Plymouth Colony tried and executed three Wampanoag men for the killing of John Sassamon, a Christian Native man who had warned the English of trouble. Fighting broke out with the Wampanoag, led by Metacom, whom the English called King Philip, and other Native nations joined in, each for its own reasons.

For a year, Native fighters destroyed more than a dozen English towns. But the colonists had far greater numbers, Native allies of their own, and help from the Mohawks to the west. Metacom was killed in August 1676, and many Native captives were sold into slavery in the Caribbean. The Native nations of southern New England lost their independence.

Enslaved People Organize

c. 1600 to 1740  ·  Brazil, Jamaica, South Carolina

Enslaved people resisted every day, by working slowly, keeping their own beliefs, and running away. The College Board asks about something more: organized resistance, many people acting together in a way that challenged the authorities.

Palmares: a state in the hills

In the hills behind the sugar coast of northeastern Brazil, people who escaped slavery built Palmares. Communities like this are called maroon communities, and in Brazil a quilombo or mocambo. Palmares was not one hideout but a cluster of many fortified towns, joined for defense, with a capital, leaders, farms, and trade with nearby settlers, who sold them guns, powder, cloth and tools for food. First reported in the late 1500s, it lasted most of the 1600s and held thousands of people: people born in Africa, people born in Brazil, and some Indigenous people and Europeans hiding from the colonial authorities.

Palmares beat back expedition after expedition, Portuguese and Dutch. In 1678 its leader, Ganga Zumba, accepted a treaty with the colonial government: freedom for those born in Palmares, in exchange for returning other runaways. Many in Palmares rejected it, and the next leader, Zumbi, fought on. In 1694 a large army of hired frontier fighters took the main town, and in 1695 Zumbi was found and killed. November 20, the day of his death, is now Brazil's Day of Black Consciousness.

Jamaica: freedom by treaty

After the English took Jamaica from Spain in 1655, people who escaped slavery built communities in the island's mountains, the Jamaica Maroons, and many more joined them over the following decades. For years the colony sent soldiers against them and could not win. In 1739 it signed treaties instead. The Maroons led by Cudjoe were promised that they would be "for ever hereafter in a perfect state of freedom and liberty," with 1,500 acres of land for themselves and their descendants.

The same treaty made them promise to send back any enslaved person who ran away to them, and to "take, kill, suppress, or destroy" other rebels on the island. Organized resistance forced a colony to recognize the Maroons' freedom, and the colony turned the Maroons into a guard for slavery.

Stono: a march for freedom

In North America, on Sunday, September 9, 1739, about twenty enslaved people gathered by the Stono River near Charleston, South Carolina, broke into a store for guns and powder, and marched south. They were heading for Spanish Florida, where the Spanish king had promised freedom to people who escaped the English colonies and became Catholic. Others joined until there were as many as a hundred. Several of the rebels came from Kongo, where Catholicism was widespread.

By that afternoon the colony's militia broke up the march, and over the following weeks and months it hunted down the survivors; one leader was not caught until 1742. Somewhere between twenty and forty white colonists died, along with dozens of rebels. The accounts disagree: one written that year counted about twenty white and forty Black dead. South Carolina answered the Stono Rebellion with the Negro Act of 1740, which tightened control over every enslaved person in the colony: it made freeing an enslaved person depend on a special act of the assembly and put every militiaman on patrol duty.

Common mistake to avoid

Do not write that maroons were "just runaways hiding in the woods." Palmares had towns, leaders, farms, trade and an army, and it made a treaty. The Jamaica Maroons forced a colony to sign one.

01

Pushed Out and Taken In

1492 to 1700s  ·  Spain, Portugal and the Ottoman Empire

In the 1490s two kinds of state faced the same people and made opposite choices. Spain and Portugal wanted one religion in each kingdom, and pushed their Jews out or forced them to convert. The Ottoman Empire wanted useful subjects, and took the refugees in. Neither choice was about kindness; each was about what the state wanted.

Spain and Portugal push a group out

In January 1492 Ferdinand and Isabella took Granada, the last Muslim kingdom in Spain. On 31 March they ordered every Jew in their kingdoms who would not become Christian to leave by the end of July. Jews could take their goods, but not gold, silver or coins. Historians' estimates of how many left run from about forty thousand to two hundred thousand. Many others converted and stayed, and historians disagree about which group was larger. Many of the exiles crossed into Portugal.

Four years later, in December 1496, Portugal's king Manuel I ordered the Jews out of his kingdom too. In 1497, instead of letting most of them leave, he had them baptized by force. In both kingdoms, converts and their descendants were then watched for signs that they still practiced Judaism in secret, and Spanish "purity of blood" rules barred people with Jewish or Muslim ancestors from some offices, colleges and religious orders, even if they were Christians themselves.

This is what the College Board means by a state that suppressed diversity. Spain and Portugal did not want different religions living side by side under the crown; they wanted one, and they were willing to lose thousands of skilled subjects to get it.

The Ottomans take them in, and how they ruled Christians and Jews

The Ottoman sultan Bayezid II let the refugees settle in his empire. Thousands came, to Istanbul, to Salonica (today Thessaloniki, in Greece) and to other cities, where Jewish communities already lived. In Salonica the newcomers set up congregations named after the places in Spain and Portugal they had come from, and by the 1500s Jews were the largest group in the city, which they stayed for centuries. Exiles from Spain set up Istanbul's first printing press, which printed books in Hebrew.

Why would a Muslim sultan welcome them? Because they were useful. The refugees were merchants, doctors, craftsmen and taxpayers, and an empire gains from people like that. A remark is often quoted in which Bayezid mocks Ferdinand for making his own kingdom poor and the sultan's rich. It is very likely a later story, but it captures the reasoning.

The Ottoman Empire already ruled millions of Christians and Jews, and it governed them by an old rule of Islamic law: they were protected subjects. They could practice their religion. Their own religious leaders ran their marriages, inheritances, schools, charity and family courts, and answered to the state for their community's taxes and good behavior. That is accommodation: making room for a group's differences. For the state, it was also cheap. It did not have to build courts and schools for every faith; it let each community run its own and collected a tax.

You may read that this was called the millet system. Use the word carefully. The tidy picture of a few neatly separated communities, each under one official chief, is mostly a nineteenth-century arrangement read backward into earlier centuries. Between 1450 and 1750 the arrangements were looser and were made community by community and city by city.

Common mistake to avoid

Do not write that the Ottomans gave everyone equal rights. Christians and Jews paid a special tax, the jizya; in an Islamic court they generally could not testify against a Muslim; they could not freely build new churches or synagogues; and the top jobs in the army and government went to Muslims. The devshirme of Topic 3.2 took Christian boys, converted them and trained them for the sultan's service, so a Christian family's son could rise to the top only by leaving his family's religion. The arrangement was room for difference, not equality.

It Depends: Which Ruler, Which Class

1556 to 1700s  ·  Mughal India and the Ottoman Empire

Two things could change how an empire treated a group. One was the ruler: a policy one emperor made, the next could unmake. The other was class: inside a single empire, a group's members could live very different lives depending on how rich and powerful their families were.

Akbar and Aurangzeb

The Mughal emperors were Muslims ruling a land where most people were Hindu. Akbar, who ruled from 1556 to 1605, made room for Hindus on purpose. In 1564 he ended the jizya. He married daughters of the Rajputs, the Hindu warrior kings of northwestern India, beginning with a princess of Amber in 1562, and he made Rajput princes some of the highest-ranking commanders and officials in his empire. Man Singh of Amber reached the highest rank open to anyone outside the emperor's own family.

Akbar was not only being generous. He needed Rajput armies and the loyalty of the Hindu majority, and he got both. This is the College Board's "utilize the ... military contributions" of a group as much as "accommodate."

A century later his great-grandson Aurangzeb brought the jizya back, in 1679, and leaned on a stricter reading of Islamic law at court. Yet he still employed many Hindu officers and nobles. Same empire, same groups, a different policy. A ruler's welcome was a choice, and the next ruler could take it back.

Ottoman women, by class

The College Board names "the varying status of different classes of women within the Ottoman Empire." The key word is varying.

At the very top, women of the sultan's family held real power. Hürrem began as an enslaved woman in the palace and became the wife of Sultan Suleiman. She paid for a mosque complex in Istanbul, designed by the architect Sinan, with a school, a soup kitchen for the poor and, by 1551, a hospital. In the next century Kösem ruled as regent for her young son Murad IV and later her grandson Mehmed IV, and was a powerful adviser to her grown son Ibrahim, at times between 1623 and 1651, when she was killed in a struggle at the palace.

In the towns, ordinary Muslim women owned property and went to the Islamic court to claim inheritances, debts and dowries, and to settle divorces. In one Anatolian city in the early 1600s, women took part in about four in ten of the property transactions recorded in its court. But the law still treated them unequally: a daughter inherited half what a son did, and a woman's word in court counted for less than a man's.

And many women were enslaved, as servants and concubines in wealthy households, with almost no say over their own lives. In one painting in this lesson, a wealthy mother receives guests in her bedroom while an enslaved woman prepares drinks in the corner: a wealthy woman and an enslaved woman in one room.

A New Elite in China

1644 to 1700s  ·  The Qing dynasty

The Manchus who conquered China in 1644 were a small minority ruling a huge Han Chinese majority. Their whole social policy follows from that: govern through the Chinese system, put a new elite on top, and keep the majority from either rebelling or swallowing the rulers.

What the Qing kept

The Manchus came from north of the Great Wall. In 1644, after rebels had captured Beijing and the last Ming emperor had killed himself, the Manchus, whose rulers had named their dynasty the Qing in 1636, took the city and began to rule China.

They did not try to make China Manchu. They kept the Ming way of governing: the civil service examinations, the Confucian bureaucracy, and the law codes. Han Chinese scholars still passed the exams and filled most government jobs, because no small conquest group could run China any other way. Emperors such as Kangxi presented themselves as model Confucian rulers. That is the continuity.

What the Qing changed

At the top they built a new elite: the banners. These were hereditary military units of Manchus, of Mongols, and of Han Chinese families who had joined the Manchus before the conquest; in fact, Han bannermen outnumbered Manchus inside the banners at first. Bannermen were paid by the state, received grain and housing, lived in walled quarters inside Beijing and other major cities, and had legal privileges that ordinary Han Chinese did not. A new political and military elite had been made by conquest.

They also restricted the Han majority. In July 1645 every Han man was ordered to shave the front of his head and wear his hair in the Manchu braid, the queue, on pain of death. Many Han resisted, because in Confucian teaching the body, hair included, was a gift from one's parents. Many top posts in the capital went to two officials, one Manchu and one Han, so that the Manchu could watch the Han. And from about 1648 a long line of ditches and willow fences, the Willow Palisade, kept Han farmers out of the Manchu homeland in the northeast.

Common mistake to avoid

Do not write that the Qing banned all marriage between Manchus and Han. The rules changed over time: an early decree allowed some such marriages, and later rules restricted them. Say instead what is clear: the Qing kept the banners separate and privileged, and made the Han wear the queue.

A New Top in the Americas

1500s to 1700s  ·  Spanish America

Conquest in the Americas threw together Spaniards, Indigenous peoples and Africans, and their children. Spanish colonial society ranked them by ancestry, which historians call the casta system, and among people counted as Spanish it favored those born in Spain. Conquest put a new elite on top.

The ranking

At the top were peninsulares, people born in Spain. The viceroys who ruled New Spain and Peru for the king were almost always Spanish-born, and Spanish-born men held many of the highest posts in the government and the Church. This was a new political elite, made by conquest.

Next came creoles, people of Spanish ancestry born in the Americas. Silver mines, great estates and trade, the new global economy of Topics 4.4 and 4.5, made many creole families very rich: a new economic elite. They resented the newcomers from Spain who got the highest jobs, a resentment that would matter a great deal later.

Below them came people of mixed ancestry, such as mestizos (Spanish and Indigenous) and mulattos (Spanish and African). Indigenous people lived under their own town governments and paid tribute to the crown. People of African ancestry were at the bottom: some were free, and many were enslaved. Even enslaved people had some standing in Spanish law: they could marry, go to court, and sometimes buy their freedom.

The paintings and the records

In the 1700s, artists in Mexico painted sets of casta paintings. Each painting showed a father, a mother and their child, with a label naming each person's category, such as "From a Spaniard and an Indian woman comes a mestizo." Some of the earliest known sets were painted around 1715 by Juan Rodríguez Juárez; most are from later in the century. Many were made for viewers in Spain.

The paintings make colonial society look like a neat chart. Real life was messier. Most of the many labels in the paintings do not appear in official records; the census used only a few broad groups. Parish priests often wrote a category into a baptism record, but how a person was described could change from one record to the next, with their wealth, their job, their clothes, their family's reputation and the company they kept. A prosperous mestizo might be recorded as Spanish; a poor creole might not be treated as one.

How we know

Historians compare the paintings with parish registers, censuses, court cases and petitions. When the same person appears under different labels in different records, that tells us categories could be argued over and could change. When a guild, a school or a religious order turned someone away because of ancestry, that tells us the categories still had real consequences. Both are true at once.

Old Elites Squeezed

1500s to 1722  ·  Ottomans, Russia, France, England

As rulers grew stronger and centralized power, the old elites who had shared it were squeezed: the Ottoman cavalry, the Russian boyars, the French nobles. But they rarely disappeared, they often kept their privileges, and in England they won.

The Ottoman cavalry and their timars

For centuries the core of the Ottoman army was the sipahi, a cavalryman who held a timar: the right to collect the taxes from a piece of land, in return for riding to war when the sultan called and bringing armed men with him. A timar was not the holder's property, and it was not supposed to pass automatically to his son.

From the late 1500s, wars were won more and more by infantry with guns, paid in cash, like the Janissaries of Topic 3.1. The sultan needed money more than he needed horsemen. So the state turned more and more timar land into tax farms: the right to collect a district's taxes, sold to whoever offered the treasury the most. From 1695 the state began selling these rights for life. The sipahis faded. Some families held on to their timars, and the timar system was not abolished until 1831, but the sipahis no longer led the army. Tax farmers and powerful provincial families, who grew rich and influential collecting taxes, rose in their place. Historians no longer call this simply the empire's "decline": the state changed shape and kept going.

The Russian boyars

The boyars were Russia's great noble families. They sat on the tsar's council, the Boyar Duma, and competed for posts by the rank of their families. Earlier tsars had already curbed them: Ivan IV seized boyar lands and killed many boyars in the years of terror called the oprichnina, from 1565 to 1572. In 1682 the old system of seats by family rank was ended.

Peter the Great went further. He replaced the boyar council with a Senate he appointed, in 1711. He ordered nobles and officials to shave their beards and dress like western Europeans, and taxed those who would not. He made every nobleman serve the state, in the army or the government, for life. And in 1722 his Table of Ranks set out fourteen ranks of service in the army, the government and the court. A man's place depended on the rank he earned, and a commoner who rose high enough became a noble.

The title "boyar" faded away. The nobles did not. They kept their estates and their power over their serfs, which the Law Code of 1649 (Topic 4.6) had tightened. They lost their independence and kept their privilege.

French nobles at court, and the English exception

In France, Louis XIV had seen the Fronde as a boy (Topic 4.6). As king he centralized power. He relied on royal officials, the intendants, to run the provinces, and in 1682 he moved his court and government to his palace at Versailles, where great nobles spent their days near him, competing for offices, pensions and his favor, instead of raising armies at home. The French nobility kept their titles, their land and their freedom from the main tax, the taille. What they lost was the power to defy the king.

But the power of old elites could rise as well as fall. In England in 1688, a group of leading nobles and a bishop invited William of Orange to bring an army. King James II fled. Parliament, a body of landowning nobles and gentry, gave the crown to William and Mary and, in the Bill of Rights of 1689, set limits on the monarchs who came after them. There the old elite won.

Common mistake to avoid

Do not write that old elites disappeared. The College Board's word is fluctuated: their power went up and down. The Ottoman sipahis faded, but tax farmers and provincial families rose. Russian and French nobles lost independence and kept privilege. English nobles and gentry gained power over the crown. Name what each lost, and what each kept or gained.

What Changed and What Did Not

By 1750 silver from a Bolivian mountain was paying taxes in China, a potato from the Andes was feeding Ireland, and roughly six million Africans had been carried across an ocean against their will. And the largest economies on earth were still the ones that had been largest in 1450, still trading along the same routes, still selling Europe goods Europe could not make. Both of those are true, and knowing why is what this unit was for.

The Circuit Closes

c. 1571 to 1750  ·  The first world economy

Long-distance trade is old and the Silk Roads were vast. What happens in this period is different in kind rather than in degree: for the first time all the world’s inhabited continents are connected in a single circuit through which goods, money and consequences travel in both directions.

A definition you can test

Use this test, because it makes "global" an argument instead of an adjective. A network is global when a change in one region produces a measurable effect in a region on the other side of the world through a chain of exchanges, and when the circuit closes rather than ending in a terminal market.

The Silk Roads of Unit 2 fail that test, and the Topic 2.1 chapter explains why: goods moved through many hands across intermediaries, Rome and Han China barely knew each other, and the Americas were not in the system at all. The exchange was enormous and it was Afro-Eurasian.

From 1571, with the Manila galleon running, the circuit closes. Silver mined at Potosi with mita labor crosses the Pacific to Manila, buys Chinese silk and porcelain from Fujianese merchants, and those goods return to Acapulco and travel on to Europe, while another and larger silver stream crosses the Atlantic to Seville and reaches Asia around Africa in Dutch and English holds. American mines, European shipping, Asian manufacture and African labor are now parts of one system in which each depends on the others.

Test it with a case. Ming China consolidates its taxes into a silver payment; the price of silver in China rises above the European level; that price gap makes it worth working a mountain in the Andes at appalling human cost; the mita is imposed on Andean villages to supply the labor; the silver reaching Europe drives a century of inflation. A fiscal reform in Beijing changes wages in Seville and the death rate in Potosi. That is what global means.

The price revolution as evidence

The Topic 4.5 chapter gives the mechanism; here it functions as proof of integration. Prices in Europe rose roughly threefold to fivefold across the sixteenth century, and they rose in countries with no American colonies as well as in Spain, which is the detail that matters. An inflation confined to the empire that owned the mines would show a Spanish story. An inflation across Europe shows that the silver moved through a connected market faster than any government could contain it.

The distributional effects, real wages falling behind prices, debtors gaining, landlords able to raise rents gaining, are the human content of that integration, and they are worth naming because they show a world market reaching people who had never seen a ship.

Use this in your answer
Strongest tool, with its mechanism
The closed circuit test. The mechanism is that a trade network becomes global rather than merely long when goods and money return to their origin through an unbroken chain, because a closed circuit transmits changes in both directions, so a tax reform in one region alters prices and labor conditions in two others and the whole system can be said to have a single economy rather than adjoining ones.
Best evidence of exclusion or limit
Global does not mean equal or integrated in the modern sense. Volumes were small relative to domestic economies, most people lived and ate locally, and the effects were fastest in money and slowest in everything else.
Comparison hook
Against the Silk Roads in Topic 2.1: those moved goods across Afro-Eurasia through many intermediaries with no closed circuit and no American participation, so Rome received silk without any Han fiscal decision touching Roman prices. Adding one ocean crossing in 1571 is what converts a long route into a world economy.

The Catastrophe and the Crops

c. 1492 to 1800  ·  Demography in both directions

The demographic story of this period runs in two opposite directions at once, and putting them in the same paragraph is what makes it an argument: one hemisphere lost most of its population to disease while the other grew on crops that came from it.

The collapse, and the chain it set off

The Topic 4.3 chapter has the mechanism in full; what belongs here is the summary and the chain. Pre-contact population estimates for the Americas commonly run from about forty to sixty million and higher, and the decline over the following century and a half is commonly estimated at something between half and ninety percent, worst in the Caribbean and in the densest mainland regions. The cause was virgin soil epidemics of Afro-Eurasian crowd diseases, compounded by warfare, forced labor and the disruption of farming, and it fell where it did because of domesticated herd animals and long isolation rather than anything about the people.

The chain to the Atlantic slave trade is what the success criteria ask you to connect, and it has middle links. Colonizers wanted silver and sugar, both extraordinarily labor-intensive. The first labor systems, encomienda and an adapted mita, drew on Indigenous communities, and those communities collapsed. European indentured servants were expensive, served fixed terms, had legal standing and died in tropical conditions. Enslaved Africans were reachable through a Portuguese supply network already running, were already working the plantation model on Madeira and Sao Tome, held a status that was permanent and heritable, had no state able to intervene for them, and survived malaria and yellow fever better than the alternatives. Roughly twelve million were embarked across the whole trade and about ten and a half million survived the crossing, with the peak in the eighteenth century.

Write the links. A student who writes that the population collapsed and therefore Africans were enslaved has skipped every decision that was made, and it is the decisions that make it history rather than weather.

Two crops, and what they did

The potato is the clearest case. It yields more calories per acre than the grains it competed with in Europe, grows in cool, wet, poor soils and at altitude, and stores underground, which also means it survives the passage of an army that would have burned a grain field. It was slow to be accepted and then spread widely across northern Europe, and it underwrote population growth in Ireland, Prussia, Poland and Russia. Its risk arrived with the dependency: a population living on one crop, and largely on a narrow genetic range of it, has no margin, which is what the Irish famine of the 1840s demonstrated.

Maize is the second, and its reach is wider. It grows fast, tolerates a range of conditions and yields heavily, and it spread into southern Europe, across Africa, and into China, where together with the sweet potato it allowed cultivation of dry hill land unsuited to rice or wheat and helped support the enormous Qing population growth of the eighteenth century. In Africa maize and cassava, the latter valuable because it grows in poor soil, resists drought and stores in the ground, became staples over large regions. The dark irony belongs in the sentence: crops from the Americas raised the carrying capacity of African populations during the centuries in which the Atlantic trade was removing millions of people.

Common mistake to avoid

Do not write the exchange as a mutual swap of foods. The traffic in plants ran both ways and the consequences did not. One hemisphere received crops that raised its population, and the other received pathogens that destroyed its population, along with livestock that damaged its agriculture and a plantation system that consumed imported labor. The accurate framing is a transfer with catastrophically asymmetric effects, and the phrase to avoid is any version of "both sides benefited from the exchange."

An outbreak in a population with no prior exposure, infecting all ages at once and disabling the society as well as the individuals.

The sequence from encomienda and mita through indenture to hereditary Atlantic slavery, each link a decision with alternatives.

The Andean tuber that raised calorie yields on poor northern soils and underwrote European population growth, at the cost of dependency.

03

What Did Not Change

c. 1450 to 1750  ·  The structural reasons

This is the half of the unit most answers omit, and the success criteria weight it equally. Three things carried on straight through the age of European expansion, and each one continued for a structural reason you can state.

Indian Ocean trade kept running, in Asian hands

Gujarati, Tamil, Malay, Arab, Armenian and Chinese merchant networks continued to carry the majority of Indian Ocean commerce throughout this period. The Portuguese cartaz of Topic 4.4 taxed a share of it from a string of forts and never controlled it, and Asian shipping continued to move rice, cotton, timber, spices, horses and pilgrims along routes that predated any European arrival.

The structural reason is that the Europeans could not replace the function. Those networks rested on credit relationships, kinship, brokerage and local knowledge built over centuries, in ports whose rulers were not European subjects, carrying an intra-Asian trade that was far larger than the European long-distance trade laid over it. A fort at a strait can tax passing cargo; it cannot manufacture the commercial relationships that produce the cargo.

Asia still made the goods

China and India remained the world's largest economies and its dominant manufacturers throughout the period. Indian cotton textiles were finer, better dyed and cheaper than European weaving could match, so much so that England legislated against them in the Calico Acts rather than compete. Chinese porcelain and silk had no European equal, and European potters could not reproduce true porcelain until Meissen in the early eighteenth century.

The structural reason is skill and scale. Indian weaving and dyeing and Chinese ceramics rested on centuries of accumulated technique, dense specialist workforces, established supply chains and enormous domestic markets that supported production at a scale European workshops had no equivalent of. Shipping does not substitute for that. What Europeans had to sell was silver, which is the balance-of-payments point from Topic 4.5 and the single most useful continuity in the unit.

The land empires and the Islamic networks held

The Ottoman, Safavid, Mughal and Qing states of Unit 3 governed most of Asia's population and wealth throughout this period, and none of them was conquered by a European power within it. Mughal territory and revenue peaked in the late seventeenth century; the Qing expanded enormously into Central Asia in the eighteenth. Where Europeans held territory in Asia it was islands and coastal enclaves.

The structural reason is the one Topic 4.4 establishes: European advantage was naval, and it evaporated inland against states with field armies, tax systems and populations many times larger. The advantage was real, and it was shaped like a coastline.

Alongside them, the Islamic commercial and religious networks of Unit 2 continued: the Hajj, the trans-Saharan routes, the scholarly and legal connections across the Dar al-Islam, and the spread of Islam in Southeast Asia and West Africa, which continued through this period and was not interrupted by European maritime activity because it did not depend on the sea lanes Europeans controlled.

How we know: estimates of what the world produced

Claims that China and India held the largest share of world output come from historical national accounting, most influentially Angus Maddison’s estimates, which reconstruct population and output per person for regions back through this period. On those figures China and India together account for roughly half of world output as late as 1700. Economic historians dispute the method vigorously, because reconstructing output per person before national statistics existed requires heavy assumptions, and rival estimates differ. What no one disputes is the direction: Asia was the center of world manufacturing throughout this period, corroborated independently by trade records showing Europe paying in bullion, and by the technological gap that made Europeans copy Asian goods rather than the reverse. Cite the direction confidently and the percentage carefully.

Use this in your answer
Strongest tool, with its mechanism
The limits of a naval advantage. The mechanism is that superiority in ships and guns lets a power control movement between places without controlling production inside them, so it can tax routes, seize harbors and shift prices while remaining a customer of the economies it sails to, which is why the same century holds both European maritime dominance and continued Asian economic dominance without contradiction.
Best evidence of exclusion or limit
Continuity is not stasis. Asian economies were reshaped by the silver they absorbed, by new crops and by European demand, so the routes and producers persisted while the volumes and money supply did not.
Comparison hook
Against Unit 3’s land empires: those expanded by absorbing contiguous territory and taxing its population, so their power grew with the ground they held. Maritime power grew with the routes it held, which is why one kind of empire is measured in provinces and the other in ports, and why the two coexisted for two and a half centuries without either destroying the other.

Deciding Which Is Which

A method  ·  For any continuity and change prompt

Continuity-and-change prompts are lost by treating them as two lists. The way to win one is to notice that most things in history change in some respects and persist in others at the same time, and to say which respect you mean.

Four questions to ask of anything

Ask about the thing or the arrangement. Slavery is a continuity as an institution and a change in its form: hereditary, racialized, agricultural and on an Atlantic scale is not what the Indian Ocean or Mediterranean systems of Unit 2 were. Say which level you mean and the answer stops contradicting itself.

Ask who it changed for. The period is a catastrophic change for Indigenous Americans, a catastrophic change for enslaved Africans, a substantial change for Western Europe, and something much closer to continuity for a Chinese farmer or an Ottoman townsman, whose new crops and silver-denominated taxes were real but whose world was not remade. A claim about change should name a population.

Ask over what span. Across 1450 to 1750 the world economy is transformed. Across 1450 to 1550 very little outside the Americas has changed yet. A prompt's dates are part of its question.

Ask what would have to be true for the opposite claim. If you want to argue European dominance by 1750, ask what Europe was selling Asia. The answer, silver, is fatal to the claim, and finding that out before a grader does is the entire value of the exercise.

A worked line

Take "to what extent did the period 1450 to 1750 transform the world economy." A strong answer says: it transformed the connections and left the centers in place. The connections are new, a closed global circuit for the first time, an Atlantic economy that did not exist, a Pacific route from 1571, and an integrated silver market that moved prices in countries with no colonies. The centers are old: China and India remained the largest economies and dominant manufacturers, Asian merchants still carried most Indian Ocean trade, and Europe paid its way with a metal rather than with anything it made.

That single sentence, connections transformed and centers unchanged, is a thesis, and everything in this unit is evidence for one half or the other of it.

Use this in your answer
Strongest tool, with its mechanism
Separating the level of the claim. The mechanism is that any historical subject exists at several levels at once, the institution and its form, the route and its operators, the connections and the centers, so a question that looks contradictory resolves as soon as you specify the level, and the specification itself is the analytical move a grader is looking for.
Best evidence of exclusion or limit
It is a method for structuring an answer, not a substitute for knowing things. Every level you name still has to be supported with a specific case.
Comparison hook
Against the Topic 3.4 chapter’s comparison method: there the task was to hold five empires against one question, here it is to hold one period against two questions. Both work by fixing what varies and stating it in the thesis rather than discovering it in the last paragraph.

Terms to use precisely

Level of claim
Whether you are describing an institution, its form, its operators or its scale, which decides whether something counts as continuity.
Scope
The population a claim applies to, which a good thesis names rather than implying everyone.
Periodization
The dates a claim covers, which change the answer and are part of the question.
Counterfactual test
Asking what would have to be true for the opposite claim, the fastest way to find the weakness in your own.
Thesis
The sentence that states what changed and what did not, at what level, for whom, which the rest of the essay supports.

Where Unit 5 Begins

c. 1750 to 1850  ·  The two developments that flip it

If Europeans were maritime specialists and commercial dependents in 1750, then something specific happens next, and knowing what it is protects every essay you will write about the nineteenth century.

Two developments, with dates

Territorial revenue in Asia. The East India Company defeated the Nawab of Bengal at Plassey in 1757 and acquired the diwani, the right to collect Bengal's land revenue, in 1765. That single change converts a trading company into a government with a tax base, which means it no longer needs to ship silver from Europe to buy Indian goods: it can buy them with revenue extracted in India. The balance-of-payments problem that had defined Europe's position in Asia for two and a half centuries stops being a problem, not because Europe started making better goods but because a company started collecting taxes.

Industrial production. Mechanized spinning and weaving from the later eighteenth century, and steam power after it, let British producers make cotton cloth more cheaply than Indian handloom weavers, reversing a relationship that had held throughout this unit. Within a few decades Britain was exporting cotton textiles to India, and Indian weaving communities were devastated. That reversal, and not any event in Unit 4, is what "European economic dominance" actually names.

Both of these are Unit 5. Writing them into Unit 4, or writing Unit 4 as though they had already happened, is the single most common structural error in this part of the course, and avoiding it is worth more than any additional fact.

What Unit 4 handed over

The period did leave Europe with things that mattered enormously for what came next, and naming them is the accurate version of "the rise of the West" rather than the inflated one. Access to American land and resources, silver above all. An Atlantic economy of plantations and coerced labor generating capital. Financial institutions built to handle long-distance risk, joint-stock companies, insurance markets, public debt and central banking. Naval and gunnery capability. And a habit of competitive state investment in maritime enterprise that had no equivalent among the land empires.

None of those add up to dominance in 1750, and all of them are inputs to what happens after. The honest sentence, and the one to end an essay with, is that in 1750 Europeans held the routes and the Americas and were customers everywhere else, and that the reversal comes from Bengal revenue and the factory rather than from the caravel.

Common mistake to avoid

The error to avoid is a single phrase reused across three centuries. If "European dominance" describes 1500, 1650, 1750 and 1850 equally, then nothing in your account has changed and no continuity-and-change prompt can be answered from it. Date the claim: maritime and American dominance from the sixteenth century, commercial dependence in Asia until the mid-eighteenth, territorial revenue in Bengal from 1765, and productive superiority only with industrialization. Four dated claims beat one undated one every time.

Use this in your answer
Strongest tool, with its mechanism
Revenue replacing bullion. The mechanism is that a trading company which acquires the right to collect land tax in a producing region no longer has to ship money in to buy goods, because it can purchase them with revenue taken locally, which converts a chronic trade deficit into a self-financing extraction and removes the constraint that had limited European trade in Asia for two centuries.
Best evidence of exclusion or limit
It applies to Bengal first and spreads unevenly, and it does not by itself explain the productive reversal, which requires industrialization as a separate development.
Comparison hook
Against the Estado da India in Topic 4.4: Portugal taxed movement through a chokepoint and remained dependent on buying what it shipped, while the Company after 1765 taxed production itself. The difference between taxing a route and taxing a field is the difference between Unit 4 and Unit 5.

Terms to use precisely

Plassey
The 1757 battle beginning East India Company territorial power in Bengal.
Diwani
The right to collect Bengal's land revenue, granted in 1765, which turned a trading company into a government.
Industrialization
Mechanized production from the later eighteenth century, which reversed the textile relationship that had held throughout this unit.
Deindustrialization
The destruction of Indian handloom weaving as cheap British cloth entered the market, the reversal seen from the other side.
Dated claim
A statement of dominance tied to a sphere and a period, which is what makes change visible across units.

Building an Answer That Scores

Each card is a full argument: the claim, the specific evidence, and the reason. The first is the unit thesis, and the third is the half most answers leave out.

Social synthesis

Economic development changed social structure unevenly

Plantation expansion, silver extraction, and widening markets changed more than trade. Plantation demand expanded Atlantic chattel slavery; colonial conquest and commerce helped produce new social categories and elite groups such as the Spanish American casta hierarchy; and the Atlantic slave trade altered demographic, gender, and family patterns in parts of Africa. At the same time, older peasant and artisan labor continued and intensified because expanding markets still depended on households and workshops. The social result was therefore uneven change rather than total replacement.

Thesis

The connections were transformed and the centers were not

Before 1492 no network joined all inhabited continents; from 1571 the Manila galleon closed the circuit, so Potosi silver mined with mita labor bought Chinese silk at Manila while a larger stream crossed the Atlantic and reached Asia around Africa. A Ming tax reform requiring payment in silver therefore set the price that made an Andean mountain worth working and drove a century of inflation in European countries that owned no colonies. That is a genuinely new world economy. Meanwhile China and India remained the largest economies and dominant manufacturers, Asian merchant networks still carried most Indian Ocean trade, and Europe paid its way in metal rather than in anything it made. Connections transformed, centers unchanged, and the unit is evidence for one half or the other.

Chains

Depopulation did not cause the slave trade; six decisions did

Pre-contact American population estimates run from about forty to sixty million and higher, and the decline over the next century and a half is commonly put between half and ninety percent, caused by virgin soil epidemics compounded by warfare, forced labor and disrupted farming. Then colonizers wanting silver and sugar drew first on encomienda and an adapted mita; those populations collapsed; European indenture was costly, time-limited, legally protected and deadly in the tropics; and enslaved Africans were reachable through an existing Portuguese network, already working the plantation model on Madeira and Sao Tome, held in a permanent heritable status with no state to intervene, and more resistant to malaria and yellow fever. About twelve million were embarked and ten and a half million survived. Every link is a decision with an alternative.

Continuity

Asian networks continued because Europeans could not replace their function

Gujarati, Tamil, Arab, Armenian and Chinese merchants carried most Indian Ocean trade throughout the period, and Portugal’s cartaz taxed a share of it from about fifty forts without controlling it. The reason is structural: those networks rested on credit, kinship, brokerage and local knowledge built over centuries, in ports whose rulers were not European subjects, serving an intra-Asian trade larger than the European traffic laid over it. A fort at a strait can tax cargo and cannot manufacture the relationships that produce it. The same logic explains manufacturing: Indian dyed cottons and Chinese porcelain rested on accumulated technique, specialist workforces and vast domestic markets, which is why England banned calicoes rather than compete and why Europe could not make porcelain until Meissen.

Periodization

The reversal comes from Bengal revenue and the factory, not the caravel

Throughout 1450 to 1750 Europeans dominated ocean routes and the Americas while paying for Asian goods in silver because they made nothing Asia wanted. Two later developments flip that. The East India Company won at Plassey in 1757 and took the diwani of Bengal in 1765, which let it buy Indian goods with Indian tax revenue instead of imported bullion. Then mechanized spinning and weaving from the later eighteenth century let British producers undersell Indian handloom weavers, and within decades Britain was exporting cotton cloth to India. Date the claim in four parts rather than reusing one phrase for three centuries, and the change between Unit 4 and Unit 5 becomes something you can actually argue.
