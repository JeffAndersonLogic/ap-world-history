Topic 5.1

By What Right?

In 1750 most rulers said their power came from God. A group of writers began asking every old authority the same question, by what right?, and answered it with reason, natural rights and the consent of the people. This chapter follows that question from the new science of the late 1600s to the reformers of the 1800s, and asks how much it had changed by 1900.

01

A New Way of Knowing

1680s to 1780s  ·  Reason and evidence

The Enlightenment's political ideas began with a method. Thinkers learned to test claims about nature with reason and evidence, and then asked whether the same method could test the claims that kings, churches and nobles made about their own power.

The old answer

In 1750 most of Europe's rulers gave the same answer to the question "who does a state belong to?" It belonged to the king, because God had put him there. This is divine right, the claim Louis XIV made at Versailles (Topic 3.2). In most countries one church was the law: Catholic in France and Spain, Anglican in England. Nobles were born into privileges, such as freedom from some taxes, and across much of central and eastern Europe peasants were serfs, bound to work their lord's land. Authority came from God, from tradition and from birth.

Reason and evidence, first for nature

In 1687 the English scientist Isaac Newton published a book showing that a few laws of motion and gravity, worked out with mathematics and tested against what astronomers observed, explained both the paths of the planets and the fall of an apple. The lesson many readers took from it was that the world runs by laws a human mind can discover, without being told by an old book or a priest.

In 1689 the English philosopher John Locke argued that every idea in our minds comes from experience. At birth, he wrote, the mind is like "white paper." Learning from evidence and experience instead of accepting what authorities say is called empiricism. Scientific lectures and experiments became popular entertainment for people who could pay, like the family in Joseph Wright's painting of an air-pump experiment, made famous by a print of 1769.

Common mistake to avoid

Do not write that the Enlightenment was against religion. Most of its writers believed in God, and some were deists, who believed God made the world and then let it run by natural laws. What they questioned was religion used as a power of government (section 02).

Then for people

The step that made the method political was simple to state. If reason and evidence can explain the planets, can they explain people: how we should live together, what a government is for, and what makes a ruler's power rightful? Enlightenment writers turned the method on government, religion, law and society, and that is where the new political ideas came from.

Use this in your answer
Strongest tool, with its mechanism

Best evidence of exclusion or limit

Comparison hook
Against divine right in Topic 3.2: Louis XIV's claim rested on God's choice and could not be tested. An empiricist asks for evidence.

Terms to use precisely

Divine right
The claim that a king's power comes from God, so no one may question or remove him.
Serf
A peasant bound to a lord's land, who owed the lord labor and could not leave freely.
Empiricism

Deism
The belief that God created the world and then let it run by natural laws.
02

The New Answers

1689 to 1763  ·  Locke, Montesquieu, Rousseau, Voltaire

Enlightenment writers started with the individual person, not the king, and came up with new answers to the question of what makes power rightful. The change was not that kings disappeared; it was that a ruler now had to answer the question.

Natural rights and the social contract

In his Second Treatise of Government (1689), Locke argued that people are born free and equal, with natural rights to their "lives, liberties and estates" (an estate is what you own). No government gives these rights, and no government may take them. People form a government to protect them.

That makes government an agreement, a social contract. "No one," Locke wrote, can be "subjected to the political power of another, without his own consent." And if a government tries to destroy the people's rights or reduce them to slavery, it puts itself "into a state of war with the people," who no longer owe it obedience. Most historians think Locke wrote most of the book in the early 1680s, when some members of Parliament were trying to keep the Catholic James from becoming king, and he published it in 1689, just after Parliament had replaced James II. Both times, his argument defended the right to resist a king.

In 1762 Jean-Jacques Rousseau, a writer from Geneva who lived in France, began The Social Contract with a famous line: "Man is born free; and everywhere he is in chains." Rousseau argued that rightful power comes from the "general will" of the people as a whole, a more collective idea than Locke's.

Limits on power

In The Spirit of the Laws (1748), the French writer Montesquieu argued that liberty is safest when the power to make laws, the power to carry them out and the power to judge are held by different people, so that one power can check another. This is the separation of powers.

Religion and the state

In 1762 a court in Toulouse condemned Jean Calas, a Protestant cloth merchant, for killing his son to stop him from becoming a Catholic. His son had most likely taken his own life. Calas was tortured and executed. Voltaire, one of the most famous writers in Europe, took up the case, published a Treatise on Tolerance in 1763, and campaigned until Calas was declared innocent in 1765. The argument was religious toleration: no government should force a faith on anyone or punish people for what they believe.

How we know: read the words

Every quotation in this section is from the writers' own books, which are still in print and free online. When you describe a claim, go back to the words: Locke's "without his own consent" is a claim about where a government's right to rule comes from, and it argues against divine right.

Use this in your answer
Strongest tool, with its mechanism
Say what made a ruler legitimate, before and after. The old answer was God and birth; the new answer was reason and the consent of the people.
Best evidence of exclusion or limit
The writers disagreed. Locke protected individual rights; Rousseau stressed the people as a whole; few of them wanted to abolish kings, and most did not mean their words for everyone (section 04).
Comparison hook
Against the Mandate of Heaven: Chinese rulers could also lose the right to rule by governing badly, but the mandate still came from Heaven. Locke's consent comes from the people.

Terms to use precisely

Natural rights
Rights people are born with, such as life, liberty and property, which no government gives or may take away.
Social contract
The idea that government is an agreement: people accept a government so that it will protect their rights.
Separation of powers
Dividing the powers to make laws, carry them out and judge among different people, so no one holds them all.
Religious toleration
The idea that no government should force a faith on anyone or punish people for their beliefs.
03

The Ideas Travel, Before the Revolutions

1751 to 1810  ·  Print, salons, letters

The ideas spread through print and conversation across Europe and the Atlantic, questioned tradition in every part of life, and came before the revolutions that began in 1775, 1789, 1791 and 1810. They gave revolutionaries their words, and they turned "the people" into the nation.

Print, salons and letters

From 1751 to 1772, Denis Diderot and his partners published the Encyclopédie in Paris: seventeen volumes of articles and eleven of pictures that tried to explain everything by reason, from how to make glass or mount a fan to what a government owes its people. The French government banned it in 1759, and the Pope condemned it, but the last ten volumes of articles came out together in 1765, and printers in other countries copied it: a second edition was printed in Lucca, Italy, from 1758.

The ideas also traveled in newspapers and pamphlets, in London's coffeehouses, in letters between writers across Europe and the Americas, and in the conversation gatherings of Paris called salons, which were often run by women. In 1784 the German philosopher Immanuel Kant summed up the attitude in a motto: "Dare to know," which he explained as having the courage to use your own understanding.

Before the revolutions

The College Board's words are that Enlightenment thought "often preceded" revolutions. The American Revolution began in 1775, the French Revolution in 1789, the Haitian Revolution in 1791, and the wars of independence in Spanish America in 1810. In Bogotá, Antonio Nariño secretly printed a Spanish translation of France's new declaration of rights (in 1793 or 1794) and was arrested for it in 1794; he later became one of the leaders of New Granada's independence.

Common mistake to avoid

Do not write that the Enlightenment caused the revolutions. The ideas came first and gave revolutionaries words to explain what they wanted, but taxes, debt, war, slavery and colonial grievances caused them too. Topic 5.2 is about those causes.

The people become a nation

If a state belongs to its people, a new question follows: which people? More and more, the answer was the nation, a people who saw themselves as one (Topic 5.2 explains what held a nation together). In 1789 the French revolutionaries wrote that "the principle of all sovereignty resides essentially in the nation." Over the next century, nationalism, loyalty to your nation above a king or an empire, became one of the strongest forces shaping states and empires: it helped build new countries and pulled at empires of many peoples. Topic 5.2 tells that story.

Use this in your answer
Strongest tool, with its mechanism
Context, not cause. Enlightenment ideas spread and came before the revolutions, so they are the intellectual and ideological context the revolutions happened in.
Best evidence of exclusion or limit
Most people in 1789 could not read the Encyclopédie. They met the ideas, if at all, through talk, sermons, pamphlets and rumor.
Comparison hook
Against the printing press of the 1400s: the technology was old by 1750. What was new was how much was printed, and the network of readers, salons and letters around it.

Terms to use precisely

Encyclopédie
The French encyclopedia of 1751 to 1772, edited by Diderot, which explained everything by reason and was banned and printed anyway.
Salon
A conversation gathering in a private home, often run by a woman, where writers and readers discussed ideas.
Sovereignty
The highest power in a state.
Nationalism
Loyalty to your nation, a people who see themselves as one, above a king or an empire.
04

Pressing the Words Further

1787 to 1888  ·  Abolition, serfs, voters

The words sounded like everyone, but the men who wrote them often did not mean everyone. Over the next century, reform movements driven by Enlightenment ideas and by religious faith pressed the same words until rights reached the enslaved, the serfs and many more voters, slowly and unevenly.

The words were bigger than their writers

Locke owned shares in the Royal African Company, an English company that traded enslaved Africans, in the 1670s. Rousseau wrote in Emile (1762) that "a woman's education must therefore be planned in relation to man." Most of the men who wrote and read the new ideas did not mean them for women, enslaved Africans or peasants, though a few did: the French philosopher Condorcet argued for women's rights in 1790. Yet the words were written down, and anyone could ask the philosophers' own question: by what right am I left out?

Abolition: ideas and faith together

In 1787 twelve men in London, nine of them Quakers, a Christian group whose members had come to see slavery as a sin, founded the Society for Effecting the Abolition of the Slave Trade. Its seal showed a kneeling African man in chains and the words "Am I not a man and a brother?", and the potter Josiah Wedgwood turned it into a medallion that supporters wore. Olaudah Equiano, who had been enslaved, published his life story in 1789 and traveled across Britain speaking against the trade.

Abolition came country by country. Britain banned the slave trade in 1807. In 1834 it began ending slavery in most of its empire, though freed people had to keep working for their former owners as unpaid "apprentices" until 1838. France abolished slavery in its colonies in 1794, brought it back in 1802, and ended it again in 1848. Brazil was the last country in the Americas, in 1888. The enslaved people of Saint-Domingue ended slavery there by revolution, which is Topic 5.2.

Serfs and voters

Serfdom ended too. In 1848 the Austrian Empire freed its peasants from the forced labor they owed their lords. In 1861 Tsar Alexander II of Russia freed more than 20 million serfs. He had reportedly told Russian nobles in 1856 that it was better to end serfdom "from above" than to wait for it to end "from below," and Russia's defeat in the Crimean War pushed him too. The freed serfs had to pay for their land over many years.

Suffrage, the right to vote, widened in steps. In 1848 France gave the vote to all adult men. Britain widened voting among men in 1832, 1867 and 1884, and even then many men could not vote.

Common mistake to avoid

Do not write that every expansion of rights came from reformers below. Some came from campaigns and revolutions; some came from rulers like Alexander II, who feared what would happen if they did not act. When you explain a change, say who made it happen and why.

Use this in your answer
Strongest tool, with its mechanism
Name both engines. The reform movements were driven by Enlightenment ideas about natural rights and by religious faith, and they took decades.
Best evidence of exclusion or limit
Ending slavery or serfdom in law did not end hard labor or poverty. Freed people and freed serfs often stayed poor, and other kinds of forced labor went on in many places.
Comparison hook
Against Topic 4.4: the plantation economy had made the Atlantic slave trade huge. By 1888 every country in the Americas had abolished slavery in law.

Terms to use precisely

Abolition
Ending slavery, or ending the slave trade, by law.
Quakers
A Christian group whose members, by the late 1700s, led some of the first campaigns against slavery.
Serfdom
A system in which peasants were bound to the land and owed labor to a lord.
Suffrage
The right to vote.
05

Women's Demands, and What Stayed

1791 to 1900  ·  De Gouges, Wollstonecraft, Seneca Falls

Women pressed the same words too, because the men who wrote about the rights of man often meant only men. Their demands challenged both political and gender hierarchies. By 1900 much had changed, and much had not.

Three demands

In September 1791 the French writer Olympe de Gouges rewrote France's declaration of rights as the Declaration of the Rights of Woman and of the Female Citizen. Where the declaration of 1789 said "men are born and remain free and equal in rights," her first article said: "Woman is born free and remains equal to man in rights." The revolutionary government executed her in November 1793.

In 1792 the English writer Mary Wollstonecraft published A Vindication of the Rights of Woman. She quoted Rousseau, who had warned that if women were educated like men, "the less power will they have over us," and answered: "I do not wish them to have power over men; but over themselves." Her claim was that women can reason, so they must be educated to think and decide for themselves.

In 1840 the American abolitionist Lucretia Mott, a Quaker minister, went to an antislavery convention in London as a delegate and was refused a seat because she was a woman. Elizabeth Cady Stanton, there with her husband, a delegate, sat with Mott and the other women, apart from the men. In July 1848 the two of them, with three other women, organized a women's rights convention in Seneca Falls, New York. Its Declaration of Sentiments, drafted by Stanton with members of the M'Clintock family, copied the Declaration of Independence and added two words: "all men and women are created equal." It complained that a woman had never been "permitted ... to exercise her inalienable right to the elective franchise," the vote. The resolution demanding the vote was the only one not passed unanimously; one hundred women and men signed the declaration.

This was feminism emerging: the claim that women and men should have equal rights. It challenged political hierarchy, who votes and who rules, and gender hierarchy, who is in charge at home and under the law. Under the law of the time, the Seneca Falls declaration complained, a married woman was "civilly dead."

What stayed by 1900

By 1900, kings and emperors still ruled most of Europe and much of the world, many of them sharing power with elected parliaments. Slavery had been abolished across the Americas, but other kinds of forced labor went on in many places. Many countries let some or all men vote. Women could vote in national elections almost nowhere: New Zealand, in 1893, was the first self-governing country where all adult women could vote in national elections.

So the Enlightenment changed the answer to "who does a state belong to?": to its people, by reason and consent, not to a king by God's will. The question it taught people to ask kept working long after the philosophers were gone. That is the change. How slowly the rights reached, and how many people were still waiting in 1900, is the continuity.

Common mistake to avoid

Do not write that rights reached everyone in 1789. The declaration of 1789 left women out of citizenship, France brought back slavery in 1802, and women voted almost nowhere in 1900. But do not swing to "nothing changed": the claim that rights belong to people by birth was new, and people used it.

The claim that women and men should have equal rights.

Continuity and Change

The question changed before the kings did

Before the Enlightenment, rulers said their power came from God, tradition and birth. Locke argued in 1689 that people are born with natural rights and that no one may be put under a government's power without consent; Montesquieu (1748) argued for the separation of powers, and Voltaire (1763) for religious toleration. What changed was what made a ruler legitimate. What continued was that kings still ruled almost everywhere.

Context

The ideas came before the revolutions

The ideas spread through the Encyclopédie (1751 to 1772, banned in France and reprinted in Italy), salons, coffeehouses and letters, and came before the revolutions of 1775, 1789, 1791 and 1810. Nariño's printed translation of the French declaration in Bogotá shows them crossing the Atlantic. If a state belongs to its people, the people could be the nation, and nationalism became a major force of the 1800s.

Continuity and Change

Reformers pressed the words further, slowly

Driven by natural rights and by faith, reformers founded the London antislavery society of 1787 (nine of its twelve founders were Quakers). Britain began ending slavery in most of its empire in 1834 and Brazil, last in the Americas, in 1888. Serfdom ended in Austria in 1848 and Russia in 1861; France gave all adult men the vote in 1848. Each change took decades.

Claims and Evidence

Women kept the words and changed who they covered

De Gouges (1791) wrote that "woman is born free and remains equal to man in rights"; Wollstonecraft (1792) wanted women to have power "over themselves"; the Seneca Falls declaration (1848) said "all men and women are created equal" and demanded the vote. Their claims challenged political and gender hierarchies, but by 1900 women could vote in national elections almost nowhere.

01

The Recipe, and America

1763 to 1787  ·  Grievance, words, chance

Many people read Enlightenment writers and went on obeying their kings. A revolution came when three things met: a grievance many people felt, the words to name it, and a chance to act. America had all three first.

Why ideas were not enough

Topic 5.1 showed that the new ideas about natural rights and the consent of the people had spread across Europe and the Americas by 1789. But reading a book is not the same as risking your life. Across this topic, three ingredients turn up again and again. The first is a grievance: a wrong that many people feel, such as an unfair tax, a privilege for a few, slavery, or a law that shuts people out of power. The second is the words: a document that names the grievance in the language of rights, something people can read aloud and rally around. The third is a chance: a moment when the old ruler is broke, far away, at war, or gone.

Common mistake to avoid

Do not write that the Enlightenment caused the revolutions. The College Board says Enlightenment ideas "influenced resistance" and that "discontent with monarchist and imperial rule" pushed people toward new kinds of government. The ideas were one cause. Name the others.

The grievance and the words

Britain won the Seven Years' War in 1763, but it came out of the war deep in debt. Parliament decided its thirteen American colonies should help pay for the soldiers who guarded them, and it taxed them with laws such as the Sugar Act of 1764 and the Stamp Act of 1765, which taxed newspapers, legal papers and playing cards. The colonists had no members in Parliament, so they answered with a slogan: "no taxation without representation." Protest reached ordinary homes: English potters even sold colonists teapots painted "No Stamp Act."

On 4 July 1776 the Continental Congress adopted the Declaration of Independence. It argued that "all men are created equal," that governments get "their just powers from the consent of the governed," and that when a government destroys people's rights, "it is the Right of the People to alter or to abolish it." That is the social contract from Topic 5.1, turned into a reason to break away.

The chance, and what it created

Britain was an ocean away, and its victory in 1763 had pushed France out of North America, so the colonists no longer needed British soldiers to protect them. Once the fighting began in 1775, France, Britain's old rival, joined the war on the American side in 1778 with money, soldiers and a navy, which helped the colonists win. By 1783 Britain had recognized the independence of the United States. In 1787 the new country wrote a constitution for a republic: a country with no king, whose leaders are elected.

The College Board calls the American Revolution and its republic "a model and inspiration for a number of the revolutions that followed." It was also limited. The republic kept slavery, and most states let only men who owned property vote.

02

France: The Bill Comes Due

1788 to 1804  ·  A kingdom remade

France's help was crucial to the American victory, and paying for it, on top of older war debts, pushed the French king to the edge of bankruptcy. One revolution's effect became the next one's chance.

The chance came from America

France had spent heavily on the American war, on top of the debts of earlier wars, and by 1788 King Louis XVI's government was nearly bankrupt. To raise new taxes, the king called the Estates-General, an assembly of the three "estates" of France: the clergy, the nobles, and everyone else. It had not met since 1614.

The grievance

The first two estates had privileges. The clergy and the nobles paid little in taxes, and the Third Estate, most of the people of France, from rich lawyers to poor peasants, paid most of them. After a bad harvest in 1788, the price of bread, the main food of the poor, soared. When the Estates-General met in May 1789, the Third Estate refused to be outvoted by the other two, and in June it declared itself the National Assembly. On 14 July a Paris crowd stormed the Bastille, a royal fortress and prison, to seize its gunpowder.

The words

In August 1789 the Assembly ended the nobles' special privileges and adopted the Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen. Its first article reads, "Men are born and remain free and equal in rights." Its third reads, "The principle of all sovereignty resides essentially in the nation." Sovereignty is the highest power in a state, and the declaration said it belonged to the nation, not to the king.

How we know: compare the documents

Put two revolutionary documents side by side and read them closely. Say each one's argument in your own words, then find one way the arguments are alike and one way they differ, and explain the difference by the grievance each document answered. That is the move the College Board's skill 3.C asks for.

What it created

France abolished its monarchy in 1792 and became a republic, and in January 1793 it executed the king. At war with much of Europe and afraid of enemies at home, the government began the Terror (1793 to 1794), during which it killed thousands of people it called enemies of the revolution. In 1799 a general, Napoleon Bonaparte, took power, and in 1804 he crowned himself emperor. His armies carried the revolution's laws, such as the end of noble privilege, across Europe, and they also made many Europeans hate French rule.

Haiti: The Words Claimed

1791 to 1825  ·  Saint-Domingue becomes Haiti

France's declaration said men are born free and equal. In Saint-Domingue, the people France held in slavery claimed those words for themselves, rose up first, and won a country of their own.

The grievance

Saint-Domingue, on the western part of the Caribbean island of Hispaniola, was France's most valuable colony. Its sugar and coffee were grown by enslaved Africans and their children, worked and punished brutally on plantations. Around 1789 the colony held more than 400,000 enslaved people and fewer than 80,000 free people, white colonists and free people of color (free people of African or mixed descent) together: more than five enslaved people for every free person.

The words, and the chance

When the 1789 declaration reached the colony, free people of color asked the Assembly in Paris for the equal rights it promised. The Assembly left the question to the colonists, and the white planters refused. In 1790 a free man of color, Vincent Ogé, led a small revolt for those rights, and early in 1791 he was executed. Paris granted free people of color equal rights only in April 1792, after the revolution in the colony had begun.

With France in revolution and the colony's white planters divided, enslaved people in the northern plain rose up in August 1791. They burned plantations across the northern plain. Toussaint Louverture, who had been born enslaved and had won his freedom before the revolution, became the revolution's most famous leader. In 1793, fighting on Spain's side against France, he announced: "I want Liberty and Equality to reign in Saint Domingue."

Common mistake to avoid

Do not write that France gave Haiti its freedom. Enslaved people rose up in 1791. French officials in the colony began freeing enslaved people in 1793 to keep their side's soldiers, and in February 1794 the government in Paris abolished slavery in all French colonies, after two and a half years of fighting. Toussaint changed sides to France a few months later. Haitians then won their independence by defeating Napoleon's army.

What it created

In 1802 Napoleon brought slavery back in other French colonies, such as Guadeloupe, and sent an army to retake Saint-Domingue. Toussaint was arrested and died in a French prison in the mountains in 1803. His officer Jean-Jacques Dessalines fought on, and yellow fever and the war destroyed the French army. On 1 January 1804 Dessalines declared independence and gave the country its old Taíno name, Haiti.

Haiti was the second independent country in the Americas, after the United States, and the first founded by people who had freed themselves from slavery. Its effects reached other countries. In April 1803, with his army in Saint-Domingue dying of yellow fever and a new war with Britain coming, Napoleon sold Louisiana to the United States. Countries that allowed slavery feared Haiti's example: the United States did not recognize Haiti until 1862, and in 1825 France demanded 150 million francs, later cut to 90 million, as the price of recognizing Haiti's independence. And in 1816 Haiti helped the next revolution (section 04).

Spanish America: An Empire With No King

1808 to 1826  ·  Bolívar and the new states

Creoles in Spain's American empire had grievances for decades. Their chance came when Napoleon, the French Revolution's general, took Spain's king, and the last revolution's creation, Haiti, helped them win.

The grievance

In Spain's American empire, creoles, people of Spanish descent born in the Americas, owned land, mines and businesses, but the highest offices in the church, the army and the government went to peninsulares, men born in Spain. Spain also controlled what the colonies could grow, make and trade. Simón Bolívar, a creole from a rich Caracas family, wrote that Spain "has kept us in a sort of permanent infancy with regard to public affairs": treated like children, kept out of running their own affairs.

The chance

In 1808 Napoleon invaded Spain and forced its king from the throne, putting his own brother in his place. Spanish America suddenly had no rightful king to obey. In 1810 creole leaders set up governments of their own, in Caracas and in Buenos Aires, at first in the name of the captive king, and many soon moved to full independence. In Mexico a priest, Miguel Hidalgo, called on Native and mestizo villagers to rise up.

The words, and help from Haiti

The wars went badly at first. In 1815, beaten and in exile on the British island of Jamaica, Bolívar wrote a long letter to a British reader, now called the Letter from Jamaica. "The hatred that the Peninsula has inspired in us," he wrote, "is greater than the ocean between us," and the tie to Spain "has been severed." He also doubted that governments "wholly representative," chosen entirely by the people's votes, suited Spanish America yet.

Then Haiti helped. In 1816 Haiti's president, Alexandre Pétion, gave Bolívar guns, supplies and men for a new expedition, and asked him in return to end slavery in the lands he freed.

What it created

By 1826 Spain had lost every colony in the Americas except Cuba and Puerto Rico. Mexico, Gran Colombia, Peru, Chile, the provinces of the Río de la Plata and others became independent countries, and Brazil broke away from Portugal in 1822 with an emperor of its own. Creole elites took the top places. Most of the new countries kept a sharp social hierarchy, and slavery lasted in several of them for decades: Colombia ended it in 1851, Venezuela and Peru in 1854.

New Ideas, and the Nation

1789 to 1878  ·  Liberalism, democracy, nationalism

Some of the revolutions' longest-lasting effects were ideas: new answers to how a country should be run, and a new loyalty, to the nation, that joined some states together and pulled peoples away from empires.

New ways to govern

Behind all four revolutions was anger at kings and empires, and the revolutions left behind new answers to an old question: how should a country be run? One was the republic, with a written constitution, a set of basic laws that even the government must follow. Another was liberalism: the belief that a constitution and an elected parliament should limit the government and protect each person's rights, such as free speech, a free press and property. Many liberals in the 1800s still wanted only men with property to vote. A third was democracy, rule by the people, which pushed for the vote to reach all men.

These ideas did not stay on paper. They helped set off new revolutions across Europe in 1830 and 1848, when crowds demanded constitutions, parliaments and wider voting. Most of the uprisings of 1848 were crushed within two years, but the demands did not go away.

From "the people" to the nation

The revolutions said that a state belongs to its people. That raised a new question: which people? More and more, people answered with the nation: everyone who shared a language, a religion, customs and a homeland. Nationalism put loyalty to the nation above loyalty to a king or an empire.

Governments learned to use that loyalty. In 1793, at war with much of Europe, revolutionary France ordered that "all Frenchmen are in permanent requisition for the service of the armies": young men would fight, married men would make weapons, women would make tents and work in hospitals, and old people would go to public squares to preach "the unity of the Republic." But nationalism could also be turned against a government, by people who felt they were a nation without a country of their own.

Nationalism joins states

Italy was split into several states, some ruled by Austria. Italian nationalists, among them Count Cavour, the chief minister of the kingdom of Piedmont-Sardinia, and the soldier Giuseppe Garibaldi, whose volunteers took Sicily and Naples in 1860, joined most of Italy into one kingdom in 1861, and added Rome in 1870. Joining states into one is called unification.

Germany was split into more than thirty states. In 1848 German liberals met in Frankfurt to unite Germany with an elected parliament and a constitution, and failed. Then Otto von Bismarck, the chief minister of Prussia, told Prussia's lawmakers in 1862 that the great questions of the time are not decided "by speeches and majority resolutions," which had been "the big mistake of 1848 and 1849," but "by iron and blood." After three wars, against Denmark, Austria and France, the German Empire was proclaimed on 18 January 1871 in the Hall of Mirrors at Versailles, the palace Louis XIV built.

Nationalism breaks an empire

The Ottoman Empire ruled many peoples in the Balkans, in southeastern Europe, Muslim and Christian. Serbs rebelled and won the right to rule themselves. Greeks rose up in 1821, and in 1830 Britain, France and Russia declared Greece an independent state; it became a kingdom under a Bavarian prince in 1832. Other Balkan peoples followed, and in 1878 Serbia, Montenegro and Romania were recognized as independent.

The Ottoman government answered with a loyalty of its own, Ottomanism. In 1856 it promised equal treatment to all its subjects, Muslim or not, and in 1869 a law made every subject an Ottoman citizen. The idea was one loyalty for everyone, to the empire, instead of to a nation. It did not stop the Balkan nations from leaving. The idea reached beyond Europe, too: in the Spanish-ruled Philippines, Filipino writers in the Propaganda Movement of the 1880s and 1890s, José Rizal among them, demanded equal rights with Spaniards, and Spain executed Rizal in 1896, during a revolution against its rule.

Common mistake to avoid

Do not write that nationalism only built countries. The same loyalty joined Italy and Germany together and pulled Greece and Serbia away from the Ottoman Empire. A cause can push in opposite directions.

Coal, Iron, and Not Enough Wood

1600s to 1709  ·  What nature gave

Britain had plenty of coal and iron, often close together and close to water, and too little wood. The shortage of wood pushed Britons to burn coal, and then to make iron with it.

How things were made in 1750

In 1750 almost everything people owned was made by hand. Most thread and cloth were made in homes. Under the putting-out system, a merchant brought raw wool or cotton to families in the countryside, who spun and wove it on their own wheels and looms, and he came back to collect the work and pay for each piece. Power came from human muscles, horses, wind and water wheels. Much of the world's cotton cloth came from India, and Britain's cotton makers grew up copying it.

Over the next century Britain built a new way of making things. The College Board lists seven factors that led to it. Two come from nature: waterways, and where coal, iron and timber were found. Five were built by people: towns, better farming, laws that protected property, resources from abroad, and saved-up money. This chapter takes them in that order.

Too little wood

By the 1600s most of Britain's old forests had been cut down, as a growing population cleared land for farms. Wood became expensive. That mattered, because wood was the fuel of the old economy. It heated homes, and it made charcoal, the fuel used to heat iron ore until the iron could be separated out. Much of the iron Britain used was bought from Sweden, and later from Russia, where forests were still large.

Britain did have coal, in huge beds, much of it close to the surface and close to rivers or the sea. So Britons burned coal instead of wood. By 1700 coal gave England about half of all its energy, much of it burned in fireplaces for heating and cooking.

Common mistake to avoid

Do not write that Britain industrialized because it had coal. Coal was one of seven factors, and China had coal too. A strong answer says what coal made cheaper or possible, and names other factors that made it worth using.

Iron made with coal

Coal could not simply replace charcoal in an iron furnace: its impurities spoiled the iron. In 1709, at Coalbrookdale, a valley beside the River Severn in Shropshire, Abraham Darby made iron using coke, a fuel made by baking coal to drive the impurities off. Coalbrookdale had coal and iron ore close by and a river to carry what it made. At first coke iron was used mostly for cast-iron pots. It spread slowly, mainly after the 1750s, as charcoal iron and imported iron grew dearer, and over the following decades ironmakers learned to make better iron with coal.

How we know: the furnace is still there

The furnace where Darby first made iron with coke survives at Coalbrookdale, rebuilt and enlarged in 1777, part of the Ironbridge Gorge World Heritage Site. Historic England, which lists the site, dates Darby's first iron made with coke there to 1709. Historians argue about how much the wood shortage caused the switch to coal and how much growing demand for fuel did; both were at work.

Water: Moving Heavy Things and Turning Wheels

1700s to 1761  ·  What nature gave, improved

Before railways, the cheapest way to move something heavy was to float it, and the fastest way to power a machine was a water wheel. Britain had coast, rivers and fast streams, and people added canals.

Coast, rivers and streams

Coal is heavy, and so is iron. Carrying them by cart on the roads of the 1700s was slow and expensive. Carrying them by water was cheap. Britain had a long coastline and rivers that boats could travel, and coal from the mines near Newcastle went by ship down the coast to London.

Fast streams mattered too. A stream could turn a water wheel, and a water wheel could turn machines all day. The first factories were built beside fast water, as section 05 shows.

Canals: water improved by people

Where nature had put no river, people dug one. A canal is a waterway dug by hand. The Duke of Bridgewater owned coal mines at Worsley, a few miles from Manchester. In 1759 and 1760 Parliament passed acts allowing him to build a canal. In 1761 it opened from Worsley to Stretford, on the edge of Manchester, and the price of coal in Manchester soon fell by about half. By 1765 it reached the town itself.

Other canal builders followed. Within a few decades canals linked coalfields, industrial towns and ports across England. Cheap fuel and cheap carriage drew mills to the places the water reached.

Common mistake to avoid

"Britain had rivers" is a fact, not an explanation. Add what the water did and what that made cheaper, with a "so."

Food, People and Towns

1700s to 1801  ·  What people built

British farmers grew more food on the same land. More food fed more people, a smaller share of them farmed, and towns filled with workers and buyers.

Better farming

Farmers had long left fields empty every few years to let the soil rest. British farmers learned to plant turnips and clover instead. Turnips fed cattle and sheep through the winter, and clover made the soil richer. More animals meant more manure for the fields, and more manure meant more grain. These crops had been grown this way in Flanders first; British farmers spread them.

Landowners also fenced off the old shared village fields into private farms, often with an act of Parliament. Between 1750 and 1819 Parliament passed more than three thousand of these acts. This enclosure let a landowner farm his land the new way. It also took away the shared land that poorer villagers had used, and historians still argue about how many people it pushed off the land.

More people, fewer of them farming

More food fed more people. Britain's population grew from about 8 million in 1761 to about 10.5 million in 1801, the year of the first census. And a smaller share of those people farmed: in 1700 almost half of English working men worked on the land, and by about 1800 estimates range from about a third to about two in five.

How we know: counting backward

No full count of farm output exists before the 1860s. Historians work it out backward: Britain's population grew, people ate at least as well, and most of the food was still grown at home, so farms must have produced much more. The estimates differ widely. Some find that farm productivity more than doubled between 1700 and 1850; others, such as the economic historian Gregory Clark, find much slower growth, with imported food feeding part of the larger population.

Towns

Many of the people who did not farm lived in towns. Urbanization, the growth of towns and cities, put many workers close together, and gave them neighbors to sell to. Manchester, a town of about 23,000 people in 1773, counted 70,409 in the census of 1801.

Laws, Cotton and Capital

1624 to 1774  ·  What people built

Building a mill was a big risk. Laws that protected property, cotton from abroad and saved-up money made the risk worth taking, and the factors worked together.

Laws that protected property

In Britain, Parliament, where landowners and merchants sat, passed the laws that enclosed fields and allowed canals to be dug. A law of 1624 banned most monopolies but kept the patent: the crown could grant the true and first inventor the sole right to use a new invention for up to fourteen years. In 1769 Richard Arkwright patented a new spinning machine. A person who invested could expect the law to protect what he built.

The protection had limits. Arkwright's 1769 spinning patent ran out in 1783, and in 1785 a court canceled his 1775 patent on his carding and preparing machines, finding his written description of them unclear; the court also heard claims that other men had invented them.

Cotton from abroad

Cotton does not grow in Britain's climate. By the 1780s most of the cotton spun in Manchester was grown by enslaved people on plantations in the Caribbean and South America. After the 1790s more and more of it came from plantations in the United States. Britain used about 2.6 million pounds of raw cotton in 1760, almost all of it spun by hand, and about 51.6 million pounds in 1800. The factory's raw material came from far away, and most of it was grown by people who were not free. (In the 1780s a quarter or more came from the Ottoman Empire, through the port of Smyrna.)

Capital

Capital is money used to build something that makes more money. Merchants, bankers and landowners had grown rich from trade, including the Atlantic trade in enslaved people and the sugar they grew, and some were ready to risk their savings on mills and machines. Arkwright's partners, Samuel Need and Jedediah Strutt, were stocking makers who had grown rich in the hosiery trade. The partners spent thousands of pounds on machines and mills before the business made a profit, at a time when a carpenter earned no more than about £36 a year.

Common mistake to avoid

Do not write that the slave trade paid for the Industrial Revolution. Historians still argue about how much of Britain's capital came from slavery and the slave trade, so say "some" or "part." What historians agree on is that most of the cotton itself was grown by enslaved people.

How the factors combined

None of these factors made an industrial revolution by itself. Watch five of them meet in one project. The Bridgewater Canal needed coal under the ground at Worsley (nature). It needed water to float the coal on (nature, improved by people). It needed acts of Parliament, passed in 1759 and 1760, to allow it to be built (law). It needed the duke's money, and he had to borrow more to pay his workmen (capital). And it needed a growing town to buy the coal (urbanization). Cheap coal then made Manchester an even better place to build mills, and the new mills made the town grow faster still.

The Factory: One Place, One Job Each

1771 to 1835  ·  The factory system

The factors made it pay to build mills. The factory then changed work itself in two ways: it gathered production in one place, and it split the work into small, specialized jobs.

One place

In 1771, Arkwright and his partners built a cotton-spinning mill at Cromford, in Derbyshire's Derwent valley. A water wheel turned the machines. It was fed by a brook and by the Cromford Sough, a tunnel that drained the nearby lead mines; because its water came from underground, it was said never to freeze.

The mill did not send cotton out to spinners' homes. It brought the workers to the machines. Everyone came to one building, at the hours the mill set, under the eye of an overseer. The village was too small to supply enough workers, so Arkwright built houses for them. Most of his workers were women and children, some as young as seven. This was the factory system: production concentrated in a single place, around one source of power. UNESCO, which protects the valley as a World Heritage Site, says it "saw the birth of the factory system."

Common mistake to avoid

Do not call Cromford the first factory, or say the steam engine started the factory system. A silk mill at Derby had some of the same features in the 1720s, and Cromford ran on water. Steam power is Topic 5.5.

One job each

Inside a cotton mill, the work was split into steps. Raw cotton was cleaned, then carded (combed so its fibers lay straight), drawn out, twisted into a loose rope called roving, and spun into thread. Cromford began with machines for spinning, and in 1775 Arkwright patented machines for the earlier steps too. From then on each step after cleaning had its own machine, tended by workers who did that job and no other. This is specialization of labor.

Adam Smith, a Scottish thinker, showed why it mattered in 1776, using a small hand workshop that made pins. Ten men shared about eighteen steps, each doing only one, two, or three of them, and together they "could make among them upwards of forty-eight thousand pins in a day." A worker who had not learned the trade, working alone, he wrote, "could scarce ... make one pin in a day, and certainly could not make twenty." Because each job was small and simple, it could be learned quickly, so a mill could hire workers with no training, many of them children.

How we know: writers of the time

In 1835 Andrew Ure, a writer who admired factories, defined one as "the combined operation of many orders of work-people, adult and young, in tending with assiduous skill a system of productive machines continuously impelled by a central power": many kinds of workers, many machines, one power source. He was praising the system, so read him for what a factory was, not for how it felt to work there.

The World's Makers in 1750

About 1750  ·  The starting point

In 1750 almost everything was made by hand, at home or in workshops, and most of the world's manufactured goods were made in China and India.

Made by hand

In 1750 a shirt, a pot, a plow or a ship was made by people using hand tools, at home or in small workshops. This is hand production. Power came from muscles, animals, wind and water wheels. That was true in Britain, too: Arkwright's mill at Cromford was still twenty years away.

The places that made the most were the places with the most people and the most skilled craftworkers. India's cotton cloth, from fine thin muslins to cheap printed calicoes, was sold across the Indian Ocean, in the Middle East, in West Africa and in Europe. Chinese silk, porcelain and cotton cloth filled China's own huge markets and traveled abroad. Indian shipbuilders built ocean-going ships of teak.

Who made how much

Nobody counted every piece of cloth in the world in 1750. But the economic historian Paul Bairoch made careful estimates, working backward from what is known about people and their work. He estimated that around 1750 China made about a third of all the world's manufactured goods and India about a quarter. All of Europe together made about a quarter. Britain made under 2 percent.

How we know: estimates, not counts

Bairoch's figures were published in 1982 and reprinted in Paul Kennedy's The Rise and Fall of the Great Powers (1987). They are built from guesses about population and output, and historians who use them say they are rough. Use them to show the size of the change, never as exact amounts.

02

Keeping the Secret, and Losing It

1789 to 1824  ·  The methods leave Britain

Britain tried to keep its new methods at home by law. They left anyway, first in the heads of skilled people.

The laws

Britain's leaders knew the new machines were worth a great deal. British law banned skilled workers in the textile trades from leaving to work abroad, and banned the export of textile machines. The ban on workers leaving was repealed in 1824; the last bans on exporting machinery were lifted in 1843.

Samuel Slater

Samuel Slater learned the cotton trade as an apprentice at Jedediah Strutt's mill at Belper, in Derbyshire. Strutt had been Arkwright's partner, and his mills ran Arkwright's machines, so Slater learned them from the inside. In 1789 he sailed for the United States without telling anyone what he knew. At Pawtucket, Rhode Island, working with American merchants, he built water-powered spinning machines from memory. The mill that still stands there was begun in 1793 and enlarged several times afterward.

How we know: the mill is still there

The Slater Mill still stands at Pawtucket, part of the Blackstone River Valley National Historical Park, run by the National Park Service. It was begun in 1793 and enlarged several times by the 1830s, so a photograph of it today shows the enlarged mill, not the first one.

John Cockerill

In Belgium, John Cockerill, the son of an English machine maker, began building machines at Seraing, near Liège, in 1817, and added ironworks there in the 1820s. Liège sat on one of the richest coalfields on the continent of Europe, with rivers to carry goods. The pattern from Topic 5.3, coal near water, was waiting there.

Machines, Money and Coal

1820s to 1900  ·  Other parts of Europe, the US, Russia, Japan

Machines and money carried the methods too, and most took root near coal, or by rivers that could turn wheels: in other parts of Europe, in the United States, and later in Russia and Japan.

Other parts of Europe

The first steam railway in the German states, from Nuremberg to Fürth, opened in 1835 with a locomotive built in England. On the continent, factories grew where coal lay, as they had in Britain: around Liège in Belgium, in the Ruhr valley of the German states, and in northern France.

The United States

In the United States, wealthy Boston merchants paid for big cotton mills at Lowell, Massachusetts, from the 1820s, beside a river that could turn their wheels. Most of the workers were young women from New England farms. By 1900 the United States made more of the world's manufactured goods than any other country.

Russia and Japan

Russia and Japan came later, mostly after 1870. A Welsh engineer, John Hughes, built ironworks in the coal country of the southern Russian Empire, in what is today eastern Ukraine, in the early 1870s; the town that grew up around them was named Yuzovka, after him (today Donetsk). In Japan the Osaka Spinning Company opened in 1883 with cotton-spinning machines made in Britain, driven by steam power, and it ran day and night.

Common mistake to avoid

Do not write that industry "spread everywhere." The College Board names four places it spread to: other parts of Europe, the United States, Russia and Japan. Within them, it clustered near coal, or by rivers strong enough to turn water wheels. How the Russian and Japanese governments pushed industry on purpose is Topic 5.6.

The Shares Move

1750 to 1900  ·  What changed

Steam-powered factories made goods far faster and more cheaply than hands could, so the regions that had them made a fast-growing share of the world's goods, and the share made in Asia fell.

What changed

A share is a part of a whole. In Bairoch's estimates, Britain's share of the world's manufacturing went from under 2 percent in 1750 to about a fifth by 1860. By 1900 Europe and the United States together made more than four-fifths of the world's manufactured goods, and the United States had passed Britain as the largest maker.

Over the same years, China's share fell from about a third to about 6 percent, and India's from about a quarter to under 2 percent.

A share is not an amount

Imagine a pizza that grows much bigger. Your slice can stay the same size while your share of the pizza shrinks. The world was making far more goods in 1900 than in 1750. So a region's share can fall even while it keeps making goods, because the total grows faster. For India, though, Bairoch's estimates suggest it also made less per person over time: it lost ground, not only share.

Common mistake to avoid

Do not write that Asia "stopped manufacturing." The College Board's own sentence says Middle Eastern and Asian countries "continued to produce manufactured goods" while their share "declined." A falling share is the change; the making that went on is the continuity.

The Old Makers Kept Making

1750 to 1900  ·  What continued, and why the share fell

India, China and the Middle East kept making cloth, ships and other goods, much of it by hand. Their share shrank because the rest of the world made far more, because machine-made goods were cheaper, and because of the trade rules of the people in power, among other causes historians still argue about.

What continued

In India, hand weaving shrank, but it did not die. Weavers kept working cloth on handlooms, and many switched to cheaper machine-spun thread so they could keep weaving. The economic historian Tirthankar Roy finds that handloom cloth held a roughly steady share of India's cloth market from the 1890s to the 1930s, and in 1908, in one province of northern India alone, about a million people still lived from handloom weaving. In China, farm families kept weaving cloth at home, more and more of it from cheaper machine-made thread. In the Ottoman Empire, the number of handlooms in the Syrian cities of Aleppo and Damascus held steady from 1860 to 1914.

Indian shipbuilders kept building, too. At the Bombay dockyard, the shipyard of the city now called Mumbai, the Wadia family of master shipbuilders built teak warships for Britain's own Royal Navy. One of them, HMS Trincomalee, launched in 1817, is still afloat today in Hartlepool, England, where its museum calls it Britain's oldest warship still afloat.

Why the share fell

One reason the share fell is that the rest of the world made far more goods. Another is that the old makers lost buyers. Cloth from Lancashire's machines was cheaper than cloth woven by hand, even in India itself. In 1815 India sold about £1.3 million worth of cotton cloth abroad and bought only about £26,000 worth from England. By 1832 it sold less than £100,000 worth and bought more than £400,000 worth. From about 1830, India bought more cotton cloth than it sold. India, which had sold cloth to the world, now bought British cloth, and it sold raw cotton to British mills.

Power mattered too. Much of India was ruled by the British East India Company, and British cotton cloth entered India at a tax of only 2½ percent. Earlier, Britain had taxed Indian cloth heavily: around 1813, Indian calicoes paid taxes of about 70 to 85 percent of their value to be sold in Britain. Historians still argue about how much each cause mattered: cheaper machine-made goods, the trade rules of the people in power, and, in India, troubles that had begun before the factories did, such as the breakup of the Mughal Empire and years of drought.

The Middle East felt the same pressure. In 1838 English cotton cloth sold in Egypt for less than cloth from Egypt's own new mills. (Who built those mills, and why, is Topic 5.6.)

Shipbuilding followed the same path. Bombay's teak ships were among the best wooden ships of their day. But by the middle of the 1800s navies were turning to ships of iron, driven by steam, and they came mostly from British shipyards and engine works. Bombay's age of teak warships came to an end.

Common mistake to avoid

Do not give one cause as the whole answer, either way. "British machines were simply better" leaves out the trade rules; "colonial rule alone destroyed Indian industry" leaves out the cheaper machines and the weavers who kept weaving. A strong answer names more than one cause and says historians weigh them differently.

Energy Before 1750

About 1750  ·  The starting point

In 1750 almost all the energy used in the world came from things that grow, and from wind and water. Each had a limit.

Things that grow, blow and flow

In 1750 a farmer plowed with a horse or an ox, a family cooked and kept warm with wood, a ship crossed the ocean by sail, and a mill turned beside a river. Food gave energy to the muscles of people and animals. Wood gave fire. Wind and water turned some machines. All of these came, in the end, from the sun and the land: crops and forests had to grow, every year, to be used.

Each had a limit you could see. A horse gets tired and must be fed from land that could grow food for people. A forest takes decades to grow back. A sailing ship waits for the wind. A water wheel works only where a stream flows strongly, and turns slowly when the water runs low. Topic 5.3's example, Arkwright's mill at Cromford, ran on a water wheel fed by a brook and a mine drain.

How we know: the world's energy, measured

Historians of energy, such as Vaclav Smil, have estimated how much energy the world used and where it came from. Their figures show that in 1850 wood, crop waste and other things that grow gave more than 90 percent of the world's energy, and that they were still the largest source in 1900, when coal gave nearly half (Our World in Data).

Energy buried underground

There was another store of energy, underground. Coal and oil are each a fossil fuel: the remains of plants and tiny sea creatures, buried for millions of years, with energy stored inside them. Coal did not need land to grow this year. Britain already burned a great deal of it for heat (Topic 5.3). The trick was to turn its heat into motion.

The Steam Engine

1712 to the 1780s  ·  From a pump to a power

The steam engine began as a pump for flooded coal mines. Watt made it use far less coal and turn wheels, so factories could run on coal instead of rivers.

A pump for mines

As British miners dug deeper for coal, water flooded the mines. In 1712 Thomas Newcomen set up a steam engine near Dudley Castle, in the English Midlands, to pump it out. A fire boiled water into steam. The steam filled a cylinder under a piston; a spray of cold water turned the steam back into water, the air pressed the piston down, and a great wooden beam rocked to work the pump. It worked, but it burned a great deal of coal, because the cylinder was cooled and heated again on every stroke.

Watt's improvement

James Watt, an instrument maker at the University of Glasgow, was repairing a model Newcomen engine when he saw the waste. In 1765 he found the fix: cool the steam in a separate chamber, the separate condenser, so the cylinder could stay hot. He patented the idea in 1769. Watt's engines used less than a third of the coal of a Newcomen engine.

Then came the change that mattered most for factories. Watt's business partner, Matthew Boulton, wanted engines that could drive mill machinery. In the 1780s Boulton and Watt built engines that turned a wheel. A wheel can drive spinning machines, looms and hammers. A mill no longer had to sit beside a fast river: it could run on coal, wherever coal could be brought, and cotton mills crowded into towns such as Manchester.

Common mistake to avoid

Watt did not invent the steam engine. Newcomen's engines had pumped mines for half a century before Watt's patent. Watt made the steam engine use far less coal, and made it turn wheels. And steam did not start the Industrial Revolution: Cromford ran on water, and many mills used water power for decades after Watt.

More Coal, Then Oil

1854 to 1900  ·  The fossil fuels revolution

Machines let people use far more energy than ever before: coal gave about half the world's energy by 1900. Oil and the internal combustion engine came next, but their big effects came later.

How much more energy

Steam engines needed coal, and coal mines needed steam engines to pump them dry, so each made the other grow. Britain mined about 65 million tons of coal in 1854 and about 225 million tons in 1900. By about 1900, coal supplied about half of all the energy used in the whole world; things that grow, such as wood, gave about the other half. The College Board calls this the fossil fuels revolution: it "greatly increased the energy available to human societies."

A person who lived through it said so. In 1865 the British economist William Stanley Jevons wrote that "with coal almost any feat is possible or easy; without it we are thrown back into the laborious poverty of early times." He was worried that Britain's cheap coal would not last forever. (His words are this lesson's Primary Source.)

Oil, and an engine that burns fuel inside itself

In 1859 Edwin Drake drilled a well at Titusville, Pennsylvania, and struck oil. For the rest of the 1800s, most oil was refined into kerosene, a fuel for lamps.

A steam engine burns its fuel under a boiler, outside the engine. An internal combustion engine burns its fuel inside the engine itself, in the cylinder. In 1876 Nikolaus Otto built a four-stroke gas engine in Germany, and in 1886 Carl Benz patented a car driven by a small engine that burned a fuel made from oil.

How we know: counting cars

Government tables of the number of cars in the United States begin with the year 1900, when there were about 8,000 in the whole country. (No state yet required cars to be registered: New York was the first, in 1901.) The internal combustion engine's big changes, to cars, trucks, tractors and airplanes, came after 1900.

The Second Industrial Revolution

1850 to 1900  ·  Four new methods

After 1850, with so much energy to use, new methods made steel cheap, made chemicals in factories, sold electric power, and cut machine parts precisely enough to swap.

Steel

Steel is iron with a small, carefully measured amount of carbon. It is stronger and tougher than ordinary iron, but before the 1850s it was slow and costly to make in large amounts. In 1856 Henry Bessemer showed that blowing air through melted iron burned out the carbon and other impurities, and the burning kept the metal hot. The United States made about 69,000 tons of steel in 1870 and about 10 million tons in 1900; Britain went from about 215,000 tons to about 4.9 million. Steel became rails, bridges, ships and machines.

Chemicals

In 1856 an eighteen-year-old London chemistry student, William Perkin, made a purple dye, mauve, from chemicals that came from coal tar, a sticky leftover of coal. It was the first synthetic dye to sell well. German chemical companies such as BASF, founded in 1865, built laboratories whose job was to invent new dyes, and in 1897 BASF began selling indigo blue made in a factory instead of from a plant. By 1914 Germany made most of the world's dyes.

Electricity

In 1882 Thomas Edison's Pearl Street Station in New York began selling electric power, lighting about 400 lamps on its first day. Electric motors could let each machine run on its own motor, instead of on belts from one big steam engine. But the change was slow: in 1899, electric motors gave less than 5 percent of the power in American factories, and it took about two more decades for them to give half. Factories had to be rebuilt to use them well.

Precision machinery

Machine tools, such as lathes and milling machines, had begun to cut metal parts to exact sizes, so that one part fit any copy of the same machine; after 1850 the method spread widely. These are interchangeable parts. Standard screw threads, set early by Joseph Whitworth in Britain in 1841 and then by William Sellers in the United States in 1864, helped machines with interchangeable parts, "from typewriters to locomotives," as the American Society of Mechanical Engineers puts it, spread.

Common mistake to avoid

Do not say "by 1900 factories ran on electricity." Electric power was new and growing, but in 1899 it gave under 5 percent of American factory power. Steam, burning coal, still drove most machines.

Longer Reach

1830 to 1904  ·  Railroads, steamships and the telegraph

Railroads, steamships and the telegraph reached the interiors of continents and joined distant markets, which led to more trade and more migration.

Railroads

Before railroads, heavy goods moved cheaply only by water, so the middle of a continent, far from rivers and the sea, was hard to reach. The Liverpool and Manchester Railway, opened in England in 1830, was the world's first railway between two cities, with trains pulled by steam locomotives. Railways spread fast: Russia had 27 kilometers of railway in 1840 and more than 53,000 in 1900; Germany went from 469 kilometers in 1840 to more than 51,000 in 1900.

India's first passenger train ran from Bombay to Thane in 1853. The first railroad across the United States was finished at Promontory Summit, Utah, in 1869. Russia began the Trans-Siberian Railway in 1891, and by 1904 trains could cross to the Pacific Ocean. A railroad could reach farms, mines and towns where no river or canal went.

Steamships

Under sail, emigrants from Norway took about 53 days on average for the whole trip to America. By steamship, the Atlantic crossing from England took about 12 days in the 1860s, and the fastest ships of the 1870s crossed in about 7 or 8 days. The Suez Canal, opened in Egypt in 1869, cut the voyage from Europe to Asia; it was of little use to sailing ships, because the winds of the Red Sea were hard to sail against, so it mostly served steamships. Steamboats also pushed up rivers into the interior of continents: in 1854 the steamer Pleiad went far up the Niger and Benue rivers in West Africa, and none of its crew died, because they took quinine, a medicine against malaria. What European empires did with this reach is Unit 6.

The telegraph

A telegraph sends messages as pulses of electricity along a wire. In 1844 Samuel Morse sent "What hath God wrought?" from Washington to Baltimore. A cable under the Atlantic worked only a few weeks in 1858; from 1866 cables carried messages across it without a lasting break, and in 1870 another undersea cable, through the Red Sea, reached Bombay. A message that once traveled as fast as a ship could now travel in minutes, so merchants could know prices in distant markets within a day.

More trade, more migration

Cheaper, faster transport and fast news joined markets. In 1870 wheat cost 57.6 percent more in Liverpool, England, than in Chicago. By 1913 the gap was only 15.6 percent. Wheat grown in the middle of North America, carried by train to the coast and by steamship across the ocean, fed workers in Britain.

Trains and ships carried people, too. Migration means people moving to live in a new place. About 55 million Europeans moved to the Americas and Australia between 1850 and 1914. Between 1846 and 1940, more than 29 million Indians and more than 19 million Chinese moved to Southeast Asia and the lands around the Indian Ocean, and tens of millions of Chinese and Russians moved into Manchuria, Siberia and Central Asia. Steamships and railways made these journeys faster and cheaper. Why people moved, and what they found, is Unit 6.

How we know: prices and passengers

Economic historians compare the price of the same good in two markets: when the gap shrinks, the markets have joined. The wheat figures are from Kevin O'Rourke and Jeffrey Williamson. The migration counts come from passenger lists and port records, gathered by Timothy Hatton and Jeffrey Williamson for Europe and by Adam McKeown for Asia.

Why a State Builds Factories

The early 1800s  ·  The exception

Most early factories were built by private owners. A small number of governments built industry themselves, wanting armies to match stronger powers.

The rule, and the exception

In Britain, Richard Arkwright and his partners paid for the mill at Cromford (Topic 5.3). In the United States, Boston merchants paid for the big cotton mills at Lowell (Topic 5.4). Governments helped in other ways, with patents, roads and taxes on foreign goods, but private owners built and owned the factories.

A small number of governments did something different. They planned factories, paid for them, owned them and protected them. This is state-sponsored industry, and the College Board names it as the exception: "a small number of states and governments promoted their own state-sponsored visions of industrialization."

The fear behind it

By the early 1800s the countries with factories, steam engines and new weapons were becoming the strongest in the world. A ruler watching them could see two dangers: a country without industry could be beaten in war, or forced to sign treaties it did not want. Waiting for merchants to build factories might take too long. So a few rulers decided to build industry themselves, to make their states strong.

Egypt's Strategy

1805 to the 1830s  ·  Muhammad Ali

Muhammad Ali, Egypt's Ottoman governor, used the state's control of farming and cotton to pay for factories, and the factories supplied his army.

A governor who wanted power of his own

In 1798 a French army under Napoleon invaded Egypt and easily defeated its rulers, the Mamluks, a class of soldier-lords. The French left in 1801. Among the Ottoman troops sent to take Egypt back was Muhammad Ali, an officer from an Albanian family. In 1805 the Ottomans named him governor of Egypt, and by 1811, after his men killed the Mamluk leaders in the Cairo Citadel, he ruled alone.

Egypt was a province of the Ottoman Empire, and Muhammad Ali was the sultan's governor. Napoleon's victory had shown what a modern European army could do. Muhammad Ali wanted Egypt strong enough that he, and his family after him, could hold it, even against the sultan. Strength meant an army, and an army needed guns, uniforms and ships.

Crops paid for factories

His government took control of Egypt's farming. It told peasants what crops to plant, bought the crops at low prices, and sold them abroad at higher ones. When there is only one allowed buyer or seller, that is a monopoly. A new crop, long-staple cotton, first grown for sale in 1820, earned a great deal of money.

With that money, from the late 1810s, the government built factories: cotton mills, and works for weapons, sugar and other goods. It bought machines in Europe and hired European experts to run them. By the early 1830s about 30 cotton mills employed about 30,000 workers, many of them making cloth and uniforms for the army. To protect the mills, Muhammad Ali kept cheap British cloth out of Egypt.

How we know: the mills and their workers

The historian Joel Beinin, writing about the first Egyptian factory workers, gives the count: "By the early 1830s 30 cotton mills were in operation with a labor force of 30,000." Another recent study also counts about 30 state textile factories in the 1830s. The U.S. Library of Congress's history of Egypt adds that "Workers were drafted into factories to weave on government looms."

Drafted workers, drafted soldiers

The workers did not choose these jobs. They were drafted, the way Egyptian peasants had long been made to dig canals and repair dams. From 1822 peasants were drafted into the army too. Forcing people to serve in the army is conscription. The army reached about 130,000 soldiers by the early 1830s. The historian Khaled Fahmy writes that Muhammad Ali's schools, factories and hospitals "were all founded to serve it." In the 1820s Muhammad Ali also sent Egyptian students to Europe to learn its sciences.

Egypt's Result

1831 to the 1840s  ·  It worked, then it stopped

The army won wars until European powers stopped it. Then, with the army cut, the monopolies ended, almost no coal and drafted workers, most of the factories failed.

It worked

For a while the strategy did what Muhammad Ali wanted. In 1831 his army, led by his son Ibrahim, invaded Syria, and it beat the sultan's own armies more than once.

Then it stopped

Britain, Austria, Russia and Prussia did not want a strong Egypt breaking the Ottoman Empire apart. In 1840 they sided with the sultan, and British warships and Ottoman troops drove Ibrahim out of Syria. In 1841 the sultan made the governorship of Egypt hereditary in Muhammad Ali's family, passing to its eldest male, but Egypt had to give up its conquests except Sudan. The sultan's decree said: "In time of peace, 18,000 men will suffice." Egypt also had to accept a trade treaty that Britain had signed with the Ottoman sultan in 1838, which ended Muhammad Ali's monopolies and opened Egypt to cheap foreign goods.

The factories had problems of their own, too. Egypt had almost no coal, so many machines were turned by animals instead of steam engines. The drafted workers resisted the work, and the managers had little experience. By the mid-1840s, about a decade after their peak in the early 1830s, most of the new factories had failed. Egypt went on growing more and more cotton, almost all of it sold abroad, much of it to the mills of Britain. What that led to is Unit 6.

How we know: historians disagree

Historians weigh the causes differently. Joel Beinin lists four: "inexperienced management, lack of adequate natural resources (especially fuel), peasant resistance to factory discipline and competition from Europe." The historian Afaf Lutfi al-Sayyid Marsot stressed Britain's hostility to a strong Egypt. The economic historians Laura Panza and Jeffrey Williamson argue that, while they lasted, "his policies were successful." Most of the factories had failed by the mid-1840s.

Japan's Reforms

1853 to 1873  ·  Outside pressure, inside reform

American and European warships and unequal treaties pushed Japan's new Meiji government to reform the country before it built industry.

Ships and treaties

In 1853 an American naval officer, Commodore Matthew Perry, sailed warships into Edo Bay, near today's Tokyo, and demanded that Japan open its ports to trade. Japan had no navy that could stop him. Treaties in 1854 and 1858 opened Japanese ports to Americans and Europeans, let foreigners accused of crimes in Japan be tried in their own countries' courts, and took away Japan's control over its own trade. A tariff agreement of 1866 held Japan's taxes on most imports to about 5 percent, and Japan did not win back full control of them until 1911. A treaty forced on a weaker country that gives the stronger side rights it does not give back is an unequal treaty.

A new government

Some samurai blamed the shogun, the military ruler who governed Japan in the emperor's name, for giving in. In 1868 a group of young samurai overthrew him and governed in the name of the fifteen-year-old emperor, Meiji. This is the Meiji Restoration. In April 1868 the new government issued the Charter Oath, which promised: "Knowledge shall be sought throughout the world so as to strengthen the foundations of imperial rule." The government's slogan was "Rich country, strong army."

Reform first

The Meiji leaders changed Japan itself. In 1871 they abolished the domains of the old lords and made one central state. The samurai lost their privileges, and men of every class were drafted into a national army. A national school system began in 1872. A new land tax, paid in money instead of rice, gave the government money to spend. From 1871 to 1873 a group of leaders, the Iwakura Mission, toured the United States and Europe to study how they worked.

Japan Builds, Then Sells

1872 to 1895  ·  From state industry to regional power

Japan's government built railways, mills and shipyards, then sold most of them to private companies, and the strength it built made Japan a regional power.

The state builds

The government built Japan's first railway, from Tokyo to Yokohama, which opened in 1872 with British engineers. In the same year it opened the Tomioka Silk Mill, with French machines and a French engineer, Paul Brunat, to teach Japanese workers, mostly young women, to reel silk by machine, so the method could spread to other mills. It built telegraph lines, shipyards, mines, and factories for weapons and for goods such as cement, glass and cloth.

Farmers paid for much of this through the land tax. Some samurai rebelled against losing their place, and in 1877 the new army of drafted commoners crushed the last great revolt.

Then the state sells

Running so many businesses cost more than the government could afford. In 1880 it decided to sell most of them to private investors. Business families such as Mitsui and Mitsubishi bought them and grew into huge business groups called zaibatsu: Mitsubishi bought the Nagasaki shipyard in 1887, and Mitsui took over the Miike coal mine, the country's largest, in 1889. Private companies such as the Osaka Spinning Company (Topic 5.4) built more. The state started key industries; private companies carried them on and built much more.

A regional power

To win back its own courts, Japan rewrote its laws on French and German models, and in July 1894 Britain agreed to end the foreign courts in Japan from 1899, so that foreigners there would be tried by Japanese law, and other countries followed; Japan won back full control of its tariffs only in 1911. Weeks later, in 1894 and 1895, Japan fought China over Korea and won. In the Treaty of Shimonoseki, China gave up its claim over Korea, gave the island of Taiwan to Japan, and agreed to pay an indemnity of 200 million taels of silver (a tael is a Chinese measure of silver). Within a week, Russia, Germany and France demanded that Japan give back one more piece of land it had won, and Japan gave in, which angered Japan's leaders. Japan, forced to open its ports forty years before, was now a regional power, with a colony of its own. What empires did with that power is Unit 6.

How we know: the treaty's own words

The Treaty of Shimonoseki, signed on 17 April 1895, still exists. Its first article says China "recognizes definitively the full and complete independence and autonomy of Korea"; its fourth says China will pay "200,000,000 Kuping taels." Korea did not become a Japanese colony until 1910.

The Old Rules and a New Argument

Around 1750 to 1776  ·  Mercantilism and Adam Smith

Under mercantilism, governments controlled trade with tariffs, bans and monopolies. In 1776 Adam Smith argued that a country, like a sensible family, should buy what others make more cheaply.

Trade as a contest

In Topic 4.5 you met mercantilism, the idea that a kingdom grew strong by selling more to other countries than it bought from them, and by keeping its colonies' trade for itself. Governments acted on it. They put tariffs, taxes on imported goods, on foreign products; they banned some goods completely; and they gave chartered companies the sole right to trade in a region. Britain's East India Company held the sole right to carry on British trade with India and China.

To a mercantilist, trade was a contest. If another country sold more to you than it bought, it was winning and you were losing.

The tailor and the shoemaker

In 1776 Adam Smith, a Scottish thinker who had been a professor at Glasgow, published An Inquiry into the Nature and Causes of the Wealth of Nations. You met the book's pin workshop in Topic 5.3. Much of the rest of it is an attack on what Smith called "the mercantile system."

Smith started from an ordinary household. "It is the maxim of every prudent master of a family, never to attempt to make at home what it will cost him more to make than to buy. The tailor does not attempt to make his own shoes, but buys them of the shoemaker." What is sensible for a family, he went on, "can scarce be folly in that of a great kingdom. If a foreign country can supply us with a commodity cheaper than we ourselves can make it, better buy it of them ..."

Tariffs and bans, Smith argued, made ordinary buyers pay more so that a few producers could profit. "Consumption is the sole end and purpose of all production," he wrote, but "in the mercantile system, the interest of the consumer is almost constantly sacrificed to that of the producer."

How we know: Smith did not say "do nothing"

Later writers used the French phrase laissez-faire, roughly "let them do it," for ideas like Smith's. The phrase is not in his book. And Smith did not want government to do nothing: he wrote that the ruler has "three duties," defense, "an exact administration of justice," and "erecting and maintaining certain public works, and certain public institutions" that no private person would build.

Britain Chooses Free Trade

1815 to 1879  ·  The Corn Laws and after

Britain repealed its Corn Laws in 1846 and ended its tariffs on factory goods by 1860, partly persuaded by Smith's ideas. Much of Western Europe followed in the 1860s, but not every country, and not for good.

Bread and rents

Britain's Corn Laws kept cheap foreign grain out of the country ("corn" meant grain). A law of 1815 banned foreign wheat until British prices rose very high, to 80 shillings a quarter (a quarter is a measure of grain); from 1828 a tariff on imported grain, highest when British prices were low, took the ban's place. The laws kept grain prices high, which kept the rents of Britain's landowners high, and landowners filled Parliament.

In 1838 factory owners and merchants in Manchester started a campaign that became the Anti-Corn Law League, led by Richard Cobden and John Bright. It held meetings, printed pamphlets and raised money across the country. Cheap bread, its speakers said, would help workers; and countries that sold their grain to Britain would have money to buy British cloth.

Not every worker trusted them. Many Chartists, members of the workers' movement for the vote, argued that high prices meant high wages, so to them the manufacturers' cry of "cheap bread" really meant cheap labor: lower wages.

Famine and repeal

In 1845 a disease destroyed much of Ireland's potato crop, and famine began. Prime Minister Robert Peel, who had once defended the Corn Laws, decided they had to go. His bill cut the tariff on grain step by step until February 1849, leaving only a small duty after that. In 1846 Parliament passed it, but most of Peel's own party voted against him, and the party split.

Britain kept going. By 1860 it had ended its tariffs on imported factory goods, keeping a few taxes, such as those on tea, sugar and wine, mainly to raise money. Britain had become a free trade country.

How we know: "partly"

The College Board says Western European countries adopted free trade "partly in response" to Smith's ideas. The history shows why "partly" is the right word: the League's campaign was paid for by manufacturers who wanted cheap food and open markets, and the historian Charles Read notes that the Irish famine "contributed to Peel's decision." Ideas, interests and a crisis worked together.

Others follow, for a while

In 1860 Britain and France signed the Cobden-Chevalier Treaty, cutting tariffs on each other's goods. In the 1860s a chain of similar treaties lowered tariffs across much of Western Europe.

Then some countries turned back. From 1879 Germany put tariffs back on imported grain and iron, to protect its farmers and its factories after cheap grain from America drove prices down. The United States raised its tariffs on factory goods in 1861, just before the Civil War began, raised them again during the war, and kept them high to the end of the century. Britain stayed with free trade.

New Tools for Business

1720 to the 1880s  ·  Limited liability, stock markets, banks

Industry needed more money than a family had. Limited liability, stock markets and banks let many people invest in one business, with less to lose.

The old way

Companies that sold shares were not new. The Dutch East India Company (VOC) sold shares in 1602 (Topic 4.5), and people bought and sold them in Amsterdam. But from 1720 to 1825, forming such a company in Britain usually took special permission from the government: a royal charter or an act of Parliament. Most businesses belonged to one family or a few partners, as Richard Arkwright's mill at Cromford did (Topic 5.3). If a partnership failed, each partner could lose his house and savings to pay its debts.

Limited liability

A railway or a steamship line cost more than any few partners could pay. A law of 1855 let companies formed by ordinary investors limit what their owners could lose to the money they had put in, not everything they owned, and a law of 1856 let almost any group of seven or more people in Britain register such a company. This is limited liability. France passed similar laws in 1863 and 1867. With less to lose, many more people with modest savings could invest.

Stock markets and banks

A stock market, or stock exchange, is where people buy and sell shares. An investor who needed money back could sell shares to someone else, without waiting for the company to close. London had had a stock exchange since the 1700s. In a railway boom in the mid-1840s, crowds of British investors rushed to buy railway shares, and many lost their savings when prices fell. An American print from 1886, "The World's Exchanges," shows the exchanges and trading halls of eight cities, from New York and Chicago to London, Paris, Berlin, Hamburg, Frankfurt and Brussels.

Banks did the rest. They lent money to companies, changed one country's money into another's, and paid bills between ports thousands of miles apart.

Businesses Across Borders

1860s to 1929  ·  HSBC, Lever, Unilever

As trade and production stretched around the world, some businesses grew to work in many countries at once, relying on banking and finance to do it.

A bank for trade with China

The bank known today as HSBC opened in Hong Kong in March 1865, as the Hongkong and Shanghai Banking Company; in 1866 it became the Hongkong and Shanghai Banking Corporation. It was started by Thomas Sutherland, a Scot who worked there for a shipping company, with a committee of merchant firms, to give local businesses better banking for their trade. It opened in Shanghai a month later and in London that July, to change money for clients in China and India. By 1900 it worked in 16 countries and territories.

One of the firms on its first committee, Dent and Company, had grown rich trading opium into China, a trade Britain had forced China to accept by war. How that happened is Unit 6.

Soap and margarine

In England, William Lever began selling Sunlight Soap in 1884, made with oils from tropical plants such as coconut and palm kernels. By 1886 he had registered the Sunlight name in the United States, and from 1888 he built a factory town for his workers, Port Sunlight. In 1890 the business became a limited company, Lever Brothers, Limited, and from 1894 it sold shares to the public. In the Netherlands, the Jurgens and Van den Bergh families of Oss turned butter trading into margarine factories and sold across the North Sea to England.

How we know: the dates of Unilever

The College Board names Unilever, "based in England and the Netherlands and operating in British West Africa and the Belgian Congo." That company did not exist until 1929, when Lever Brothers joined the Dutch margarine makers, who had merged in 1927. Lever's company began plantations in the Belgian Congo in 1911, and how it treated the Congolese who worked them is part of Unit 6. So Unilever is where these nineteenth-century businesses ended up, not a company of the years this unit covers.

Cheaper Goods, for Some

1780s to 1900  ·  Consumer goods and living standards

Better factory methods made goods more available, more affordable and more varied, and living standards rose, much faster after about 1860, for some people and not for others.

More, cheaper, more kinds

Around 1750, cotton thread was still spun by hand, one thread at a time. Once machines spun it (Topic 5.3), its price fell fast: by 1815 even the coarsest cotton yarn cost about a third of its early 1780s price, even after taking rising prices into account. Factories kept improving how they made things. The economic historian Knick Harley found that the price of cotton cloth "fell impressively" while machines spun the yarn, and "continued to fall thereafter as technology improved in weaving and finishing." Soap that grocers had once cut from long bars came in wrapped bars with a brand name, like Sunlight, advertised to every household. Ships and railways (Topic 5.5) brought fruit, tea and wheat from far away to city markets.

Who gained

A family's standard of living is how well it lives: what it can afford to eat, wear and buy. In Britain, one careful estimate finds that working families' standard of living rose by less than 15 percent from the 1780s to the 1850s; most historians agree it grew much faster from about 1860 to 1900.

How we know: measuring "for some"

The economic historian Charles Feinstein estimated that the average working-class family's standard of living rose "by less than 15 percent between the 1780s and 1850s." Some economic historians had found faster gains before 1850, and Feinstein wrote to answer them. Most historians agree that real wages, what pay could actually buy, grew much faster from about 1860. But other measures, such as children's height and how many babies died, look less hopeful up to 1900. Many city workers stayed poor (Topic 5.9), and many of the people who grew the cotton, palm oil and tea did not share the gains (Unit 6).

Not everyone gained from this new economy, and not everyone accepted it. Workers organized, reformers pushed for new laws, and some thinkers imagined a different system. That is Topic 5.8.

One City, Two Descriptions

Industrial capitalism made some people rich and left many working long hours for low pay in crowded towns. Two descriptions of Manchester show both, and show how a source's maker shapes what it says.

Engels in Manchester

Friedrich Engels was a young German whose father was a partner in a cotton business with a mill near Manchester. He was sent to England to learn the business. In 1845 he published a book, in German, for readers in Germany: The Condition of the Working Class in England in 1844. It describes the streets where Manchester's mill workers lived.

A directory was a book listing a town's businesses and their addresses. Workers Organize

1799 to 1906  ·  Britain, Germany, the United States

Workers organized for better conditions, shorter hours, higher wages and a voice in government. In Britain, unions went from a crime to a legal standing; in Britain and Germany, workers built political parties.

From a crime to a right

A single worker who asked for more pay could be fired. Workers together could strike, stopping work until the employer gave way. That is what a labor union is for. In Britain, the Combination Acts of 1799 and 1800 made it illegal for workers to combine to demand higher wages or shorter hours. Parliament repealed them in 1824; a law of 1825 then limited what unions could lawfully do.

In 1834 six farm laborers from Tolpuddle, in Dorset, were sentenced to seven years' transportation to Australia for swearing a secret oath when they formed a union. A mass protest followed, with a petition of about 800,000 names, and in 1836 they were pardoned.

Skilled workers built lasting unions for their trades. In June 1868 unions met in Manchester as the first Trades Union Congress. The Trade Union Act of 1871 said that a union's purposes were not unlawful just because they were "in restraint of trade," which gave unions legal standing. After years of campaigning, a law of 1847 limited women and young people in textile mills to ten hours of work a day from 1848.

Common mistake to avoid

"After 1824, unions could do as they liked." Not quite. The 1824 repeal ended the ban and forming a union was no longer a crime, but the 1825 law limited what unions could do, and the Tolpuddle laborers were convicted in 1834 under a law against secret oaths. A law of 1871 gave unions legal standing, but a law passed the same day made picketing a crime until 1875. A change can come in steps.

The Charter

Workers also wanted a voice in making the laws. In 1838 the Chartists published a People's Charter with six points: a vote for every man over 21, a secret ballot, no property requirement for members of Parliament, pay for members, districts of equal size, and a new Parliament elected every year. Over 1.2 million people signed their first petition in 1839, and the Chartists claimed over three million for the second in 1842. Parliament rejected both, and a third in 1848.

The Chartists lost every petition, but five of their six points later became law, one only roughly: the property requirement went in 1858, the vote reached many working men in 1867 and 1884 and every man over 21 in 1918, the secret ballot came in 1872, districts were redrawn closer to equal size in 1885, and members were paid from 1911. A new Parliament every year never came.

Parties in different countries

In Germany, Ferdinand Lassalle founded a workers' party in 1863, and August Bebel and Wilhelm Liebknecht founded another in 1869. They united in 1875. Chancellor Otto von Bismarck banned socialist meetings and newspapers in 1878; in February 1890, while it was still banned, the party won more votes than any other, about 20 percent; the law lapsed that September, and in October the party renamed itself the Social Democratic Party.

In Britain, unions and socialists founded the Labour Representation Committee in February 1900 to elect working men to Parliament; in 1906 it won 29 seats and took the name Labour Party. In the United States, the American Federation of Labor, founded in December 1886 with Samuel Gompers as president, joined unions of skilled workers who wanted higher wages, shorter hours and better conditions.

Imagining a Different Society

1800 to 1891  ·  Owen, Marx and Engels

Discontent with who held power encouraged new ideas. Socialists wanted the means of making wealth owned in common; Marx predicted that workers would overthrow the owners and build a classless society he called communism.

Owen and socialism

Robert Owen took over the management of the New Lanark cotton mills in Scotland in 1800. He shortened working hours and opened a school for the workers' children in 1816. In 1825 he bought land at New Harmony, Indiana, for a community where people would share their work and its rewards; it broke up within two years.

In the late 1820s, Owen's followers began to call their ideas socialism: the idea that land, factories and the other things a society uses to make wealth should be owned and run in common, for everyone, and not by private owners for their own profit.

The Manifesto

In 1847 a small group of radicals in London, the Communist League, asked Karl Marx and Friedrich Engels to set out its ideas. Their Manifesto of the Communist Party was printed in German in London in February 1848. The English translation of 1888, which Engels checked, begins its first section: "The history of all hitherto existing society is the history of class struggles."

Industry, Marx argued, was splitting society "into two great hostile camps": the bourgeoisie, who owned the factories, and the proletarians, wage workers who owned nothing but their ability to work. The workers would one day overthrow the owners, end private ownership of the means of production ("the theory of the Communists may be summed up in the single sentence: Abolition of private property"), and build a society without classes: communism. The Manifesto ends: "The proletarians have nothing to lose but their chains. They have a world to win. Working Men of All Countries, Unite!" (The German original reads "Proletarians of all countries, unite!"; "Workers of the world, unite!" is a later, popular English version.)

How we know: socialism and communism

In his preface of 1888, Engels explained why the book was not called a socialist manifesto: in 1847, he wrote, "socialism was a middle-class movement, communism a working-class movement." Socialism was the wider family of ideas; communism, as Marx and Engels used the word, was a working-class movement for revolution and its end point. Marx lived in London from 1849 until his death in 1883, and published the first volume of Capital in 1867.

What the ideas did by 1900

No revolution like the one Marx predicted won lasting power in an industrial country before 1900. The Paris Commune of 1871, which Marx praised as a workers' government, was crushed within about ten weeks. His ideas spread through parties instead. In 1891 Germany's Social Democrats adopted a program, drafted mainly by Karl Kautsky, based on Marx's ideas, while they kept winning seats in elections. In July 1889 socialist parties from many countries met in Paris and called for workers everywhere to march on May 1, 1890, for an eight-hour working day.

Common mistake to avoid

"Marx's revolution came in the 1800s." No communist party ruled a country until the twentieth century (Unit 7).

Reformers and Governments

1833 to 1889  ·  Britain and Germany

Governments, organizations and individuals pushed political, social, educational and urban reforms. Some reforms answered workers; Bismarck's were also a weapon against their party.

Four kinds of reform

A reform changes a law or a system to fix a problem without overthrowing it. The College Board names four kinds, and Britain had all of them.

Political. After the Reform League, a workers' organization, held huge meetings in London's Hyde Park in 1866 and 1867, the Reform Act of 1867 added about 938,000 voters, many of them working men in towns, and the act of 1884 brought in many voters in the countryside. No woman could vote in a British national election until 1918.

Social. The Factory Act of 1833 banned children under nine from most textile mills (silk mills were left out), limited children of nine to thirteen to nine hours a day, and appointed four inspectors. The Mines Act of 1842, brought in by Lord Ashley, a member of Parliament, after a government inquiry into children's work in mines, stopped all women and girls, and boys under ten, from working inside mines.

Educational. The Education Act of 1870, written by William Forster, a government minister, after a campaign by the National Education League, set up elected school boards to provide elementary schools where church schools fell short, and let them require attendance; a law of 1880 made every local authority require it.

Urban. In the hot summer of 1858 the River Thames, which carried London's sewage, smelled so foul it was called the "Great Stink." London's engineer Joseph Bazalgette then built 82 miles of large brick sewers, fed by about 1,100 miles of street sewers, to carry the waste away; the work was completed in 1875. The crisis in the cities is Topic 5.9's story.

A government reforms to beat its critics

Germany's government reformed for another reason. Bismarck banned the socialists in 1878. Then a message from the emperor in 1881 promised to cure social ills "not only through the repression of Social Democratic excesses" but also by "the consistent and positive promotion of workers' welfare." The Reichstag passed health insurance in 1883, accident insurance in 1884, and insurance for old age and disability in 1889.

How we know: Bismarck's motive

Bismarck said so himself. In a speech to the Reichstag in 1884 he argued that the socialists' leaders needed "dissatisfied classes": "dissatisfied workers are just what they need." Insurance was meant, in part, to make workers less dissatisfied. Reform could answer workers and also be a weapon against their party.

The Old Empires Modernize, and Meet Resistance

1789 to 1898  ·  Istanbul and Beijing

For the Ottoman Empire and Qing China, the pressure came from stronger outside powers, more and more of them industrial. Both governments tried to modernize their armies and economies, and both met resistance from inside.

The Ottoman Empire

After a lost war with Russia (1787 to 1792), Sultan Selim III built a new infantry in the 1790s, the "New Order," with European-style uniforms, imported guns and French instructors. The Janissaries, the old army corps, saw it as a threat, and important conservative religious scholars sided with them. A revolt overthrew Selim in 1807. In June 1826 Sultan Mahmud II destroyed the Janissaries and built a new army along European lines.

On 3 November 1839, at Gülhane in Istanbul, the government announced the Tanzimat ("reorganization"). Its decree promised "perfect security for life, honor, and property," "a regular system of assessing taxes" and a regular system of military service, and it said that "the Muslim and non-Muslim subjects of our lofty Sultanate shall, without exception, enjoy our imperial concessions." Mahmud II's government had opened a state fez factory in Istanbul in the 1830s; around 1850 it was fitted with steam engines and machines from Britain and Belgium. The state added an ironworks in 1843 and telegraph lines from 1855.

A decree of 1856 promised equal treatment to Christians and Jews. Many Muslims, who had long held a higher place than Christians and Jews, resented losing it; some Christian church leaders were unhappy too. In December 1876 the empire got a constitution and a parliament. In February 1878 Sultan Abdulhamid II suspended the constitution and dissolved the parliament.

Qing China

Britain defeated China in the Opium War of 1839 to 1842, and Britain and France defeated it again from 1856 to 1860 (Unit 6), while the Taiping Rebellion tore through the country until 1864. In 1861 Prince Gong set up an office for foreign affairs, and officials such as Zeng Guofan, Li Hongzhang and Zuo Zongtang began what was called Self-Strengthening: learning Western ways of making weapons and ships while keeping China's own system of rule. They built the Jiangnan Arsenal in Shanghai (1865), a navy yard at Fuzhou (1866) and a steamship company (1872).

In 1862 the court opened the Tongwen Guan in Beijing to train translators. In early 1867 Prince Gong asked the court to add astronomy and mathematics, taught partly by Western teachers, and to invite young degree-holders from the civil service examinations to study there. "Western learning is something urgently in need of study," his memorial said. Woren, a senior scholar and the emperor's tutor, objected: "establishing a country rests on valuing propriety and righteousness, not on schemes and stratagems." The court backed the prince, and the plan went ahead, but of about 98 who applied, only about 30 were admitted, and few were the well-educated young officials the plan had hoped for.

In 1894 and 1895 Japan defeated China's new navy. In June 1898 the young Guangxu Emperor declared that reform was national policy and issued decree after decree. In September the Empress Dowager Cixi and officials who opposed the reforms seized power, confined the emperor, and executed six reformers.

Common mistake to avoid

"Cixi spent the navy's money, so China lost in 1895." It is a popular story. The historian Benjamin Elman finds that Cixi's spending on the Summer Palace "did not use up the imperial treasury or leave nothing for the Chinese navy," though tight funding did limit the fleet; the deeper problems were a disorganized empire and poorly trained crews. And the slogan "Chinese learning as the essence, Western learning for practical use" is often pinned on the 1860s. The idea goes back that far, but the slogan in this form appeared in the 1890s and was made famous in 1898.

New Classes, Old Families

1750 to 1900  ·  Britain

Industry made a middle class that rose by money and education and a working class that lived on wages. But the landed families who had been on top in 1750 were still near the top in 1900.

The old ladder

Every society has a social hierarchy, a ladder of rank. In 1750 most people in Britain lived in the countryside. At the top were landowning families, the aristocracy (lords with titles) and the gentry (landowners without them), whose wealth and rank came from land they inherited. Below them were farmers, craftsmen and laborers. Most families worked together, on the farm or at home, where women spun thread and children helped.

Two classes grow

Industry made two groups bigger and more important than ever. The middle class included factory owners, merchants and bankers, and the people who ran things for them, from engineers, doctors and lawyers to managers and clerks. Their place came from money they made and the education they paid for, not from land or titles. In 1832 the Reform Act gave the vote, in the towns, to men who occupied property worth ten pounds a year: in effect, most middle-class men. It excluded women by its own wording.

The industrial working class was made up of people who owned no land and no tools, and lived on the wages they earned in mills, mines, docks and workshops, at the hours the employer set. Their unions and parties are Topic 5.8's story.

Who stayed on top

The landed families did not disappear. They kept their land, their titles and much of their power; historians of the landed class describe it as still dominant in wealth and politics until the 1880s. From 1895 to 1902 Britain's prime minister was Lord Salisbury, a marquess and a great landowner. Some rich middle-class families bought country estates, sent their sons to the same schools as the gentry, and married into landed families, though historians debate how many (Lawrence and Jeanne Stone found few newcomers entering the landed elite). Those who did climbed the old ladder rather than breaking it.

Common mistake to avoid

"Industry created social classes." There was a ladder of rank long before the factories, and its top rungs held. What industry did was add new rungs and change how people climbed: by money and education as well as by birth. The opposite mistake is "nothing changed": the middle class won the vote in 1832, and the class that lived on wages grew fast.

Work and Home, Split by Class

1830s to 1900  ·  Britain, the United States, Japan

Women and children had always worked. Industry moved work out of the home and split women's lives by class: working-class women and children earned wages because their families needed them, and middle-class women, whose families did not, were increasingly kept to the home.

Working-class families

In working-class families one wage was rarely enough to pay for rent and food, so women, and often children, earned too. A survey of factories in 1833 found that women and girls were a majority of the workers in Britain's cotton, flax and silk mills, though not in its wool mills. Many more women worked in domestic service, as maids and cooks in other people's houses: the census of 1851 suggests about four in ten women with a recorded job were servants, the largest single job for women. Others sewed or made goods at home, paid by the piece.

Women were usually paid less than men. Some historians find that much of the gap came from the jobs women were given, which paid less; others put more weight on custom and unequal pay for the same work, and the debate goes on. The pattern spread with industry: in Japan in 1900, women made up about 62 percent of the workers in private factories, most of them young women in textile mills.

The middle-class home

In middle-class families the husband's earnings were enough, so the wife was increasingly expected to stay out of paid work and to run the home and raise the children. Writers called the home a woman's proper "sphere"; historians call the idea separate spheres. In 1861 Isabella Beeton's best-selling Book of Household Management, in its first chapter, "The Mistress," begins: "As with the commander of an army, or the leader of any enterprise, so is it with the mistress of a house. Her spirit will be seen through the whole establishment; and just in proportion as she performs her duties intelligently and thoroughly, so will her domestics follow in her path."

Running such a house was real work, and the "domestics" who followed the mistress were servants, most of them working-class women. One class's home ran on the other class's wages.

How we know: an ideal, not a photograph

Advice books like Beeton's, and pictures like Currier & Ives's The Four Seasons of Life: Middle Age (about 1868), tell us what middle-class families were told a home should be. They do not prove every family lived that way. The historian Amanda Vickery has warned that many women's lives never fit the "separate spheres" picture, which is why the College Board says middle-class women were "increasingly" limited to the home: an ideal that grew over the century.

What kept going for women

In both classes, women were paid less and had fewer rights than men. In England and Wales, a married woman's property passed under her husband's control; the Married Women's Property Act of 1870 let her keep the money she earned, and the Act of 1882 let women married from 1883 hold all their property as their own. No woman could vote for Parliament.

Common mistake to avoid

"In the 1800s women stopped working." Working-class women and children earned wages, and middle-class women ran households, often with servants. The opposite mistake is "all women worked in factories": domestic service, not the factory, was the largest single job for working women (about four in ten), and many worked at home. Name the class before you describe a woman's life.

Cities That Could Not Keep Up

1773 to 1851  ·  Manchester, England and Wales

Industrial towns grew faster than anyone could build homes, sewers and water pipes for the people who came, and that speed brought the six challenges the College Board names.

How fast

Urbanization is the growth of cities. Manchester, the great cotton town, had about 23,000 people in a count made in 1773, about 70,000 in the township at the first national census in 1801, and more than 300,000 in the borough by 1851. (The numbers depend on where the town's edge is drawn: the borough was larger than the old township.) In 1851 the census found, for the first time, more than half the people of England and Wales living in towns, though only just.

Six challenges

Pollution: coal smoke hung over the towns, and waste ran into the rivers. Friedrich Engels described Manchester's river Irk in 1845 as "coal-black, foul-smelling ... full of debris and refuse." Poverty: many wages barely paid the rent. Housing shortages: houses were split into rooms, and whole families lived in one room or a damp cellar. Insufficient infrastructure: the new streets had no sewers, no clean water and no rubbish collection. Public health crises: section 04. Increased crime: below.

How we know: counting crime

The number of people tried for serious crimes in England and Wales rose to peaks in 1842 and 1848, years of hard times, and then fell for the rest of the century (Gatrell and Hadden). But historians warn that these numbers count prosecutions, not crimes: as towns set up police forces, more crimes were caught and charged. So say "more people were charged with crimes," not "crime exploded."

Strongest tool, with its mechanism

Best evidence of exclusion or limit
"At times," says the College Board: not every town, not every year. The reforms that answered these problems are Topic 5.8's.
Comparison hook

Urbanization
The growth of cities, as people move from the countryside to towns.
Infrastructure

04

What It Did to Life

1831 to 1854  ·  Manchester and London

Crowded towns with dirty water made disease spread, and in the fastest-growing cities people's lives were shorter: measured in health, the standard of living there was far below the nation's.

Cholera

Cholera, a disease of the gut that spreads through water fouled with human waste, reached Britain in 1831. In 1848 and 1849 about 53,000 cholera deaths were registered in England and Wales. These were public health crises: disasters for a whole population, not only for the sick.

In 1854 the London physician John Snow mapped the cholera deaths around Broad Street in Soho and traced them to one public water pump. His map, printed in a report of 1855, marks each death with a small black bar; the bars stack up around the pump. A cartoon in Punch two years earlier, "A Court for King Cholera" (1852), had blamed crowding and filth. Snow showed the route was the water.

How long people lived

A family's standard of living is how well it lives, including its health and how long its members live. In the 1840s a baby born in Manchester could expect to live only about 25 to 28 years, depending on how the historians count, against about 40 to 42 for England and Wales as a whole.

Common mistake to avoid

"Workers in industrial cities died at 25." Life expectancy at birth is an average, and it is pulled down hard by the many babies and young children who died. A Manchester worker who survived childhood could expect decades more. The gap between the city and the nation is the evidence: the city was far deadlier. The opposite mistake is "cities were as healthy as the countryside."

Not Only Britain: New York, and 1900

1850 to 1900  ·  New York, Britain

The College Board ties these challenges to "global capitalism." Wherever industry and trade drew people in faster than homes were built, the same story played out, and by 1900 cities were only beginning to catch up.

New York, 1890

Manhattan, the heart of New York City, grew from about 515,000 people in 1850 to about 1.4 million in 1890, many of them immigrants who came for work. In 1890 the reporter Jacob Riis published How the Other Half Lives. "To-day three-fourths of its people live in the tenements," he wrote, "and the nineteenth century drift of the population to the cities is sending ever-increasing multitudes to crowd them." A tenement was a crowded apartment building for poor renters.

Riis went with the police on a visit after midnight and photographed one room on Bayard Street by flash: "In a room not thirteen feet either way slept twelve men and women, two or three in bunks set in a sort of alcove, the rest on the floor ... Most of the men were lodgers, who slept there for five cents a spot."

How we know: a reformer's camera

Riis wanted to shock comfortable readers into supporting reform, and he chose and set up his scenes for that purpose. His book also carries prejudiced lines about immigrant groups. His photographs and counts are strong evidence of crowding; his judgments of the people in them are evidence of his own point of view.

Contextualize it

To contextualize a source, place it inside the bigger story that explains it. John Snow's 1854 map of cholera deaths around one London water pump happened inside the rapid growth of industrial cities: crowded streets still drew their water from shared pumps and wells that waste could reach, so one fouled pump could kill hundreds of neighbors. Try the same move on Riis's Bayard Street room in the Skill Builder.

By 1900

Later in the century, cities began to catch up, building sewers and clean water supplies and setting up police forces (Topic 5.8 has London's new sewers after 1858). Life expectancy in England and Wales began a lasting rise from the 1870s, and wages rose faster after about 1860 (Topic 5.7). But in 1900 many city workers were still poor, and a ladder of rank, rebuilt, still stood.

Between 1750 and 1900 industry changed how the world made and moved things. But a change has a size, a reach and a limit. This chapter measures all three, sorts out the other great change of the age, the revolution in ideas and governments, and shows how to connect the evidence into an argument.

01

Making: Factories and Coal

1750 to 1900  ·  Britain, Europe, the United States

Industry changed how goods were made and what they cost, and moved the making of the world's goods from Asia to Europe and the United States. The gains in living standards were real, slow at first, and unequal.

The starting line

To measure a change you need a starting line. In 1750 almost everything people bought was made by hand, at home or in small workshops, and most of the world's manufactured goods were made in Asia. The economic historian Paul Bairoch estimated that China made about a third of them and India about a quarter, while all of Europe together made about a quarter and Britain under 2 percent. Almost all the energy people used came from things that grow: food for the muscles of people and animals, wood for fire, and wind and water for ships and mills.

How we know: estimates, not counts

Nobody in 1750 counted every piece of cloth in the world. Bairoch's shares are a historian's estimates, built from what records survive, and historians who use them say so. What they show well is the size and direction of the change, not exact amounts. Say "estimated" when you use them.

A new source of energy

The change began with energy. Steam engines turned the heat of burning coal into motion, and from the 1780s James Watt and Matthew Boulton built engines that turned a wheel, so an engine could drive machines. A mill no longer had to sit beside a fast river; it could run wherever coal could be brought. Coal mining and steam engines grew together, and by about 1900 coal supplied about half of all the energy used in the world. That is the size of the change: not just new machines, but a new kind of energy, stored underground for millions of years.

Cheaper goods, and whose

Industrial capitalism, factories and businesses run by owners and investors for profit, kept improving how things were made, so consumer goods became more available, more affordable and more varied, the three words the College Board uses. By 1815 even the coarsest cotton yarn cost about a third of what it had in the early 1780s, after rising prices are taken into account. Soap came in wrapped bars with a brand name, like Sunlight, sold from 1884. And the making moved: Britain's share of the world's manufacturing rose from under 2 percent in 1750 to about a fifth by 1860, and by 1900 Europe and the United States together made more than four-fifths of the world's manufactured goods.

Did people live better? The College Board's answer is careful: standards of living rose "for some." One careful estimate finds British working families' standard of living rose by less than 15 percent from the 1780s to the 1850s, and most historians agree it grew much faster from about 1860 to 1900. In the fast-growing towns, many people's health was far worse than the nation's (Topic 5.9).

Common mistake to avoid

"Industry made everyone richer." The College Board's own words are "for some." Owners and the middle class gained first; working families gained slowly, then faster after about 1860; and many city workers were still poor in 1900. The opposite mistake, "workers gained nothing," goes too far the other way.

Moving: Rails, Steam, and Wire

1830 to 1913  ·  the Americas, Russia, India, the oceans

Railroads, steamships and the telegraph reached the interiors of continents around the world, so trade and migration grew. The change in moving reached further than the change in making.

Into the interior

Before the railroad, heavy goods moved cheaply only by water, and the interior of a continent, far from the sea or a big river, was hard to reach. Railways changed that, and not only in Britain. Russia had 27 kilometers of railway in 1840 and more than 53,000 in 1900. India's first passenger train ran in 1853. The first railroad across the United States was finished in 1869.

Ships and news changed too. On the main emigrant route, from Liverpool to New York, a voyage took more than 40 days on average in 1853, when emigrant ships still went under sail; by 1869, as steamships took over, it took about 16. By the 1840s a telegraph message could travel along a wire in minutes; Samuel Morse's line from Washington to Baltimore opened in 1844. From 1866 cables under the Atlantic carried messages without a lasting break, and in 1870 a cable reached Bombay, in India.

More trade, more migration

When moving goods got cheaper and faster, markets far apart began to behave like one market. In 1870 wheat cost 57.6 percent more in Liverpool, England, than in Chicago; by 1913, just after this unit's period, it cost only 15.6 percent more. Wheat grown in the middle of North America, carried by train to the coast and by steamship across the ocean, fed workers in Britain.

People moved too. Migration means moving to live in a new place. About 55 million Europeans moved to the Americas and Australia between 1850 and 1914, and between 1846 and 1940 more than 29 million Indians and more than 19 million Chinese moved to Southeast Asia and the lands around the Indian Ocean. Why they moved, and what they found, is Unit 6.

How we know: a price gap

Historians cannot measure "connection" directly, but they can measure prices. When the same grain costs nearly the same in two cities an ocean apart, it means moving it has become cheap and fast. A gap that shrinks from 57.6 percent to 15.6 percent is a number that shows a change over time.

03

How Far It Reached

1840s to 1908  ·  India, China, Egypt, Japan, Britain

The change was deep where it reached, but it did not reach everyone. The old makers kept making, most people still farmed, and the gains went to some more than others.

A witness weighs his century

In 1898 Alfred Russel Wallace, a British naturalist who had worked out the theory of evolution by natural selection on his own, separately from Charles Darwin (their papers were read together in 1858), wrote a book weighing his century, The Wonderful Century. His preface claims that "not only is our century superior to any that have gone before it, but that it may be best compared with the whole preceding historical period." Then he turns: "But this is only one side of the shield." More than half his book is about the century's "Failures."

How we know: reading a witness

Wallace lived through most of the century, which makes him a valuable witness, but a witness has a point of view. He wanted land taken out of private hands, and his "Failures" also include attacks on vaccination and a defense of phrenology, ideas scientists rejected. Use him as a claim to test against other evidence, not as the answer.

The old makers, and the farmers

India, China and the Middle East did not stop working. Their share of the world's goods shrank, and China's fell from about a third in 1750 to about 6 percent in 1900, partly because the rest of the world's production grew much faster. But in 1908, in just one province of northern India, about a million people still lived from weaving cloth on handlooms.

And most people still farmed. In India, the census of 1901 found about two-thirds of the people living by farming. Even in the industrial United States, about two in five workers still farmed in 1900. Until about 1900, wood and other things that grow were still the world's largest source of energy.

Gains for some

Only a few states built industry, and not all of it lasted: Muhammad Ali's Egypt built factories for its army, and most had closed by the mid-1840s, while Meiji Japan built railways and mills that lasted (Topic 5.6). Inside industrial countries, the ladder of rank was rebuilt, not ended: landed families kept much of their power, and in 1900 Britain's prime minister, Lord Salisbury, was a marquess. In the 1840s a baby born in Manchester could expect to live only about 25 to 28 years, against about 40 to 42 for England and Wales as a whole, averages pulled down by the many babies who died (Topic 5.9).

Wallace went further. "One of the most prominent features of our century," he wrote, "has been the enormous and continuous growth of wealth, without any corresponding increase in the well-being of the whole people." Test it. The gains were unequal, as he says. But historians find that working families' living standards did rise, slowly to the 1850s and faster after about 1860. Wallace is partly right and partly too far.

Common mistake to avoid

"A shrinking share means the old makers stopped." A share is a part of a whole. China's and India's makers kept working; the whole grew much faster around them. The opposite mistake, "nothing changed for Asia's makers," forgets that India came to buy British cloth and sell raw cotton (Topic 5.4).

The Other Revolution: Ideas and Nations

1687 to 1900  ·  Europe and the Americas

The revolution in ideas and governments was the age's other great change. It began before the factories spread, from ideas and grievances, and it met industry later.

By what right?

The Enlightenment was a movement of thinkers who used reason and evidence, first to explain nature and then to question old authorities. They reexamined the role of religion in public life: Voltaire's Treatise on Tolerance (1763) argued that no government should force a faith on anyone. And they wrote new political ideas. In 1689 John Locke argued that people are born with natural rights to "lives, liberties and estates," and that government is a social contract that rests on the people's consent (Topic 5.1).

The ideas came first

The College Board says Enlightenment thought "often preceded" revolutions. The dates show it. The American Revolution began in 1775, the French in 1789, the Haitian in 1791, and the wars for independence in Spanish America in 1810. Factory methods, meanwhile, were only beginning to leave Britain: a copy of Arkwright's mill opened at Ratingen, in Germany, in 1784, Samuel Slater's spinning mill in Rhode Island was begun in 1793, John Cockerill's works in Belgium in 1817, and the big growth in Russia and Japan came mostly after 1870 (Topic 5.4). So the Atlantic revolutions were not industry's. Its causes, Topic 5.2 showed, were a grievance, the words to name it, and a chance to act.

Common mistake to avoid

"Industry caused the revolutions." Check the dates: France's revolution began in 1789, while factories were still almost all in Britain. The opposite mistake, "the two revolutions had nothing to do with each other," forgets where they met: Prussia's railways and iron, Meiji Japan and the Chartists, below.

New nations, and where the two revolutions met

The revolutions built new countries, the United States, Haiti and the new republics of Spanish America. Each was a nation-state, a state that claims to belong to one people, its nation. They also left a new loyalty, nationalism, which joined most of Italy into one kingdom in 1861 and the German states into an empire in 1871, and pulled Greeks and other Balkan peoples away from the Ottoman Empire (Topic 5.2).

Later, the two revolutions met. Railways and iron helped Prussia win the wars that united Germany. Nationalism and fear of stronger powers pushed Meiji Japan to build railways and mills to make the nation strong (Topic 5.6). In industrial Britain, the Chartists used the language of rights to demand the vote for working men (Topic 5.8). And the old order held on in politics too: in 1900 kings and emperors still ruled most of Europe, and women could vote in national elections almost nowhere; New Zealand, in 1893, was the first self-governing country where all adult women could (Topic 5.1).

Make the Argument

The whole unit

A list of evidence is not an argument. An argument explains how the pieces relate, and an argument about extent takes a side, names a change and a continuity, and says for whom and why.

Three ways evidence connects

Led to. Coal and steam engines led to railroads and steamships, which led to cheap North American wheat feeding workers in Britain. Each piece is a cause of the next.

The same pattern. Railways reached the interior of Russia and the interior of India: the same change, in two different empires.

A contrast. Lancashire's power looms made cloth more cheaply than India's hand weavers could, yet a million people in one Indian province still lived from the handloom in 1908. Set a change beside a continuity, and you can say how much changed.

What a thesis about extent needs

Your thesis is the claim that answers the question, and you must be able to defend it with evidence. A thesis about extent takes a side: to a great extent, to a limited extent, or great in some ways and limited in others. It names a change and a continuity. And it says for whom or where the change was greatest, and why. "Industrialization changed the world a lot" does none of this: it fits any century in history.

Common mistake to avoid

"Everything changed" and "nothing really changed" are both easy to write and both wrong. The first forgets the farmers, the handloom weavers and the kings; the second forgets the railroads, the telegraph and the price of cloth. A good answer is a measurement in between, with evidence.
