Topic 5.1

By What Right?

In 1750 most rulers said their power came from God. A group of writers began asking every old authority the same question, by what right?, and answered it with reason, natural rights and the consent of the people. This chapter follows that question from the new science of the late 1600s to the reformers of the 1800s, and asks how much it had changed by 1900.

01

A New Way of Knowing

1680s to 1780s  ·  Reason and evidence

The Enlightenment's political ideas began with a method. Thinkers learned to test claims about nature with reason and evidence, and then asked whether the same method could test the claims that kings, churches and nobles made about their own power.

The old answer

In 1750 most of Europe's rulers gave the same answer to the question "who does a state belong to?" It belonged to the king, because God had put him there. This is divine right, the claim Louis XIV made at Versailles (Topic 3.2). In most countries one church was the law: Catholic in France and Spain, Anglican in England. Nobles were born into privileges, such as freedom from some taxes, and across much of central and eastern Europe peasants were serfs, bound to work their lord's land. Authority came from God, from tradition and from birth.

Reason and evidence, first for nature

In 1687 the English scientist Isaac Newton published a book showing that a few laws of motion and gravity, worked out with mathematics and tested against what astronomers observed, explained both the paths of the planets and the fall of an apple. The lesson many readers took from it was that the world runs by laws a human mind can discover, without being told by an old book or a priest.

In 1689 the English philosopher John Locke argued that every idea in our minds comes from experience. At birth, he wrote, the mind is like "white paper." Learning from evidence and experience instead of accepting what authorities say is called empiricism. Scientific lectures and experiments became popular entertainment for people who could pay, like the family in Joseph Wright's painting of an air-pump experiment, made famous by a print of 1769.

Common mistake to avoid

Do not write that the Enlightenment was against religion. Most of its writers believed in God, and some were deists, who believed God made the world and then let it run by natural laws. What they questioned was religion used as a power of government (section 02).

Then for people

The step that made the method political was simple to state. If reason and evidence can explain the planets, can they explain people: how we should live together, what a government is for, and what makes a ruler's power rightful? Enlightenment writers turned the method on government, religion, law and society, and that is where the new political ideas came from.

Best evidence of exclusion or limit

Divine right
The claim that a king's power comes from God, so no one may question or remove him.
Serf
A peasant bound to a lord's land, who owed the lord labor and could not leave freely.
Empiricism

Deism
The belief that God created the world and then let it run by natural laws.
02

The New Answers

1689 to 1763  ·  Locke, Montesquieu, Rousseau, Voltaire

Enlightenment writers started with the individual person, not the king, and came up with new answers to the question of what makes power rightful. The change was not that kings disappeared; it was that a ruler now had to answer the question.

Natural rights and the social contract

In his Second Treatise of Government (1689), Locke argued that people are born free and equal, with natural rights to their "lives, liberties and estates" (an estate is what you own). No government gives these rights, and no government may take them. People form a government to protect them.

That makes government an agreement, a social contract. "No one," Locke wrote, can be "subjected to the political power of another, without his own consent." And if a government tries to destroy the people's rights or reduce them to slavery, it puts itself "into a state of war with the people," who no longer owe it obedience. Most historians think Locke wrote most of the book in the early 1680s, when some members of Parliament were trying to keep the Catholic James from becoming king, and he published it in 1689, just after Parliament had replaced James II. Both times, his argument defended the right to resist a king.

In 1762 Jean-Jacques Rousseau, a writer from Geneva who lived in France, began The Social Contract with a famous line: "Man is born free; and everywhere he is in chains." Rousseau argued that rightful power comes from the "general will" of the people as a whole, a more collective idea than Locke's.

Limits on power

In The Spirit of the Laws (1748), the French writer Montesquieu argued that liberty is safest when the power to make laws, the power to carry them out and the power to judge are held by different people, so that one power can check another. This is the separation of powers.

Religion and the state

In 1762 a court in Toulouse condemned Jean Calas, a Protestant cloth merchant, for killing his son to stop him from becoming a Catholic. His son had most likely taken his own life. Calas was tortured and executed. Voltaire, one of the most famous writers in Europe, took up the case, published a Treatise on Tolerance in 1763, and campaigned until Calas was declared innocent in 1765. The argument was religious toleration: no government should force a faith on anyone or punish people for what they believe.

How we know: read the words

Every quotation in this section is from the writers' own books, which are still in print and free online. When you describe a claim, go back to the words: Locke's "without his own consent" is a claim about where a government's right to rule comes from, and it argues against divine right.

Use this in your answer
Strongest tool, with its mechanism
Say what made a ruler legitimate, before and after. The old answer was God and birth; the new answer was reason and the consent of the people.
Best evidence of exclusion or limit
The writers disagreed. Locke protected individual rights; Rousseau stressed the people as a whole; few of them wanted to abolish kings, and most did not mean their words for everyone (section 04).
Comparison hook
Against the Mandate of Heaven: Chinese rulers could also lose the right to rule by governing badly, but the mandate still came from Heaven. Locke's consent comes from the people.

Terms to use precisely

Natural rights
Rights people are born with, such as life, liberty and property, which no government gives or may take away.
Social contract
The idea that government is an agreement: people accept a government so that it will protect their rights.
Separation of powers
Dividing the powers to make laws, carry them out and judge among different people, so no one holds them all.
Religious toleration
The idea that no government should force a faith on anyone or punish people for their beliefs.
03

The Ideas Travel, Before the Revolutions

1751 to 1810  ·  Print, salons, letters

The ideas spread through print and conversation across Europe and the Atlantic, questioned tradition in every part of life, and came before the revolutions that began in 1775, 1789, 1791 and 1810. They gave revolutionaries their words, and they turned "the people" into the nation.

Print, salons and letters

From 1751 to 1772, Denis Diderot and his partners published the Encyclopédie in Paris: seventeen volumes of articles and eleven of pictures that tried to explain everything by reason, from how to make glass or mount a fan to what a government owes its people. The French government banned it in 1759, and the Pope condemned it, but the last ten volumes of articles came out together in 1765, and printers in other countries copied it: a second edition was printed in Lucca, Italy, from 1758.

The ideas also traveled in newspapers and pamphlets, in London's coffeehouses, in letters between writers across Europe and the Americas, and in the conversation gatherings of Paris called salons, which were often run by women. In 1784 the German philosopher Immanuel Kant summed up the attitude in a motto: "Dare to know," which he explained as having the courage to use your own understanding.

Before the revolutions

The College Board's words are that Enlightenment thought "often preceded" revolutions. The American Revolution began in 1775, the French Revolution in 1789, the Haitian Revolution in 1791, and the wars of independence in Spanish America in 1810. In Bogotá, Antonio Nariño secretly printed a Spanish translation of France's new declaration of rights (in 1793 or 1794) and was arrested for it in 1794; he later became one of the leaders of New Granada's independence.

Common mistake to avoid

Do not write that the Enlightenment caused the revolutions. The ideas came first and gave revolutionaries words to explain what they wanted, but taxes, debt, war, slavery and colonial grievances caused them too. Topic 5.2 is about those causes.

The people become a nation

If a state belongs to its people, a new question follows: which people? More and more, the answer was the nation, a people who saw themselves as one (Topic 5.2 explains what held a nation together). In 1789 the French revolutionaries wrote that "the principle of all sovereignty resides essentially in the nation." Over the next century, nationalism, loyalty to your nation above a king or an empire, became one of the strongest forces shaping states and empires: it helped build new countries and pulled at empires of many peoples. Topic 5.2 tells that story.

Use this in your answer
Strongest tool, with its mechanism
Context, not cause. Enlightenment ideas spread and came before the revolutions, so they are the intellectual and ideological context the revolutions happened in.
Best evidence of exclusion or limit
Most people in 1789 could not read the Encyclopédie. They met the ideas, if at all, through talk, sermons, pamphlets and rumor.
Comparison hook
Against the printing press of the 1400s: the technology was old by 1750. What was new was how much was printed, and the network of readers, salons and letters around it.

Terms to use precisely

Encyclopédie
The French encyclopedia of 1751 to 1772, edited by Diderot, which explained everything by reason and was banned and printed anyway.
Salon
A conversation gathering in a private home, often run by a woman, where writers and readers discussed ideas.
Sovereignty
The highest power in a state.
Nationalism
Loyalty to your nation, a people who see themselves as one, above a king or an empire.
04

Pressing the Words Further

1787 to 1888  ·  Abolition, serfs, voters

The words sounded like everyone, but the men who wrote them often did not mean everyone. Over the next century, reform movements driven by Enlightenment ideas and by religious faith pressed the same words until rights reached the enslaved, the serfs and many more voters, slowly and unevenly.

The words were bigger than their writers

Locke owned shares in the Royal African Company, an English company that traded enslaved Africans, in the 1670s. Rousseau wrote in Emile (1762) that "a woman's education must therefore be planned in relation to man." Most of the men who wrote and read the new ideas did not mean them for women, enslaved Africans or peasants, though a few did: the French philosopher Condorcet argued for women's rights in 1790. Yet the words were written down, and anyone could ask the philosophers' own question: by what right am I left out?

Abolition: ideas and faith together

In 1787 twelve men in London, nine of them Quakers, a Christian group whose members had come to see slavery as a sin, founded the Society for Effecting the Abolition of the Slave Trade. Its seal showed a kneeling African man in chains and the words "Am I not a man and a brother?", and the potter Josiah Wedgwood turned it into a medallion that supporters wore. Olaudah Equiano, who had been enslaved, published his life story in 1789 and traveled across Britain speaking against the trade.

Abolition came country by country. Britain banned the slave trade in 1807. In 1834 it began ending slavery in most of its empire, though freed people had to keep working for their former owners as unpaid "apprentices" until 1838. France abolished slavery in its colonies in 1794, brought it back in 1802, and ended it again in 1848. Brazil was the last country in the Americas, in 1888. The enslaved people of Saint-Domingue ended slavery there by revolution, which is Topic 5.2.

Serfs and voters

Serfdom ended too. In 1848 the Austrian Empire freed its peasants from the forced labor they owed their lords. In 1861 Tsar Alexander II of Russia freed more than 20 million serfs. He had reportedly told Russian nobles in 1856 that it was better to end serfdom "from above" than to wait for it to end "from below," and Russia's defeat in the Crimean War pushed him too. The freed serfs had to pay for their land over many years.

Suffrage, the right to vote, widened in steps. In 1848 France gave the vote to all adult men. Britain widened voting among men in 1832, 1867 and 1884, and even then many men could not vote.

Common mistake to avoid

Do not write that every expansion of rights came from reformers below. Some came from campaigns and revolutions; some came from rulers like Alexander II, who feared what would happen if they did not act. When you explain a change, say who made it happen and why.

Use this in your answer
Strongest tool, with its mechanism
Name both engines. The reform movements were driven by Enlightenment ideas about natural rights and by religious faith, and they took decades.
Best evidence of exclusion or limit
Ending slavery or serfdom in law did not end hard labor or poverty. Freed people and freed serfs often stayed poor, and other kinds of forced labor went on in many places.
Comparison hook
Against Topic 4.4: the plantation economy had made the Atlantic slave trade huge. By 1888 every country in the Americas had abolished slavery in law.

Terms to use precisely

Abolition
Ending slavery, or ending the slave trade, by law.
Quakers
A Christian group whose members, by the late 1700s, led some of the first campaigns against slavery.
Serfdom
A system in which peasants were bound to the land and owed labor to a lord.
Suffrage
The right to vote.
05

Women's Demands, and What Stayed

1791 to 1900  ·  De Gouges, Wollstonecraft, Seneca Falls

Women pressed the same words too, because the men who wrote about the rights of man often meant only men. Their demands challenged both political and gender hierarchies. By 1900 much had changed, and much had not.

Three demands

In September 1791 the French writer Olympe de Gouges rewrote France's declaration of rights as the Declaration of the Rights of Woman and of the Female Citizen. Where the declaration of 1789 said "men are born and remain free and equal in rights," her first article said: "Woman is born free and remains equal to man in rights." The revolutionary government executed her in November 1793.

In 1792 the English writer Mary Wollstonecraft published A Vindication of the Rights of Woman. She quoted Rousseau, who had warned that if women were educated like men, "the less power will they have over us," and answered: "I do not wish them to have power over men; but over themselves." Her claim was that women can reason, so they must be educated to think and decide for themselves.

In 1840 the American abolitionist Lucretia Mott, a Quaker minister, went to an antislavery convention in London as a delegate and was refused a seat because she was a woman. Elizabeth Cady Stanton, there with her husband, a delegate, sat with Mott and the other women, apart from the men. In July 1848 the two of them, with three other women, organized a women's rights convention in Seneca Falls, New York. Its Declaration of Sentiments, drafted by Stanton with members of the M'Clintock family, copied the Declaration of Independence and added two words: "all men and women are created equal." It complained that a woman had never been "permitted ... to exercise her inalienable right to the elective franchise," the vote. The resolution demanding the vote was the only one not passed unanimously; one hundred women and men signed the declaration.

This was feminism emerging: the claim that women and men should have equal rights. It challenged political hierarchy, who votes and who rules, and gender hierarchy, who is in charge at home and under the law. Under the law of the time, the Seneca Falls declaration complained, a married woman was "civilly dead."

What stayed by 1900

By 1900, kings and emperors still ruled most of Europe and much of the world, many of them sharing power with elected parliaments. Slavery had been abolished across the Americas, but other kinds of forced labor went on in many places. Many countries let some or all men vote. Women could vote in national elections almost nowhere: New Zealand, in 1893, was the first self-governing country where all adult women could vote in national elections.

So the Enlightenment changed the answer to "who does a state belong to?": to its people, by reason and consent, not to a king by God's will. The question it taught people to ask kept working long after the philosophers were gone. That is the change. How slowly the rights reached, and how many people were still waiting in 1900, is the continuity.

Common mistake to avoid

Do not write that rights reached everyone in 1789. The declaration of 1789 left women out of citizenship, France brought back slavery in 1802, and women voted almost nowhere in 1900. But do not swing to "nothing changed": the claim that rights belong to people by birth was new, and people used it.

Continuity and Change

The question changed before the kings did

Before the Enlightenment, rulers said their power came from God, tradition and birth. Locke argued in 1689 that people are born with natural rights and that no one may be put under a government's power without consent; Montesquieu (1748) argued for the separation of powers, and Voltaire (1763) for religious toleration. What changed was what made a ruler legitimate. What continued was that kings still ruled almost everywhere.

Context

The ideas came before the revolutions

The ideas spread through the Encyclopédie (1751 to 1772, banned in France and reprinted in Italy), salons, coffeehouses and letters, and came before the revolutions of 1775, 1789, 1791 and 1810. Nariño's printed translation of the French declaration in Bogotá shows them crossing the Atlantic. If a state belongs to its people, the people could be the nation, and nationalism became a major force of the 1800s.

Continuity and Change

Reformers pressed the words further, slowly

Driven by natural rights and by faith, reformers founded the London antislavery society of 1787 (nine of its twelve founders were Quakers). Britain began ending slavery in most of its empire in 1834 and Brazil, last in the Americas, in 1888. Serfdom ended in Austria in 1848 and Russia in 1861; France gave all adult men the vote in 1848. Each change took decades.

Claims and Evidence

Women kept the words and changed who they covered

De Gouges (1791) wrote that "woman is born free and remains equal to man in rights"; Wollstonecraft (1792) wanted women to have power "over themselves"; the Seneca Falls declaration (1848) said "all men and women are created equal" and demanded the vote. Their claims challenged political and gender hierarchies, but by 1900 women could vote in national elections almost nowhere.

01

The Recipe, and America

1763 to 1787  ·  Grievance, words, chance

Many people read Enlightenment writers and went on obeying their kings. A revolution came when three things met: a grievance many people felt, the words to name it, and a chance to act. America had all three first.

Why ideas were not enough

Topic 5.1 showed that the new ideas about natural rights and the consent of the people had spread across Europe and the Americas by 1789. But reading a book is not the same as risking your life. Across this topic, three ingredients turn up again and again. The first is a grievance: a wrong that many people feel, such as an unfair tax, a privilege for a few, slavery, or a law that shuts people out of power. The second is the words: a document that names the grievance in the language of rights, something people can read aloud and rally around. The third is a chance: a moment when the old ruler is broke, far away, at war, or gone.

Common mistake to avoid

Do not write that the Enlightenment caused the revolutions. The College Board says Enlightenment ideas "influenced resistance" and that "discontent with monarchist and imperial rule" pushed people toward new kinds of government. The ideas were one cause. Name the others.

The grievance and the words

Britain won the Seven Years' War in 1763, but it came out of the war deep in debt. Parliament decided its thirteen American colonies should help pay for the soldiers who guarded them, and it taxed them with laws such as the Sugar Act of 1764 and the Stamp Act of 1765, which taxed newspapers, legal papers and playing cards. The colonists had no members in Parliament, so they answered with a slogan: "no taxation without representation." Protest reached ordinary homes: English potters even sold colonists teapots painted "No Stamp Act."

On 4 July 1776 the Continental Congress adopted the Declaration of Independence. It argued that "all men are created equal," that governments get "their just powers from the consent of the governed," and that when a government destroys people's rights, "it is the Right of the People to alter or to abolish it." That is the social contract from Topic 5.1, turned into a reason to break away.

The chance, and what it created

Britain was an ocean away, and its victory in 1763 had pushed France out of North America, so the colonists no longer needed British soldiers to protect them. Once the fighting began in 1775, France, Britain's old rival, joined the war on the American side in 1778 with money, soldiers and a navy, which helped the colonists win. By 1783 Britain had recognized the independence of the United States. In 1787 the new country wrote a constitution for a republic: a country with no king, whose leaders are elected.

The College Board calls the American Revolution and its republic "a model and inspiration for a number of the revolutions that followed." It was also limited. The republic kept slavery, and most states let only men who owned property vote.

02

France: The Bill Comes Due

1788 to 1804  ·  A kingdom remade

France's help was crucial to the American victory, and paying for it, on top of older war debts, pushed the French king to the edge of bankruptcy. One revolution's effect became the next one's chance.

The chance came from America

France had spent heavily on the American war, on top of the debts of earlier wars, and by 1788 King Louis XVI's government was nearly bankrupt. To raise new taxes, the king called the Estates-General, an assembly of the three "estates" of France: the clergy, the nobles, and everyone else. It had not met since 1614.

The grievance

The first two estates had privileges. The clergy and the nobles paid little in taxes, and the Third Estate, most of the people of France, from rich lawyers to poor peasants, paid most of them. After a bad harvest in 1788, the price of bread, the main food of the poor, soared. When the Estates-General met in May 1789, the Third Estate refused to be outvoted by the other two, and in June it declared itself the National Assembly. On 14 July a Paris crowd stormed the Bastille, a royal fortress and prison, to seize its gunpowder.

The words

In August 1789 the Assembly ended the nobles' special privileges and adopted the Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen. Its first article reads, "Men are born and remain free and equal in rights." Its third reads, "The principle of all sovereignty resides essentially in the nation." Sovereignty is the highest power in a state, and the declaration said it belonged to the nation, not to the king.

How we know: compare the documents

Put two revolutionary documents side by side and read them closely. Say each one's argument in your own words, then find one way the arguments are alike and one way they differ, and explain the difference by the grievance each document answered. That is the move the College Board's skill 3.C asks for.

What it created

France abolished its monarchy in 1792 and became a republic, and in January 1793 it executed the king. At war with much of Europe and afraid of enemies at home, the government began the Terror (1793 to 1794), during which it killed thousands of people it called enemies of the revolution. In 1799 a general, Napoleon Bonaparte, took power, and in 1804 he crowned himself emperor. His armies carried the revolution's laws, such as the end of noble privilege, across Europe, and they also made many Europeans hate French rule.

Haiti: The Words Claimed

1791 to 1825  ·  Saint-Domingue becomes Haiti

France's declaration said men are born free and equal. In Saint-Domingue, the people France held in slavery claimed those words for themselves, rose up first, and won a country of their own.

The grievance

Saint-Domingue, on the western part of the Caribbean island of Hispaniola, was France's most valuable colony. Its sugar and coffee were grown by enslaved Africans and their children, worked and punished brutally on plantations. Around 1789 the colony held more than 400,000 enslaved people and fewer than 80,000 free people, white colonists and free people of color (free people of African or mixed descent) together: more than five enslaved people for every free person.

The words, and the chance

When the 1789 declaration reached the colony, free people of color asked the Assembly in Paris for the equal rights it promised. The Assembly left the question to the colonists, and the white planters refused. In 1790 a free man of color, Vincent Ogé, led a small revolt for those rights, and early in 1791 he was executed. Paris granted free people of color equal rights only in April 1792, after the revolution in the colony had begun.

With France in revolution and the colony's white planters divided, enslaved people in the northern plain rose up in August 1791. They burned plantations across the northern plain. Toussaint Louverture, who had been born enslaved and had won his freedom before the revolution, became the revolution's most famous leader. In 1793, fighting on Spain's side against France, he announced: "I want Liberty and Equality to reign in Saint Domingue."

Common mistake to avoid

Do not write that France gave Haiti its freedom. Enslaved people rose up in 1791. French officials in the colony began freeing enslaved people in 1793 to keep their side's soldiers, and in February 1794 the government in Paris abolished slavery in all French colonies, after two and a half years of fighting. Toussaint changed sides to France a few months later. Haitians then won their independence by defeating Napoleon's army.

What it created

In 1802 Napoleon brought slavery back in other French colonies, such as Guadeloupe, and sent an army to retake Saint-Domingue. Toussaint was arrested and died in a French prison in the mountains in 1803. His officer Jean-Jacques Dessalines fought on, and yellow fever and the war destroyed the French army. On 1 January 1804 Dessalines declared independence and gave the country its old Taíno name, Haiti.

Haiti was the second independent country in the Americas, after the United States, and the first founded by people who had freed themselves from slavery. Its effects reached other countries. In April 1803, with his army in Saint-Domingue dying of yellow fever and a new war with Britain coming, Napoleon sold Louisiana to the United States. Countries that allowed slavery feared Haiti's example: the United States did not recognize Haiti until 1862, and in 1825 France demanded 150 million francs, later cut to 90 million, as the price of recognizing Haiti's independence. And in 1816 Haiti helped the next revolution (section 04).

Spanish America: An Empire With No King

1808 to 1826  ·  Bolívar and the new states

Creoles in Spain's American empire had grievances for decades. Their chance came when Napoleon, the French Revolution's general, took Spain's king, and the last revolution's creation, Haiti, helped them win.

The grievance

In Spain's American empire, creoles, people of Spanish descent born in the Americas, owned land, mines and businesses, but the highest offices in the church, the army and the government went to peninsulares, men born in Spain. Spain also controlled what the colonies could grow, make and trade. Simón Bolívar, a creole from a rich Caracas family, wrote that Spain "has kept us in a sort of permanent infancy with regard to public affairs": treated like children, kept out of running their own affairs.

The chance

In 1808 Napoleon invaded Spain and forced its king from the throne, putting his own brother in his place. Spanish America suddenly had no rightful king to obey. In 1810 creole leaders set up governments of their own, in Caracas and in Buenos Aires, at first in the name of the captive king, and many soon moved to full independence. In Mexico a priest, Miguel Hidalgo, called on Native and mestizo villagers to rise up.

The words, and help from Haiti

The wars went badly at first. In 1815, beaten and in exile on the British island of Jamaica, Bolívar wrote a long letter to a British reader, now called the Letter from Jamaica. "The hatred that the Peninsula has inspired in us," he wrote, "is greater than the ocean between us," and the tie to Spain "has been severed." He also doubted that governments "wholly representative," chosen entirely by the people's votes, suited Spanish America yet.

Then Haiti helped. In 1816 Haiti's president, Alexandre Pétion, gave Bolívar guns, supplies and men for a new expedition, and asked him in return to end slavery in the lands he freed.

What it created

By 1826 Spain had lost every colony in the Americas except Cuba and Puerto Rico. Mexico, Gran Colombia, Peru, Chile, the provinces of the Río de la Plata and others became independent countries, and Brazil broke away from Portugal in 1822 with an emperor of its own. Creole elites took the top places. Most of the new countries kept a sharp social hierarchy, and slavery lasted in several of them for decades: Colombia ended it in 1851, Venezuela and Peru in 1854.

New Ideas, and the Nation

1789 to 1878  ·  Liberalism, democracy, nationalism

Some of the revolutions' longest-lasting effects were ideas: new answers to how a country should be run, and a new loyalty, to the nation, that joined some states together and pulled peoples away from empires.

New ways to govern

Behind all four revolutions was anger at kings and empires, and the revolutions left behind new answers to an old question: how should a country be run? One was the republic, with a written constitution, a set of basic laws that even the government must follow. Another was liberalism: the belief that a constitution and an elected parliament should limit the government and protect each person's rights, such as free speech, a free press and property. Many liberals in the 1800s still wanted only men with property to vote. A third was democracy, rule by the people, which pushed for the vote to reach all men.

These ideas did not stay on paper. They helped set off new revolutions across Europe in 1830 and 1848, when crowds demanded constitutions, parliaments and wider voting. Most of the uprisings of 1848 were crushed within two years, but the demands did not go away.

From "the people" to the nation

The revolutions said that a state belongs to its people. That raised a new question: which people? More and more, people answered with the nation: everyone who shared a language, a religion, customs and a homeland. Nationalism put loyalty to the nation above loyalty to a king or an empire.

Governments learned to use that loyalty. In 1793, at war with much of Europe, revolutionary France ordered that "all Frenchmen are in permanent requisition for the service of the armies": young men would fight, married men would make weapons, women would make tents and work in hospitals, and old people would go to public squares to preach "the unity of the Republic." But nationalism could also be turned against a government, by people who felt they were a nation without a country of their own.

Nationalism joins states

Italy was split into several states, some ruled by Austria. Italian nationalists, among them Count Cavour, the chief minister of the kingdom of Piedmont-Sardinia, and the soldier Giuseppe Garibaldi, whose volunteers took Sicily and Naples in 1860, joined most of Italy into one kingdom in 1861, and added Rome in 1870. Joining states into one is called unification.

Germany was split into more than thirty states. In 1848 German liberals met in Frankfurt to unite Germany with an elected parliament and a constitution, and failed. Then Otto von Bismarck, the chief minister of Prussia, told Prussia's lawmakers in 1862 that the great questions of the time are not decided "by speeches and majority resolutions," which had been "the big mistake of 1848 and 1849," but "by iron and blood." After three wars, against Denmark, Austria and France, the German Empire was proclaimed on 18 January 1871 in the Hall of Mirrors at Versailles, the palace Louis XIV built.

Nationalism breaks an empire

The Ottoman Empire ruled many peoples in the Balkans, in southeastern Europe, Muslim and Christian. Serbs rebelled and won the right to rule themselves. Greeks rose up in 1821, and in 1830 Britain, France and Russia declared Greece an independent state; it became a kingdom under a Bavarian prince in 1832. Other Balkan peoples followed, and in 1878 Serbia, Montenegro and Romania were recognized as independent.

The Ottoman government answered with a loyalty of its own, Ottomanism. In 1856 it promised equal treatment to all its subjects, Muslim or not, and in 1869 a law made every subject an Ottoman citizen. The idea was one loyalty for everyone, to the empire, instead of to a nation. It did not stop the Balkan nations from leaving. The idea reached beyond Europe, too: in the Spanish-ruled Philippines, Filipino writers in the Propaganda Movement of the 1880s and 1890s, José Rizal among them, demanded equal rights with Spaniards, and Spain executed Rizal in 1896, during a revolution against its rule.

Common mistake to avoid

Do not write that nationalism only built countries. The same loyalty joined Italy and Germany together and pulled Greece and Serbia away from the Ottoman Empire. A cause can push in opposite directions.

Coal, Iron, and Not Enough Wood

1600s to 1709  ·  What nature gave

Britain had plenty of coal and iron, often close together and close to water, and too little wood. The shortage of wood pushed Britons to burn coal, and then to make iron with it.

How things were made in 1750

In 1750 almost everything people owned was made by hand. Most thread and cloth were made in homes. Under the putting-out system, a merchant brought raw wool or cotton to families in the countryside, who spun and wove it on their own wheels and looms, and he came back to collect the work and pay for each piece. Power came from human muscles, horses, wind and water wheels. Much of the world's cotton cloth came from India, and Britain's cotton makers grew up copying it.

Over the next century Britain built a new way of making things. The College Board lists seven factors that led to it. Two come from nature: waterways, and where coal, iron and timber were found. Five were built by people: towns, better farming, laws that protected property, resources from abroad, and saved-up money. This chapter takes them in that order.

Too little wood

By the 1600s most of Britain's old forests had been cut down, as a growing population cleared land for farms. Wood became expensive. That mattered, because wood was the fuel of the old economy. It heated homes, and it made charcoal, the fuel used to heat iron ore until the iron could be separated out. Much of the iron Britain used was bought from Sweden, and later from Russia, where forests were still large.

Britain did have coal, in huge beds, much of it close to the surface and close to rivers or the sea. So Britons burned coal instead of wood. By 1700 coal gave England about half of all its energy, much of it burned in fireplaces for heating and cooking.

Common mistake to avoid

Do not write that Britain industrialized because it had coal. Coal was one of seven factors, and China had coal too. A strong answer says what coal made cheaper or possible, and names other factors that made it worth using.

Iron made with coal

Coal could not simply replace charcoal in an iron furnace: its impurities spoiled the iron. In 1709, at Coalbrookdale, a valley beside the River Severn in Shropshire, Abraham Darby made iron using coke, a fuel made by baking coal to drive the impurities off. Coalbrookdale had coal and iron ore close by and a river to carry what it made. At first coke iron was used mostly for cast-iron pots. It spread slowly, mainly after the 1750s, as charcoal iron and imported iron grew dearer, and over the following decades ironmakers learned to make better iron with coal.

How we know: the furnace is still there

The furnace where Darby first made iron with coke survives at Coalbrookdale, rebuilt and enlarged in 1777, part of the Ironbridge Gorge World Heritage Site. Historic England, which lists the site, dates Darby's first iron made with coke there to 1709. Historians argue about how much the wood shortage caused the switch to coal and how much growing demand for fuel did; both were at work.

Water: Moving Heavy Things and Turning Wheels

1700s to 1761  ·  What nature gave, improved

Before railways, the cheapest way to move something heavy was to float it, and the fastest way to power a machine was a water wheel. Britain had coast, rivers and fast streams, and people added canals.

Coast, rivers and streams

Coal is heavy, and so is iron. Carrying them by cart on the roads of the 1700s was slow and expensive. Carrying them by water was cheap. Britain had a long coastline and rivers that boats could travel, and coal from the mines near Newcastle went by ship down the coast to London.

Fast streams mattered too. A stream could turn a water wheel, and a water wheel could turn machines all day. The first factories were built beside fast water, as section 05 shows.

Canals: water improved by people

Where nature had put no river, people dug one. A canal is a waterway dug by hand. The Duke of Bridgewater owned coal mines at Worsley, a few miles from Manchester. In 1759 and 1760 Parliament passed acts allowing him to build a canal. In 1761 it opened from Worsley to Stretford, on the edge of Manchester, and the price of coal in Manchester soon fell by about half. By 1765 it reached the town itself.

Other canal builders followed. Within a few decades canals linked coalfields, industrial towns and ports across England. Cheap fuel and cheap carriage drew mills to the places the water reached.

Common mistake to avoid

"Britain had rivers" is a fact, not an explanation. Add what the water did and what that made cheaper, with a "so."

Food, People and Towns

1700s to 1801  ·  What people built

British farmers grew more food on the same land. More food fed more people, a smaller share of them farmed, and towns filled with workers and buyers.

Better farming

Farmers had long left fields empty every few years to let the soil rest. British farmers learned to plant turnips and clover instead. Turnips fed cattle and sheep through the winter, and clover made the soil richer. More animals meant more manure for the fields, and more manure meant more grain. These crops had been grown this way in Flanders first; British farmers spread them.

Landowners also fenced off the old shared village fields into private farms, often with an act of Parliament. Between 1750 and 1819 Parliament passed more than three thousand of these acts. This enclosure let a landowner farm his land the new way. It also took away the shared land that poorer villagers had used, and historians still argue about how many people it pushed off the land.

More people, fewer of them farming

More food fed more people. Britain's population grew from about 8 million in 1761 to about 10.5 million in 1801, the year of the first census. And a smaller share of those people farmed: in 1700 almost half of English working men worked on the land, and by about 1800 estimates range from about a third to about two in five.

How we know: counting backward

No full count of farm output exists before the 1860s. Historians work it out backward: Britain's population grew, people ate at least as well, and most of the food was still grown at home, so farms must have produced much more. The estimates differ widely. Some find that farm productivity more than doubled between 1700 and 1850; others, such as the economic historian Gregory Clark, find much slower growth, with imported food feeding part of the larger population.

Towns

Many of the people who did not farm lived in towns. Urbanization, the growth of towns and cities, put many workers close together, and gave them neighbors to sell to. Manchester, a town of about 23,000 people in 1773, counted 70,409 in the census of 1801.

Laws, Cotton and Capital

1624 to 1774  ·  What people built

Building a mill was a big risk. Laws that protected property, cotton from abroad and saved-up money made the risk worth taking, and the factors worked together.

Laws that protected property

In Britain, Parliament, where landowners and merchants sat, passed the laws that enclosed fields and allowed canals to be dug. A law of 1624 banned most monopolies but kept the patent: the crown could grant the true and first inventor the sole right to use a new invention for up to fourteen years. In 1769 Richard Arkwright patented a new spinning machine. A person who invested could expect the law to protect what he built.

The protection had limits. Arkwright's 1769 spinning patent ran out in 1783, and in 1785 a court canceled his 1775 patent on his carding and preparing machines, finding his written description of them unclear; the court also heard claims that other men had invented them.

Cotton from abroad

Cotton does not grow in Britain's climate. By the 1780s most of the cotton spun in Manchester was grown by enslaved people on plantations in the Caribbean and South America. After the 1790s more and more of it came from plantations in the United States. Britain used about 2.6 million pounds of raw cotton in 1760, almost all of it spun by hand, and about 51.6 million pounds in 1800. The factory's raw material came from far away, and most of it was grown by people who were not free. (In the 1780s a quarter or more came from the Ottoman Empire, through the port of Smyrna.)

Capital

Capital is money used to build something that makes more money. Merchants, bankers and landowners had grown rich from trade, including the Atlantic trade in enslaved people and the sugar they grew, and some were ready to risk their savings on mills and machines. Arkwright's partners, Samuel Need and Jedediah Strutt, were stocking makers who had grown rich in the hosiery trade. The partners spent thousands of pounds on machines and mills before the business made a profit, at a time when a carpenter earned no more than about £36 a year.

Common mistake to avoid

Do not write that the slave trade paid for the Industrial Revolution. Historians still argue about how much of Britain's capital came from slavery and the slave trade, so say "some" or "part." What historians agree on is that most of the cotton itself was grown by enslaved people.

How the factors combined

None of these factors made an industrial revolution by itself. Watch five of them meet in one project. The Bridgewater Canal needed coal under the ground at Worsley (nature). It needed water to float the coal on (nature, improved by people). It needed acts of Parliament, passed in 1759 and 1760, to allow it to be built (law). It needed the duke's money, and he had to borrow more to pay his workmen (capital). And it needed a growing town to buy the coal (urbanization). Cheap coal then made Manchester an even better place to build mills, and the new mills made the town grow faster still.

The Factory: One Place, One Job Each

1771 to 1835  ·  The factory system

The factors made it pay to build mills. The factory then changed work itself in two ways: it gathered production in one place, and it split the work into small, specialized jobs.

One place

In 1771, Arkwright and his partners built a cotton-spinning mill at Cromford, in Derbyshire's Derwent valley. A water wheel turned the machines. It was fed by a brook and by the Cromford Sough, a tunnel that drained the nearby lead mines; because its water came from underground, it was said never to freeze.

The mill did not send cotton out to spinners' homes. It brought the workers to the machines. Everyone came to one building, at the hours the mill set, under the eye of an overseer. The village was too small to supply enough workers, so Arkwright built houses for them. Most of his workers were women and children, some as young as seven. This was the factory system: production concentrated in a single place, around one source of power. UNESCO, which protects the valley as a World Heritage Site, says it "saw the birth of the factory system."

Common mistake to avoid

Do not call Cromford the first factory, or say the steam engine started the factory system. A silk mill at Derby had some of the same features in the 1720s, and Cromford ran on water. Steam power is Topic 5.5.

One job each

Inside a cotton mill, the work was split into steps. Raw cotton was cleaned, then carded (combed so its fibers lay straight), drawn out, twisted into a loose rope called roving, and spun into thread. Cromford began with machines for spinning, and in 1775 Arkwright patented machines for the earlier steps too. From then on each step after cleaning had its own machine, tended by workers who did that job and no other. This is specialization of labor.

Adam Smith, a Scottish thinker, showed why it mattered in 1776, using a small hand workshop that made pins. Ten men shared about eighteen steps, each doing only one, two, or three of them, and together they "could make among them upwards of forty-eight thousand pins in a day." A worker who had not learned the trade, working alone, he wrote, "could scarce ... make one pin in a day, and certainly could not make twenty." Because each job was small and simple, it could be learned quickly, so a mill could hire workers with no training, many of them children.

How we know: writers of the time

In 1835 Andrew Ure, a writer who admired factories, defined one as "the combined operation of many orders of work-people, adult and young, in tending with assiduous skill a system of productive machines continuously impelled by a central power": many kinds of workers, many machines, one power source. He was praising the system, so read him for what a factory was, not for how it felt to work there.

The World's Makers in 1750

About 1750  ·  The starting point

In 1750 almost everything was made by hand, at home or in workshops, and most of the world's manufactured goods were made in China and India.

Made by hand

In 1750 a shirt, a pot, a plow or a ship was made by people using hand tools, at home or in small workshops. This is hand production. Power came from muscles, animals, wind and water wheels. That was true in Britain, too: Arkwright's mill at Cromford was still twenty years away.

The places that made the most were the places with the most people and the most skilled craftworkers. India's cotton cloth, from fine thin muslins to cheap printed calicoes, was sold across the Indian Ocean, in the Middle East, in West Africa and in Europe. Chinese silk, porcelain and cotton cloth filled China's own huge markets and traveled abroad. Indian shipbuilders built ocean-going ships of teak.

Who made how much

Nobody counted every piece of cloth in the world in 1750. But the economic historian Paul Bairoch made careful estimates, working backward from what is known about people and their work. He estimated that around 1750 China made about a third of all the world's manufactured goods and India about a quarter. All of Europe together made about a quarter. Britain made under 2 percent.

How we know: estimates, not counts

Bairoch's figures were published in 1982 and reprinted in Paul Kennedy's The Rise and Fall of the Great Powers (1987). They are built from guesses about population and output, and historians who use them say they are rough. Use them to show the size of the change, never as exact amounts.

Keeping the Secret, and Losing It

1789 to 1824  ·  The methods leave Britain

Britain tried to keep its new methods at home by law. They left anyway, first in the heads of skilled people.

The laws

Britain's leaders knew the new machines were worth a great deal. British law banned skilled workers in the textile trades from leaving to work abroad, and banned the export of textile machines. The ban on workers leaving was repealed in 1824; the last bans on exporting machinery were lifted in 1843.

Samuel Slater

Samuel Slater learned the cotton trade as an apprentice at Jedediah Strutt's mill at Belper, in Derbyshire. Strutt was Arkwright's partner, so Slater learned Arkwright's machines from the inside. In 1789 he sailed for the United States without telling anyone what he knew. At Pawtucket, Rhode Island, working with American merchants, he built water-powered spinning machines from memory. The mill that still stands there was begun in 1793 and enlarged several times afterward.

How we know: the mill is still there

The Slater Mill still stands at Pawtucket, part of the Blackstone River Valley National Historical Park, run by the National Park Service. It was begun in 1793 and enlarged several times by the 1830s, so a photograph of it today shows the enlarged mill, not the first one.

John Cockerill

In Belgium, John Cockerill, the son of an English machine maker, began building machines at Seraing, near Liège, in 1817, and added ironworks there in the 1820s. Liège sat on one of the richest coalfields on the continent of Europe, with rivers to carry goods. The pattern from Topic 5.3, coal near water, was waiting there.

Machines, Money and Coal

1820s to 1900  ·  Other parts of Europe, the US, Russia, Japan

Once machines and money could travel too, the methods spread faster, and most took root near coal, or by rivers that could turn wheels: in other parts of Europe, in the United States, and later in Russia and Japan.

Other parts of Europe

The first railway in the German states, from Nuremberg to Fürth, opened in 1835 with a locomotive built in England. On the continent, factories grew where coal lay, as they had in Britain: around Liège in Belgium, in the Ruhr valley of the German states, and in northern France.

The United States

In the United States, wealthy Boston merchants paid for big cotton mills at Lowell, Massachusetts, from the 1820s, beside a river that could turn their wheels. Most of the workers were young women from New England farms. By 1900 the United States made more of the world's manufactured goods than any other country.

Russia and Japan

Russia and Japan came later, mostly after 1870. A Welsh engineer, John Hughes, built ironworks in the coal country of southern Russia in the early 1870s; the town that grew up around them was named Yuzovka, after him. In Japan the Osaka Spinning Company opened in 1883 with cotton-spinning machines made in Britain, driven by steam power, and it ran day and night.

The Shares Move

1750 to 1900  ·  What changed

Steam-powered factories made goods far faster and more cheaply than hands could, so the regions that had them made a fast-growing share of the world's goods, and the share made in Asia fell.

What changed

A share is a part of a whole. In Bairoch's estimates, Britain's share of the world's manufacturing went from under 2 percent in 1750 to about a fifth by 1860. By 1900 Europe and the United States together made more than four-fifths of the world's manufactured goods, and the United States had passed Britain as the largest maker.

Over the same years, China's share fell from about a third to about 6 percent, and India's from about a quarter to under 2 percent.

A share is not an amount

Imagine a pizza that grows much bigger. Your slice can stay the same size while your share of the pizza shrinks. The world was making far more goods in 1900 than in 1750. So a region's share can fall even while it keeps making goods, because the total grows faster. For India, though, Bairoch's estimates suggest it also made less per person over time: it lost ground, not only share.

Common mistake to avoid

Do not write that Asia "stopped manufacturing." The College Board's own sentence says Middle Eastern and Asian countries "continued to produce manufactured goods" while their share "declined." A falling share is the change; the making that went on is the continuity.

The Old Makers Kept Making

1750 to 1900  ·  What continued, and why the share fell

India, China and the Middle East kept making cloth, ships and other goods, mostly by hand. Their share shrank because the rest of the world made far more, because machine-made goods were cheaper, and because of the trade rules of the people in power, among other causes historians still argue about.

What continued

In India, hand weaving shrank, but it did not die. Weavers kept working cloth on handlooms, and many switched to cheaper machine-spun thread so they could keep weaving. The economic historian Tirthankar Roy finds that handloom cloth held a roughly steady share of India's cloth market from the 1890s to the 1930s, and in 1908, in one province of northern India alone, about a million people still lived from handloom weaving. In China, farm families kept weaving cloth at home, more and more of it from cheaper machine-made thread. In the Ottoman Empire, the number of handlooms in the Syrian cities of Aleppo and Damascus held steady from 1860 to 1914.

Indian shipbuilders kept building, too. At the Bombay dockyard, the shipyard of the city now called Mumbai, the Wadia family of master shipbuilders built teak warships for Britain's own Royal Navy. One of them, HMS Trincomalee, launched in 1817, is still afloat today in Hartlepool, England, where its museum calls it Britain's oldest warship still afloat.

Why the share fell

One reason the share fell is that the rest of the world made far more goods. Another is that the old makers lost buyers. Cloth from Lancashire's machines was cheaper than cloth woven by hand, even in India itself. In 1815 India sold about £1.3 million worth of cotton cloth abroad and bought only about £26,000 worth from England. By 1832 it sold less than £100,000 worth and bought more than £400,000 worth. From about 1830, India bought more cotton cloth than it sold. India, which had sold cloth to the world, now bought British cloth, and it sold raw cotton to British mills.

Power mattered too. Much of India was ruled by the British East India Company, and British cotton cloth entered India at a tax of only 2½ percent. Meanwhile Britain taxed Indian cloth heavily: around 1813, Indian calicoes paid taxes of about 70 to 85 percent of their value to be sold in Britain. Historians still argue about how much each cause mattered: cheaper machine-made goods, the trade rules of the people in power, and, in India, troubles that had begun before the factories did, such as the breakup of the Mughal Empire and years of drought.

The Middle East felt the same pressure. In 1838 English cotton cloth sold in Egypt for less than cloth from Egypt's own new mills. (Who built those mills, and why, is Topic 5.6.)

Shipbuilding followed the same path. Bombay's teak ships were among the best wooden ships of their day. But by the middle of the 1800s navies were turning to ships of iron, driven by steam, and they came mostly from British shipyards and engine works. Bombay's age of teak warships came to an end.

Common mistake to avoid

Do not give one cause as the whole answer, either way. "British machines were simply better" leaves out the trade rules; "colonial rule alone destroyed Indian industry" leaves out the cheaper machines and the weavers who kept weaving. A strong answer names more than one cause and says historians weigh them differently.

What Happens When Freight Gets Cheap

1825 to 1900  ·  Rails and national markets

The railway’s importance is not speed. It is the collapse in the cost of moving heavy things overland, which had been the binding constraint on economic life for the whole of this course, and everything else follows from removing it.

The constraint it removed

Before railways, moving bulk goods overland was so expensive that grain or coal became worthless within a modest distance of where it was produced. That is why every earlier chapter in this course puts trade on water: the Topic 2.3 chapter's point that a ship carries rice when a camel cannot is the same point. Canals helped enormously and could only go where the terrain allowed and froze in winter.

The Stockton and Darlington opened in 1825 and the Liverpool and Manchester in 1830, and the second is the one that mattered, because it connected a manufacturing city to a port and proved the commercial case. Britain then built a network within a generation, and every industrializing state followed, often with the state financing it, as the Topic 5.4 chapter describes.

Four consequences, and each is a mechanism worth writing. A national market: when it costs little to move goods from an interior town to a port, a producer sells to the whole country rather than to the county, which rewards large-scale production and destroys local producers who had been protected by distance. Interior settlement: land far from navigable water becomes worth farming, which is what fills the American Midwest, the Argentine pampas and the Ukrainian grain belt, and what floods European markets with cheap grain in the 1870s. Standard time: railways cannot run safely on local solar time, so Britain adopted railway time and the world eventually adopted time zones, which means the clock everyone lives by is a railway artifact. Cities that need not be ports: a factory can sit at a rail junction, and Chicago is the pure case.

What the railway needed back

The interdependence runs in both directions, and this is the half that makes the argument. A railway required coal, in enormous quantities, so it expanded the coal industry that had produced the engine that made it possible. It required iron and then steel for rails, locomotives, bridges and wheels, on a scale that transformed metallurgy and created the demand Bessemer answered. It required civil engineering: cuttings, embankments, tunnels and bridges, which built a profession. And it required capital on a new scale, since a railway is expensive before it earns anything, which is exactly why the joint-stock company and the stock exchange of Topic 5.7 matter when they do.

And it broke its own rails, constantly. Wrought iron rails under heavy locomotives wore out in a few years, and the resulting demand for something better is a direct commercial pressure on the steel problem, which is where section 02 begins.

How we know: prices converged

The claim that railways created national markets is testable, and economic historians test it with price series. If a market is integrated, the price of a bulk commodity in two distant places should converge, because a large gap invites someone to buy in the cheap place and sell in the dear one until the gap is only the cost of carriage. Grain and coal price series across the nineteenth century show exactly that: wide regional divergence before the railway and close tracking after it, and the same convergence appears between continents once steamships and the Suez Canal cut ocean freight. It is a good example of a claim about structure being demonstrated by ordinary commercial records rather than by anyone’s testimony.

Cheap Steel and Instant News

1844 to 1880s  ·  Bessemer and the telegraph

Two changes in the middle of the century removed the remaining ceilings. One made the strongest available structural material cheap enough to use by the mile, and the other made a message independent of anything carrying it, over any distance, in any weather, at a price ordinary commerce could pay.

The Bessemer process

Understand the problem first. Wrought iron is soft and bends; cast iron is hard and brittle and shatters under shock; steel, iron with a controlled small proportion of carbon, is both strong and tough, and before 1856 it was made in small batches by slow processes and cost so much that it was used for tools, springs and blades rather than for structures.

Henry Bessemer's converter, announced in 1856, blew air through molten pig iron. The oxygen burned out the excess carbon and other impurities, and the burning generated the heat, so the process needed little additional fuel and took minutes rather than days. Cost per ton fell dramatically, and steel output rose by orders of magnitude across the following decades. Later improvements matter and can be named briefly: the open-hearth process allowed better control and the use of scrap, and the Gilchrist Thomas basic lining of 1879 allowed the use of the phosphoric ores that most of continental Europe actually had, which is a large part of why German steel output overtook Britain's.

Then the consequences, which are the interdependence the criteria want. Rails that lasted many times longer than iron ones, which is what let railways carry heavier loads at higher speeds. Bridges and, with elevators, the steel-framed buildings that make a tall city possible. Ships of steel rather than iron, larger and lighter. Machinery and machine tools able to take higher stresses. And armaments, which is the Unit 6 connection: cheap steel is also artillery, and the industrial gap of Topic 5.4 becomes a military gap.

The telegraph

Get the claim right, because the obvious version of it is false. The telegraph was not the first way of sending a message faster than a horse. Beacon chains are ancient, and from 1794 Claude Chappe's optical telegraph relayed coded signals between hilltop towers across France, carrying a message from Paris to Lille in minutes. What the optical system could not do was work at night, work in fog, cross an ocean, or carry the traffic of ordinary business, because every message passed through a chain of operators who could read it and it belonged to the state.

So the break of 1844 is about scale and reliability, not about speed alone. Before it, a message that had to cross an ocean or arrive in bad weather still traveled at the speed of the fastest ship, which meant London to Calcutta took months. Samuel Morse's line between Washington and Baltimore in 1844 changed that, and the network spread along the railways, which is not a coincidence: railways needed telegraphs to signal whether a single-track section was clear, and telegraphs needed the railway's cleared, patrolled right of way to run wire along.

Submarine cable came next, across the Channel in 1851, a durable transatlantic cable in 1866 after earlier failures, and lines to India, Australia and around the world within the following decade. By the 1870s a merchant in London could know a price in Bombay the same day.

What that changed, stated as mechanisms. Commerce: goods could be bought and sold against known prices in distant markets rather than against a guess two months old, which reduced risk, and it also let commodity prices converge worldwide and made futures markets possible. Empire: an order from London could reach a colonial governor in hours, which centralized decisions that distance had previously delegated, and the Topic 4.4 chapter's account of overlapping jurisdictions as a solution to slow communication becomes obsolete. News: wire services and mass newspapers, which is part of how the national publics of Topic 5.2 were made. War: mobilization by timetable, which the railways enabled and the telegraph coordinated.

Common mistake to avoid

Do not present these as separate inventions in a list, which is the form the criteria explicitly ask you to avoid. Write the chain: coal mines flooded, so an engine was built to pump them; the engine needed coal, so more mines opened; moving coal needed railways; railways wore out iron rails, which made cheap steel commercially urgent; Bessemer steel in 1856 made rails, bridges and ships possible at scale; railways needed telegraphs to signal, and telegraphs used railway rights of way. Each link is a bottleneck created by the previous solution. Two links written that way demonstrate the interdependence better than eight inventions listed.

The Environment Where the Factories Were

c. 1800 to 1900  ·  Coal, rivers, forests

An economy that burns fossil carbon at scale rewrites its own landscape, and Britain did it first and most completely. This is the visible half of industrialization’s environmental cost, and it fell on the people who lived in the industrial cities.

Coal smoke

Industrial cities burned coal for power, for heating and for domestic cooking simultaneously, in valleys that often trapped the smoke. The result was continuous and severe air pollution: soot that blackened buildings, sulfurous smogs that reduced visibility to a few yards, and respiratory disease as an ordinary condition of urban life. Contemporaries did not need instruments to detect it, and their descriptions of Manchester, Sheffield and the Black Country are consistent and vivid.

It was understood as a cost and treated as an acceptable one. Britain legislated on smoke abatement from the mid-century and enforcement was weak, because smoke was read as the visible sign of employment, and a factory owner prosecuted for smoke could point at the wages he paid. That trade-off, jobs against air, is a nineteenth-century argument that has not gone away.

And the natural world recorded it. The peppered moth's dark form spread through industrial regions because soot-darkened trees made the pale form conspicuous to birds, which is one of the most cited cases of observed natural selection and is, for this chapter, evidence that the change in the atmosphere was large enough to reshape a species within decades.

Water, forests and the carbon nobody was counting

Rivers became industrial sewers, receiving dye works, tanneries, bleach and gasworks waste along with untreated human sewage from cities with no system to handle it. The Thames in the mid-century was effectively anaerobic through London, and the Topic 5.9 chapter takes up what that did to public health and Topic 5.8 what was eventually built about it.

Forests fell, and here the geography matters. Britain had largely deforested earlier and shifted to coal, which is one reason it could industrialize on the scale it did. Where industrialization came later or where wood remained the fuel, the clearance was contemporaneous: eastern North America, central Europe, and, for railway sleepers, mine props and steamship fuel, enormous quantities of timber worldwide. A railway consumes forest in the form of sleepers as continuously as it consumes coal.

And the change no one was measuring: the systematic burning of fossil carbon began here. Svante Arrhenius calculated in 1896 that changing atmospheric carbon dioxide would change global surface temperature, and he expected any effect to be slow and thought it might be beneficial. It is worth knowing that the physical relationship was identified by a nineteenth-century scientist working on the ice ages, because it dates the knowledge, and the modern consequences of this period's energy choice belong to Unit 9 rather than here.

The Footprint Was Somewhere Else

c. 1840 to 1910  ·  Guano, rubber, and who dug them

An industrial economy consumes raw materials it does not produce, so its environmental and human cost is exported to wherever those materials are. Two commodities carry this argument better than any others, because both were worthless before industry needed them and catastrophic afterward.

Guano: fertilizer, indenture, debt and war

Guano is accumulated seabird excrement, and on the rainless islands off Peru's Pacific coast it had built up in deposits tens of meters deep over millennia. It is extraordinarily rich in nitrogen and phosphorus, which is to say it is concentrated fertilizer, and industrializing Europe needed exactly that: cities full of factory workers had to be fed from soils that were being cropped harder than ever, and European agriculture was running short of nitrogen.

From the 1840s Peru exported guano on an enormous scale, mostly to Britain and the United States, and the state took the revenue through contracts with foreign merchant houses. Digging it was appalling work, in choking ammoniac dust, in heat, on cliffs, and it was done substantially by indentured Chinese laborers, perhaps ninety thousand or more brought to Peru under contracts obtained by deception or coercion, alongside convicts and enslaved and formerly enslaved people. Mortality and suicide were high enough to generate diplomatic complaint and eventually an end to the traffic.

Then the whole arc, which is why this is the best case in the chapter. The revenue was borrowed against, so Peru built railways and public works on guano-backed debt; the accessible deposits were depleted within a few decades; the attention moved to nitrates in the Atacama, which Peru and Bolivia and Chile disputed and which produced the War of the Pacific from 1879, in which Chile took the nitrate territory; Peru defaulted; and then, in 1909 to 1913, the Haber-Bosch process for fixing atmospheric nitrogen made industrial fertilizer without any deposit at all, and the commodity that had built and broken an economy stopped mattering.

Write that sequence and you have demonstrated, in one commodity, resource booms, coerced migrant labor, debt dependency, a war over raw materials, and technological obsolescence. It is the whole century.

Rubber: the Congo and the Amazon

Rubber was a curiosity until Goodyear's vulcanization in the 1840s made it stable across temperatures, and then industry could not get enough of it: it insulated telegraph and electrical cable, sealed steam machinery, and made the drive belts that transmitted power from engine to machine in every factory. Bicycle and then automobile tires multiplied demand again from the 1890s. Rubber came from wild trees in tropical forests, which meant tapping had to be done by people spread across enormous areas, and that is the problem the systems below were built to solve.

In the Congo Free State, held from 1885 as the personal possession of Leopold II of Belgium, the answer was terror. Villages were assigned rubber quotas; a state force, the Force Publique, and concession-company militias enforced them by taking women and children hostage against delivery, by flogging, and by mutilation, with severed hands collected as proof that ammunition had been used on people. Villages that could not meet a quota, as wild vines were exhausted and tappers had to go further, were burned. Estimates of the population decline over the period run into the millions, from killing, starvation, flight and disease in a population under sustained duress, and while the numbers are contested the mechanism is not.

In the Amazon, during the boom centered on Manaus and Iquitos, the pattern was debt bondage: a tapper was advanced supplies at prices he could not repay from what he produced, and was held by the debt. On the Putumayo, the Peruvian Amazon Company's operations against Indigenous peoples involved documented killing, torture and enslavement on a scale that produced a British parliamentary investigation.

The boom ended for a technological reason worth knowing. Seeds taken from Brazil to Kew and then to Southeast Asia established plantation rubber in Malaya and the Dutch East Indies, which was far cheaper to produce than wild tapping, and the Amazon economy collapsed. Plantation rubber had its own coercive labor systems, so this is a relocation rather than a resolution.

How we know: the reformers used the company&rsquo;s own numbers

The Congo case was exposed largely by people using the state’s own paperwork against it. E. D. Morel, working as a shipping clerk in Liverpool, noticed that ships arriving from the Congo carried rubber and ivory of enormous value while ships going out carried mainly guns, ammunition and chains rather than trade goods, and concluded that nothing was being purchased, since no commerce was taking place. Missionary testimony and photographs followed, and Roger Casement’s official British consular report of 1904 investigated on the ground; Casement later reported on the Putumayo in the same way. It is a good model of historical argument: a discrepancy in ordinary commercial records, which the company had no motive to falsify because it did not expect anyone to read them that way, supported by direct testimony gathered afterward.

Interdependence

Each technology solved a bottleneck the last one created

That is a chain of causes, not a list of inventions.

Change

1844 is when information stopped depending on the weather and the sea

Optical signaling was older than this, since Chappe’s tower relays crossed France from 1794 in minutes, but they failed at night, in fog and at the coast, so any message crossing water still moved at the speed of a ship and every distant administration delegated authority to compensate for stale information. That is why the Mongol yam and the Roman post differ from each other in degree and from an electrical cable in kind. Morse’s Washington to Baltimore line in 1844, the Channel cable in 1851 and the durable transatlantic cable of 1866 broke it. Environment

Smoke was profitable because nobody paid for it

Industrial cities burned coal for power, heat and cooking at once, producing soot, sulfurous smogs and routine respiratory disease, while dye works, tanneries and gasworks turned rivers into industrial sewers. Britain legislated on smoke abatement from mid-century and enforced it weakly, because a factory owner prosecuted for smoke could point at the wages he paid, and the trade-off of jobs against air was resolved in favor of jobs. The producer paid for coal, labor and machinery and not for the atmosphere, so the cost was real and fell on whoever breathed it, which is what an externality is. The peppered moth’s dark form spreading through sooted regions is the change registering in a species within decades.

Two Classes That Had Not Existed Before

c. 1780 to 1900  ·  Bourgeoisie and proletariat

Europe had always had rich and poor. What industrialization produced was two groups defined by their relationship to productive property rather than by birth, and the fact that neither of them fitted the old order is the reason the nineteenth century argued so much.

What each word names

The bourgeoisie is the class that owns capital: factory owners, mine owners, merchants, bankers, and around them the professionals and managers whose position depends on the same economy. Its wealth comes from ownership and enterprise rather than from land held by inherited title, and its status is not conferred by birth, which is precisely why the landed aristocracy of the old order despised it and why it wanted political power to match its money.

The proletariat is the class that owns no productive property and lives by selling its labor for wages. That definition is doing more work than it appears to. A peasant with a strip of land, a craftsman with his own tools, or a household with a loom in the parlor all owned the means of producing something, however poorly they lived. A factory worker owns nothing but the capacity to work, which means the only thing he can withhold is his labor, and that single fact explains why the strike becomes the characteristic weapon of the century and why Topic 5.8 is about the law's response to it.

So the novelty is structural rather than a matter of wealth. Both classes are defined by a relationship to productive property, which is a way of sorting people that neither feudal rank nor the estates of Topic 5.1 had used.

Why the old order could not absorb them

The traditional order had a place for a lord, a peasant, a priest and a guild master, and it justified each by tradition, mutual obligation and divine order. It had no place for a self-made cotton manufacturer richer than a duke, and no obligation running toward a man employed by a company that owed him nothing but his wage on Friday.

The bourgeoisie therefore demanded political representation proportionate to its wealth, which is what the Reform Act of 1832 in section 04 partly concedes. And the proletariat, having no property qualification and no customary protector, demanded either the vote or a different economy, which is what everything in section 02 after liberalism is about.

A word of caution about the vocabulary. These are analytical categories rather than groups people always used of themselves, and both were internally divided: a skilled engineer and a casual dock laborer had little in common beyond wages, and a small workshop master sat somewhere between the two. Use the terms as tools and do not treat them as tribes.

What Causes This, and What Should Be Done

c. 1776 to 1867  ·  The ideologies

Ask one question of every ideology in this topic, what causes industrial poverty and what is the remedy, and they stop being definitions to memorize and become arguments you can compare. Five answers, and each one implies a different policy.

Liberalism and conservatism

Liberalism. Core commitments: individual rights, free markets, government limited to protecting person, property and contract, careers open to talent, and a constitution rather than an absolute monarch. Its answer on poverty is that it is a stage and a price. Markets allocate labor and capital efficiently; interference makes things worse; poverty reflects the state of productive capacity, or an individual's failure, or an oversupply of labor, and the remedy is growth plus the removal of restrictions on trade and enterprise. Note that this is the ideology of the bourgeoisie in the same sense the aristocracy had monarchism, and that liberals were reformers rather than conservatives, since they were attacking aristocratic privilege, established churches and trade restrictions.

Conservatism. Core commitments: social order, inherited institutions, established religion, hierarchy understood as natural and reciprocal, and change that is gradual and organic rather than designed. Edmund Burke's reaction to the French Revolution is the founding statement. Its answer on poverty is that it is the consequence of dissolving the bonds that used to protect people: the market treats a person as a unit of labor, where a landlord, a parish and a church had obligations toward him. The remedy is paternal responsibility, restored obligation and, notably, sometimes regulation of the factory, which is why some of the strongest support for limiting working hours came from landed Tories with no love for northern manufacturers.

That last point is worth pausing on, because it inverts the expected alignment and is the kind of detail that lifts an essay. On factory regulation, conservatives and radical workers were frequently on the same side against liberal manufacturers, since the manufacturers regarded a legal limit on hours as an interference with free contract.

Utopian socialism, Marxism, and the Manifesto

Utopian socialism. Owen, Fourier, Saint-Simon and their followers held that competition itself was the cause: an economy organized around individuals competing produces misery even when it produces wealth, so the remedy is to reorganize production cooperatively. Their method was demonstration rather than revolution. Robert Owen ran New Lanark as a profitable mill with shorter hours, no young children employed, decent housing and a school, in order to prove that humane conditions were compatible with profit, and then founded New Harmony in Indiana, which failed. Marx and Engels named them utopian because, in their view, the socialists appealed to reason and goodwill and had no theory of what force would actually bring the change about.

Marxism. Its answer is that poverty is not a defect of industrial capitalism but a product of how it works. Under the materialist conception of history, the way a society organizes production determines its politics, law and ideas, and history moves through stages as each economic order generates the class that overthrows it. Under the theory of class conflict, the bourgeoisie and proletariat have structurally opposed interests, because profit derives from paying workers less than the value their labor creates, so no goodwill resolves it. The Communist Manifesto of 1848 states this compressed: that the history of all hitherto existing society is the history of class struggles; that capitalism revolutionizes production constantly and thereby produces and concentrates the class that will end it; that the state is an instrument of the ruling class; that private property in the means of production should be abolished; and that workers of all countries should unite, because their common interest as a class crosses the national boundaries of Topic 5.2.

And the fifth answer, which the ideologies mostly missed. Political economy in the Malthusian line held that poverty was caused by population growing faster than food supply, so relief merely encouraged more of it, and the Poor Law of section 03 is built on that view. It belongs in this list because it was enormously influential in policy even though nobody organized a movement around it.

Common mistake to avoid

Do not compare these by listing their features. Compare them by running one question through all of them, which is what the success criterion asks. On the cause of industrial poverty: liberalism says a stage of growth or individual failure; conservatism says the destruction of customary obligation; utopian socialism says competition itself; Marxism says the extraction of surplus from labor, which is the system operating correctly. On the remedy: growth and free trade; restored paternal duty and regulation; cooperative communities; and revolution abolishing private property in the means of production. Two of those, written out in that structure, score better than five definitions.

What Governments Actually Did

1802 to 1889  ·  Factory Acts to social insurance

Governments did not adopt any of the five answers. They legislated piecemeal, under pressure, for reasons that were usually about order, military fitness or party advantage, and the resulting body of law is the origin of the modern state’s responsibility for its citizens’ welfare.

Britain: regulate the factory, harden the workhouse

The Factory Acts came in a sequence, and the sequence is the argument. Early measures from 1802 applied to pauper apprentices and were unenforceable. The Act of 1833 is the turning point, and not because of its limits, which restricted the hours of children by age band in textile mills: it created a small corps of factory inspectors with the power to enter premises. A law with an inspector is a different kind of law from a law without one, and this is the birth of the regulatory state. The Act of 1847, the Ten Hours Act, limited women and young persons to ten hours, which in practice limited the working day for everyone, since a mill cannot run its machinery without them. Later acts extended the principle beyond textiles.

Two things about who supported this. It passed with support from Tory paternalists such as Lord Ashley and from working-class agitation, against opposition from liberal manufacturers who argued that hours were a matter of free contract and that profit was made in the last hour of the day. And it was regulation by category: legislators willing to protect children and women as dependents were unwilling to say that an adult man could not sell his own hours, which is why the ten-hour day arrived sideways.

The Poor Law Amendment Act of 1834 ran in the opposite direction and shows the same period doing both. The old parish relief system was replaced with a national workhouse regime built on the principle of less eligibility: conditions inside must be worse than those of the poorest independent laborer outside, so that only the genuinely desperate would enter. Families were separated inside, work was deliberately grim, and the intent was to make relief so unattractive that able-bodied men would take any wage rather than claim it. It is the Malthusian answer from section 02 written into statute, and it was hated with an intensity that shaped a generation of working-class politics.

Germany: insurance without democracy

Bismarck's social insurance legislation is the other case the success criteria name, and it is genuinely the origin of the welfare state: accident and workplace injury insurance in the early 1870s and consolidated in 1884, health insurance in 1883, and old-age and invalidity pensions in 1889, financed by contributions from workers and employers and administered through the state.

The motive is the part worth knowing, because it is not benevolence and not socialism. Bismarck had outlawed the Social Democratic Party's organization under the Anti-Socialist Laws of 1878, and the insurance program was the other half of the same policy: suppress the movement and remove its grievance, so that workers would look to the imperial state rather than to a party for security. He said as much in substance, arguing that a man with a pension to look forward to is more governable.

So the lesson to carry is that welfare provision here was a conservative instrument of political stabilization, introduced by an authoritarian regime, ahead of Britain and long ahead of the United States. If your model of the nineteenth century is that reform tracks democracy, Germany breaks it, and that is exactly why it is worth citing.

How we know: the state investigated itself in public

Much of what is known about industrial conditions comes from the British parliamentary papers, the blue books: the Sadler Committee of 1832 on child labor, the Ashley Mines Commission of 1842, Chadwick’s sanitary report of the same year, and decades of factory inspectors’ reports. They contain sworn testimony from workers and children, in their own words, with names and places. Two things follow. First, they are evidence produced in order to legislate, so witnesses were sometimes selected for effect and employers complained of exaggeration, which is a real caution. Second, and more striking, these are the documents Engels and Marx used, so the most influential attack on industrial capitalism was built substantially out of the British state’s own published inquiries into itself.

Widening the Franchise, and the Argument It Handed to Women

1832 to 1918  ·  Reform Acts and suffrage

Britain extended the vote three times in fifty years, each time under pressure and each time to a group whose exclusion had become politically expensive, and every extension made the exclusion of women harder to defend without saying openly what the principle really was.

Three Acts, and why each one passed

1832. The Great Reform Act redistributed seats from depopulated boroughs to the new industrial cities, Manchester and Birmingham among them, which had been unrepresented while a handful of voters returned members for empty hillsides. It extended the vote to a portion of the middle class on a property qualification. The context was a genuine fear of revolution after 1830, and the effect was to admit the bourgeoisie of section 01 to the political nation while leaving the working class outside, which is precisely why Chartism followed: a mass movement from 1838 demanding universal male suffrage, secret ballot, equal constituencies, payment of members, no property qualification and annual parliaments, with petitions carrying millions of signatures. It was rejected and the movement collapsed, and five of its six demands are now ordinary features of British democracy.

1867. Extended the vote to urban working-class male householders, roughly doubling the electorate. It passed under a Conservative government partly through party calculation, a gamble that newly enfranchised workers might vote Conservative, which is a reminder that franchise reform is rarely granted on principle.

1884. Extended the same qualification to county householders, largely agricultural workers, bringing the male electorate to a substantial majority of adult men. Universal male suffrage in Britain arrives only in 1918, in the same act that first admitted women over thirty.

Women, work and the vote

Start with the work, because the suffrage argument grows out of it. Women had always worked, and industrialization changed the terms rather than introducing the fact. Working-class women were employed in large numbers in textile mills, in mines until legislation barred them underground in 1842, in workshops, in the sweated trades and, in by far the largest numbers, in domestic service. They were paid substantially less than men for comparable work, on the justification that a man earned a family wage and a woman earned a supplement, which functioned as a self-fulfilling argument. They were excluded from skilled apprenticeships and from most craft unions, which is part of why Topic 5.8's labor movement was slow to represent them.

Then the legal position. Under coverture, described in the Topic 5.1 chapter, a married woman's property and earnings belonged to her husband until the Married Women's Property Acts of 1870 and 1882 changed it, so a woman could be employed and legally unable to keep her wage.

The suffrage argument is the Topic 5.1 move applied to a franchise that had just been justified in universal-sounding language. If the vote follows from being a rational adult contributing to the national economy and paying taxes, then a woman who does all three is excluded on grounds nobody can state without abandoning the principle. Once the property qualification fell away and the vote was defended as a right rather than as a privilege attached to property, the only remaining justification was sex, which had to be asserted rather than argued. The movement that followed, constitutional campaigning from the 1860s and militant campaigning from 1903, is Unit 6 territory, and the argument was constructed here.

Topic 5.7

Somebody Else Carries the Risk

Industry needed more money than any partnership could raise, and the money would not come while an investor could lose his house over a company he did not manage. The nineteenth century solved that, brilliantly, with a set of legal and financial instruments. Every one of them works by moving risk somewhere else, and this chapter follows where it went, from a shareholder’s balance sheet to an Egyptian tax collector.

The Sentence That Unlocked the Money

1844 to 1862  ·  The Companies Acts

The single most consequential financial innovation of the century is a legal rule about how much an investor can lose. Get the counterfactual right, what an investor faced before it, and the entire structure of modern industry follows from one change.

What an investor faced before

Under a partnership, and under most company forms before mid-century, liability was unlimited. If the business failed owing money, creditors could pursue the personal assets of every partner or shareholder: house, savings, everything. One investor could be made to pay the whole debt if the others had nothing.

Follow the consequence. If putting a hundred pounds into a railway could cost you ten thousand, then you invest only in businesses you can watch personally, run by people you know, in a trade you understand. Capital stays local, concentrated and cautious, and the total pool available to industry is a small fraction of the nation's savings. Meanwhile the Topic 5.5 chapter's railways, steel works and telegraph networks needed sums no group of mutually acquainted merchants could raise.

Limited liability reverses it: a shareholder's exposure is capped at the amount invested, and the company is a separate legal person that owes its own debts. Now a schoolteacher can put fifty pounds into a railway in a county she has never visited, because the worst case is losing fifty pounds. Multiply that by a whole propertied and salaried population and you have a capital market.

The legislative sequence in Britain is worth knowing precisely, because the success criteria name the endpoint. The Joint Stock Companies Act of 1844 allowed incorporation by simple registration rather than by an individual act of Parliament or a royal charter, which had been the old route and had kept incorporation a privilege. The Limited Liability Act of 1855 introduced the cap. And the Companies Act of 1862 consolidated the law into the modern form, general, cheap and available to anyone, which is why it is the turning point.

Where the risk went, and the arguments against

Nothing here removes risk; it relocates it. If a shareholder cannot be pursued, then a creditor who is not paid, a supplier left with unpaid invoices, a worker owed wages, absorbs the loss instead. That is precisely what contemporaries objected to, and the objection was strong enough to delay the reform for decades: critics argued it would license reckless speculation, encourage fraud and let men trade with other people's money while risking none of their own.

The compensating device was disclosure. Registration, published accounts, a register of directors and, over time, audit requirements are the price of the privilege: the public cannot pursue your assets, so the public gets to see your books. That trade, limited exposure in exchange for transparency, is still the basis of company law.

And a company is also a perpetual person, which matters as much as the liability cap. A partnership dissolves when a partner dies or withdraws; a company continues, holds property, sues and is sued in its own name, and can therefore commit to a forty-year project. The Topic 4.4 chapter's chartered companies had some of this by special grant; what changes in 1862 is that it becomes available to anybody who fills in a form.

Common mistake to avoid

Do not write that joint-stock companies were invented in the nineteenth century. The Topic 4.4 chapter has the VOC in 1602 and the English East India Company in 1600, both with tradable shares. Two things are new here and both belong in the sentence. First, incorporation stops being a privilege granted by charter or by act of Parliament to a favored few and becomes a right available by registration to anyone. Second, liability is capped, which is what makes investing in a distant enterprise you cannot supervise a rational act. Together they convert a rare instrument of state-backed monopoly into the ordinary vehicle of business, which is why company numbers rise steeply after 1862.

Where the Money Was Raised and Who Decided

c. 1820 to 1900  ·  Markets and bankers

Two institutions turned scattered savings into industrial plant. A stock exchange made a long-term investment sellable at short notice, and an investment bank decided which enterprises deserved the money, which made a handful of banking houses among the most powerful actors of the century.

The exchange, and the problem it solves

A stock exchange is an organized market where shares and bonds are bought and sold. Its function is liquidity, and liquidity is the point most students miss. A railway takes five years to build and forty to pay off, but an investor may need his money next year for a daughter's dowry or a bad harvest. Without a resale market he must either commit for forty years or not invest; with one, he can sell his shares to somebody else tomorrow while the railway keeps the capital. The exchange therefore converts long-term illiquid projects into holdings people are willing to make, without the project losing its funding.

It also produces a continuous public price, which is a judgment on an enterprise's prospects that can be read by anyone, and which allows borrowing against securities as collateral. And it produces speculation and panics, since a market that prices expectations will sometimes price them wrongly and all at once: the railway manias of the 1840s, the crash of 1873 and the recurring panics of the century are the same institution working and failing.

The banks, and what a banker actually did

An investment bank does not principally take deposits and lend; it underwrites. It agrees to buy an issue of shares or bonds from a company or a government, and then sells them to investors, taking the difference and carrying the risk of failing to place them. Doing that requires the one asset that cannot be bought quickly, which is the trust of investors, so a house whose name on a prospectus was taken as a guarantee of soundness could place an issue that an unknown promoter could not.

The success criteria name three and each illustrates a different role. The Rothschilds, operating across London, Paris, Frankfurt, Vienna and Naples, were the great sovereign lenders of the century, financing governments and wars and, through the family network, moving information and money across borders faster than states could. Deutsche Bank, founded in 1870, exemplifies the German model of a universal bank that took equity stakes in industrial firms, sat on their boards and coordinated whole sectors, which is a large part of how the state-led industrialization of Topic 5.4 was financed. The House of Morgan in the United States financed and then reorganized railroads and industries, imposing consolidation on chaotic markets, and in the panic of 1907 J. P. Morgan personally organized the rescue of the American banking system, because there was no central bank to do it, which is a substantial part of why the Federal Reserve was created in 1913.

Note what that concentration means. A small number of houses decided which railways, mines and steel works were built and which governments could borrow, and they did so on commercial judgment answerable to nobody. That is the fact behind the political anxiety about financial power in the later century, and it is also the mechanism section 04 depends on.

Free Trade Was a Policy Before It Was a Principle

1817 to 1900  ·  Ricardo, the Corn Laws, gold

Britain adopted free trade at the exact moment it had the world’s cheapest manufactures, and other countries adopted tariffs at the exact moment they did not. That is not hypocrisy on anyone’s part; it is what the theory predicts, and saying so is the difference between describing the doctrine and understanding it.

Comparative advantage, and what it does and does not say

David Ricardo set out comparative advantage in 1817, and it is one of the genuinely counterintuitive results in economics, so it is worth stating carefully. Even if one country produces everything more efficiently than another, both gain by specializing in what they produce relatively best and trading. His example was English cloth and Portuguese wine. The insight is that what matters is opportunity cost, what you give up to make a thing, rather than absolute efficiency.

Now note what it does not say. It says nothing about which specialization you end up with, and it treats that as given rather than as something policy creates. If a country's comparative advantage is in raw cotton because another country's policy destroyed its weaving, as the Topic 5.4 chapter describes for India, then specializing according to comparative advantage locks in that position. Industrial goods and primary products also behave differently over time, since manufacturing generates skills, machinery and further industries while a mine generates a mine. That is the critique the Topic 5.4 chapter's protectionists made and it is the standard argument for infant-industry tariffs.

The Corn Laws, and the gold standard

The Corn Laws were tariffs on imported grain, kept high after 1815 to protect landowners' rents by keeping domestic grain prices up. The fight over them was a class fight in plain view. Manufacturers wanted repeal, because cheap bread means workers can live on lower wages and because countries that sold Britain grain would buy British cloth. Landowners wanted them kept. The Anti-Corn Law League, led by Cobden and Bright, ran what was effectively the first modern political campaign, with mass meetings, a periodical press and organized voter registration.

Repeal came in 1846, pushed through by Peel against most of his own party, with the Irish famine as the immediate occasion, and it split the Conservative Party. It marks the moment industrial interests decisively outweighed landed ones in British policy, and it committed Britain to free trade for the rest of the century.

The gold standard is the other half of the international system. Under it a currency is defined as a fixed weight of gold and is convertible into it, so exchange rates between countries on the standard are effectively fixed. Britain was on gold from the early nineteenth century and most major economies joined by the 1870s. Its advantage is certainty: a merchant or an investor lending abroad for thirty years knows what he will be repaid in, which is exactly what makes large-scale international investment possible, and it is therefore a precondition for section 04.

Its cost is that a government gives up control of its own money. If gold flows out, the money supply must contract, which means falling prices and wages and rising unemployment, and the government cannot expand credit to relieve it without breaking convertibility. So the adjustment falls on wage earners rather than on the exchange rate. That is the risk transfer this chapter is about, applied to a whole economy, and it is why farmers and workers in debt campaigned against gold, most famously in the United States in the 1890s.

How we know: countries change doctrine when their position changes

The clearest evidence that trade doctrine follows competitive position is that states switch sides when their position switches. Britain was intensely protectionist under the mercantilism of Topic 4.5 while it was catching up, and became the champion of free trade once it was ahead. The United States kept high protective tariffs through the whole period of its industrialization and became the leading advocate of open markets in the twentieth century, once it led. Germany moved to protection in 1879 while building its heavy industry. The German economist Friedrich List made exactly this argument at the time, that a country which has reached the top kicks away the ladder it climbed. When a doctrine correlates that neatly with interest, treat the interest as the better explanation of the policy.

04

Control Without Conquest

1838 to 1900  ·  Debt, treaties and commissions

A state does not have to be annexed to be governed. If a foreign body collects its taxes, foreign treaties set its tariffs and foreign courts try foreigners inside it, it has lost the powers that make sovereignty operative, and the instrument was a loan rather than an army.

How a loan becomes a government

The sequence is the same in every case and is worth learning as a sequence. A government borrows on European capital markets, usually for railways, ports, public works or a war. Because it is judged a risky borrower, the terms are hard: high interest, and bonds issued at a discount, so a state that owes a hundred may have received seventy. Servicing the debt requires foreign currency, which requires exports, which pushes the economy further toward primary products. When revenue falls short, it borrows again to service the first loan. At default, bondholders press their own governments, and the settlement is not forgiveness but control of the revenue: a foreign commission takes designated taxes at source to pay the creditors first.

Egypt is the case that runs the whole sequence. The Topic 5.4 chapter leaves it stripped of its industry by the Treaty of Balta Liman. It then borrowed enormously under Ismail for the Suez Canal, railways and public works, defaulted in the 1870s, sold its Suez shares to the British government, accepted an Anglo-French Dual Control over its finances, and, when a nationalist movement objected to foreign management of the country's money, was invaded and occupied by Britain in 1882. Debt to occupation in a generation.

The Ottoman empire supplies the purest institutional form. After default in 1875, the Ottoman Public Debt Administration was established in 1881: a body controlled by European bondholders' representatives, with its own staff of thousands, which collected specified Ottoman revenues, the salt and tobacco monopolies, stamp duties, silk tithes and others, directly, and applied them to the debt before the Ottoman treasury saw them. A sovereign empire with its own army, sultan and law had a foreign agency inside it collecting its taxes, and it lasted until the empire did.

Latin America is the diffuse version. British and later American capital financed railways, ports, utilities and government borrowing; the railways were laid to move exports to the coast rather than to integrate an internal market; debt service required export earnings; and when governments defaulted, the pressure ranged from gunboats to the American practice, in the Caribbean and Central America in the early twentieth century, of taking over customs houses directly. No annexation, and comprehensive influence over what these economies produced.

China is the treaty-port version. After the Opium Wars, a series of treaties from 1842 opened ports to foreign residence and trade, capped Chinese tariffs at a low fixed rate, granted extraterritoriality so foreigners were tried under their own consuls rather than Chinese law, and added indemnities that had to be borrowed to pay. From 1854 the Chinese Maritime Customs Service, which collected the tariff, was run by foreign inspectors-general. China was never colonized and was not, in the relevant respects, in control of its own commerce.

What is being claimed, precisely

Be exact, because this argument is easy to overstate. These states retained their flags, their rulers, their armies and their internal administration, and in Egypt's case that ended anyway. What they lost was a specific and identifiable set of powers: the tariff, which Topic 5.4 shows is the instrument of industrialization; jurisdiction over foreigners on their own soil; and the disposal of their own tax revenue. Those three are what a state uses to direct its economy, so losing them means the economy is directed elsewhere.

And name the local decisions too, or the account becomes one in which things are done to passive states. Ismail chose to borrow at ruinous rates for prestige projects. Ottoman and Latin American governments borrowed to fund wars, courts and patronage as well as infrastructure. Local elites frequently profited from the arrangement and defended it. Structure and decision are both in the record, and a good answer has both.

Common mistake to avoid

Do not treat economic imperialism as a lesser or partial version of colonialism, and do not treat it as merely ordinary lending. It is a distinct arrangement with its own logic: it is cheaper than conquest, since bondholders pay nothing for an administration, it carries no responsibility for governing, and it is largely invisible, because there is no flag change to point at. That is precisely why it persisted after formal empires ended, and why the Unit 6 chapters on spheres of influence should be read against this one. The test to apply is not whether a country was annexed but whether it controlled its tariff, its courts and its revenue.

Mechanism

Limited liability is a rule about the worst case, and that is why it mattered

Before it, an investor in a failed business could be pursued for its whole debt, house and savings included, so people only funded ventures they could personally supervise and capital stayed local and cautious. Capping exposure at the sum invested lets a saver put fifty pounds into a railway in a county she has never seen, because fifty pounds is the most she can lose, and multiplying that across a whole propertied population creates a capital market able to fund railways, steel and telegraphs. Britain got there in stages: registration without a charter in 1844, the liability cap in 1855, and the Companies Act of 1862 making cheap general incorporation available to anyone. The risk did not vanish; it moved to unpaid creditors, suppliers and workers, which is what disclosure requirements were extracted to offset.

Causation

Britain adopted free trade when it had the cheapest goods

Ricardo showed in 1817 that both parties gain by specializing where their opportunity cost is lowest, even when one produces everything more efficiently. But the theory takes the specialization as given, and open markets favor whoever is already cheapest, so the doctrine was rational for Britain and ruinous for a late industrializer. The Corn Laws fell in 1846 because manufacturers wanted cheap bread and export markets while landowners wanted rents, and Peel split his party to repeal them. The proof that position drives doctrine is that states switch sides: Britain was protectionist under mercantilism while catching up, the United States kept high tariffs throughout its industrialization, and Germany protected iron and steel from 1879.

Trade-off

The gold standard bought certainty and charged wage earners for it

Defining a currency as a fixed weight of gold makes exchange rates between participating countries effectively fixed, which is what lets an investor lend abroad for thirty years knowing what he will be repaid in, and that certainty is the precondition for the enormous international investment of the period. The cost is that a government gives up control of its money supply: if gold flows out, credit must contract, so prices and wages fall and unemployment rises, and the state cannot relieve it without breaking convertibility. The adjustment therefore falls on wage earners and debtors rather than on the exchange rate, which is why indebted farmers and workers campaigned against gold, most visibly in the United States in the 1890s.

Structure

Ask who controls the tariff, the courts and the revenue

Agreeing to Ask for More Money Was a Crime

1799 to 1875  ·  Combination and conspiracy

Start every answer about nineteenth-century labor here. An individual worker was free to negotiate and had no power; workers acting together had power and were breaking the law. That asymmetry is not incidental to the story, it is the story.

Britain: the Combination Acts

The Combination Acts of 1799 and 1800 made it a criminal offense for workers to combine to raise wages or shorten hours, with summary conviction before magistrates who were frequently employers themselves. They were passed during the war with revolutionary France, when any organization of working men was read as potential sedition, and they sat alongside older statutes and the common law.

The logic offered was consistency with freedom of contract: a wage is a contract between an employer and an individual worker, and a combination interferes with that individual's freedom to make his own bargain. In practice the effect was to leave one party to that bargain able to wait and the other unable to eat, which is why the doctrine was defended most vigorously by people who never had to sell a day of labor.

Repeal came in 1824 and, after a wave of strikes, was narrowed in 1825 so that unions could exist but almost any effective action, picketing, persuasion, obstruction, remained prosecutable as intimidation or as restraint of trade. Legal existence without lawful action is the position British unions occupied for fifty years. The Tolpuddle Martyrs, agricultural laborers transported to Australia in 1834 for administering an unlawful oath rather than for the union itself, is the case that shows how prosecutors worked around the repeal. Full protection came only with the legislation of 1871 and 1875, which legalized peaceful picketing and removed criminal conspiracy from trade disputes.

The United States: conspiracy in the courts

The American version was judge-made rather than statutory. Under the doctrine of criminal conspiracy, courts held that workers combining to raise wages were conspiring to injure trade, a line running from the Philadelphia cordwainers' cases early in the century. Commonwealth v. Hunt in Massachusetts in 1842 is the usual turning point, holding that a union was not unlawful in itself and that legality depended on its purposes and means, which opened a door and left judges holding the handle.

And the instrument that mattered most came later: the labor injunction. A federal judge could order a strike to stop, on the grounds that it obstructed interstate commerce or the mails or damaged property, and a worker who continued was then in contempt of court, punishable immediately without a jury. That is how the Pullman strike in section 02 was broken. The Sherman Antitrust Act of 1890, written against corporate combinations, was applied to labor unions as combinations in restraint of trade, which is one of the sharper ironies in the unit.

Now the consequence to carry into everything else. If organizing is criminal and there is no legal machinery for arbitration, the tactics available to workers are the ones that do not require legal standing: machine-breaking, as the Topic 5.3 chapter describes among the Luddites; friendly societies disguised as social clubs; petitions and mass movements such as Chartism; sudden unplanned strikes; and, where those failed, violence. Historians describing early labor as disorganized or riotous are frequently describing what the law had left available.

Common mistake to avoid

Do not write that unions were illegal because the state was hostile to workers as such, which is too blunt to be useful and cannot explain the Factory Acts of Topic 5.6 passing in the same decades. The doctrine that did the work was freedom of contract, and it was applied with genuine consistency: it also struck down laws protecting workers, on the ground that a maximum-hours statute interfered with an adult’s right to sell his own labor, which is exactly the reasoning of the American case Lochner v. New York in 1905. A neutral-sounding principle applied evenly between parties of very unequal power produces systematically unequal results, and naming that mechanism is worth more than an accusation of bias.

02

Three Strikes and Who Broke Them

1877 to 1894  ·  Railroad, Haymarket, Pullman

The three events the success criteria name form a sequence, and the sequence is about escalating state involvement: local violence, then a political prosecution, then federal troops and a court order. Each one also taught the labor movement a lesson about what was survivable.

The Great Railroad Strike, 1877

In the depression following the panic of 1873, the Baltimore and Ohio cut wages for the third time, and workers at Martinsburg stopped the trains. It spread along the rail network with no central organization at all, to Baltimore, Pittsburgh, Chicago, St Louis and beyond, drawing in other workers and the unemployed, and in Pittsburgh a confrontation with militia left dozens dead and railroad property burned on a large scale.

State militias proved unreliable, since local men would not always fire on neighbors, so federal troops were used to reopen the lines, in some places diverted from Reconstruction duties in the South. Roughly a hundred people died nationally.

The significance is that this was the first strike in the United States to become a national event and to be suppressed as one. Employers and governments concluded they needed better instruments, and built them: state armories in cities, expanded militias, and closer coordination between companies and courts. Labor concluded that spontaneous mass action produced martyrs, which pushes the movement in the direction section 03 describes.

Haymarket, 1886, and Pullman, 1894

Haymarket. The eight-hour day was the great demand of the mid-1880s, and a general strike for it began on 1 May 1886. At a rally in Chicago's Haymarket Square on 4 May, called after police killed strikers at the McCormick works the previous day, someone threw a bomb at police; the officer killed by it and others who died in the ensuing shooting, along with civilian dead, produced a wave of public fear. Eight anarchists were tried, the identity of the bomb-thrower never established and the prosecution built substantially on their published views rather than on evidence of the act; four were hanged, one died in custody, and the survivors were later pardoned by Governor Altgeld, who stated publicly that the trial had been unfair.

The consequence, and it is the one the criteria point at: Haymarket allowed employers and the press to identify labor organizing with anarchism, bombs and foreigners, since most of the accused were German-born immigrants. The eight-hour movement collapsed, the Knights of Labor, which had grown to enormous size and had no connection to the bombing, was fatally damaged by association, and a lasting association between unionism and subversion entered American public argument. May Day is commemorated internationally because of Haymarket, which is a fact many Americans do not know about their own history.

Pullman. George Pullman built a company town outside Chicago where his workers rented his houses and bought from his shops. In the depression of 1893 he cut wages roughly a quarter and did not cut rents, which is the detail that made the grievance unanswerable. The workers struck, and the American Railway Union under Eugene Debs backed them with a boycott: its members would not handle trains with Pullman cars, which stopped rail traffic across much of the country.

The response is the section's point. The railroads attached mail cars to Pullman trains, so that stopping a train obstructed the United States mail. The federal government obtained an injunction under that theory and under the Sherman Act, President Cleveland sent federal troops over the objection of the governor of Illinois, the strike was broken, about thirty people were killed, and Debs was imprisoned for contempt. Debs went into prison a trade unionist and came out a socialist, and ran for president five times.

How we know: the coverage is the evidence, twice over

Newspaper archives from these events are abundant and violently partisan, which makes them useful in two different ways. Read as reports, they must be treated with caution, since major papers described strikers as a mob, an insurrection or a communist conspiracy, and Haymarket coverage convicted the defendants weeks before the trial. Read as evidence of opinion, they are excellent, because they show precisely how the public argument was framed and by whom. Set them against the labor press, union minute books, court transcripts and the pardon message Governor Altgeld issued in 1893, which reviews the trial record in detail, and you can reconstruct both what happened and what people were told had happened. Those are two separate historical questions and this topic needs both.

Why Britain Got a Labour Party

c. 1880 to 1906  ·  Two labor movements

Two industrial societies with similar problems produced different labor movements: one that formed its own political party and one that decided politics was a trap. The reasons are structural, and giving four of them is how you answer the comparative question the success criteria pose.

What the AFL chose, and why

The American Federation of Labor, founded in 1886 under Samuel Gompers, adopted what it called pure and simple unionism: organize skilled workers by craft, pursue wages, hours and conditions through collective bargaining with employers, build strike funds and benefit schemes, and stay out of party politics beyond rewarding friends and punishing enemies at the ballot box.

The reasoning was drawn from the wreckage in section 02. Broad, politically ambitious organizations such as the Knights of Labor had been destroyed; radicalism had been used to justify hanging men at Haymarket; and the courts had shown they would break any strike that looked like a challenge to the system. Organizing the skilled, who were hard to replace, around demands an employer could actually concede was a strategy for surviving in that environment, and it worked in its own terms. It also excluded most workers: the unskilled, immigrants, Black workers and women were largely outside the craft unions, which is a cost worth naming.

Four structural reasons for the difference

The sequence of the vote and industrialization. British working-class men were largely excluded from the franchise until 1867 and 1884, described in the Topic 5.6 chapter, so they spent decades organizing politically to obtain it, through Chartism and after, and arrived with a tradition of independent political action and a class identity built by exclusion. Most American white men had the vote well before industrialization, so they entered the industrial era already inside two existing parties, and the natural move was to work within them rather than to build a third.

Ethnic and racial division. The American workforce was continuously renewed by immigration from many countries, speaking different languages, in a society with legally enforced racial hierarchy. Employers used ethnic divisions deliberately in hiring and in strikebreaking, and many unions excluded Black workers and, in the West, campaigned for Chinese exclusion. A class identity is harder to build across those divisions, and Britain’s workforce, whatever its regional differences and its substantial Irish immigration, was not divided in the same way or to the same degree.

State violence and the courts. American strikes were broken by federal troops, state militias and private armies such as the Pinkertons, and by injunctions, more routinely and more lethally than British strikes were. That raises the cost of confrontation and rewards the AFL's narrower strategy, and it also meant American radicals were more likely to be imprisoned or deported than elected.

Mobility and the shape of the electoral system. Land in the West, high internal mobility and comparatively higher wages weakened the sense of a permanent proletarian condition, and first-past-the-post voting in single-member districts punishes a new party severely, since a third party can win a substantial share of the vote and almost no seats. Britain had the same electoral system and a working class concentrated in industrial constituencies where a party could actually win them.

The British outcome followed: the Trades Union Congress and socialist societies formed a Labour Representation Committee in 1900, which became the Labour Party in 1906, and the Taff Vale judgment of 1901, which made unions liable for damages caused by strikes, convinced many unions that they needed their own members in Parliament rather than sympathetic ones. In the United States, socialism reached a real but minority following under Debs and no labor party was established.

04

Cotton, Sewers, Schools and Drink

1831 to 1874  ·  Abolition and reform

The other great movements of the century look unrelated to labor and share a cause with it. Each is a response to something industrialization created: a slave economy it made profitable, a city it filled faster than the city could cope, a workforce it needed literate, and a misery it produced and then blamed on drink.

Cotton, and the abolition argument

The structural connection is the sharpest single fact in this unit and it should be written as a chain. Lancashire's mills, described in the Topic 5.3 chapter, needed raw cotton in enormous and growing quantities. The American South, after the cotton gin made short-staple cotton profitable to process, supplied a very large share of it, grown by enslaved people whose numbers rose across the period. So the most advanced industrial economy in the world ran on a raw material produced by slavery, and the enslaved population of the United States grew because industrial demand made the crop worth expanding. Industrial capitalism and Atlantic slavery were not sequential stages; they were contemporaneous and connected.

Abolitionists used exactly that. Consumer boycotts of slave-grown sugar and cotton put the connection on a household shopping list, and free-produce movements sold goods certified as slave-free. The argument was that a British worker's cloth and a British family's sweetener implicated them in a system they claimed to abhor, which turns a distant moral question into a domestic one, and it is the ancestor of every ethical consumption campaign since.

British abolition came in stages: the slave trade in 1807, then slavery in the empire by the act of 1833, effective from 1834 with an apprenticeship system that kept the formerly enslaved working for their former owners and was ended early, in 1838, under pressure. Two things are usually left out. First, the act compensated the enslavers, twenty million pounds, an enormous fraction of national expenditure, financed by a loan that was not fully repaid until the twenty-first century, and it compensated the enslaved with nothing. Second, and decisively, enslaved people forced the timing. The Baptist War in Jamaica over Christmas 1831 and into 1832, led by the deacon Samuel Sharpe and involving tens of thousands, began as a general strike for wages and became the largest rising in the British Caribbean; it was suppressed with hundreds killed in the fighting and hundreds executed afterward, including Sharpe. The scale of the reprisals, reported in Britain, persuaded Parliament that maintaining slavery would mean permanent war. Abolition was argued for in London and made unavoidable in Jamaica.

The Cotton Famine of 1861 to 1865 shows the dependency from the other end. The Union blockade cut off Southern cotton, Lancashire mills closed, and hundreds of thousands of workers were thrown into destitution. British industry responded by developing supply in India, Egypt and Brazil, permanently restructuring the global cotton trade, and Egyptian cotton is the crop that underwrote the borrowing in the Topic 5.7 chapter. And a considerable number of Lancashire workers, though not all, supported the Union and the blockade that was starving them, on antislavery grounds, which is one of the more striking pieces of evidence about how far the abolitionist argument had reached.

Sewers, schools and temperance

Public health. The crisis itself is the Topic 5.9 chapter's; the movement is here. Edwin Chadwick's Report on the Sanitary Condition of the Labouring Population of 1842 assembled statistical evidence that mortality tracked living conditions with a precision nobody could dismiss, and argued that disease cost the state money in lost labor and poor relief, which is the argument that moved Parliament. Chadwick was wrong about the mechanism, holding to miasma theory, that disease came from foul air, and the practical prescriptions, drainage, water supply and refuse removal, were right anyway. John Snow's investigation of the Broad Street pump in 1854 and later germ theory supplied the correct explanation. The Great Stink of 1858, when the Thames became unbearable to a Parliament sitting beside it, produced the money for Joseph Bazalgette's London sewer system, an engineering work on a scale that only industrial technology made possible. Industrialization caused the crisis and supplied the cure.

Education. Britain's Elementary Education Act of 1870, the Forster Act, created school boards to fill the gaps left by church schools, and compulsory attendance and the removal of fees followed within two decades. The motives are worth naming honestly and they were mixed: an economy needing workers who could read instructions and do arithmetic; a state that had just enfranchised urban working men in 1867 and, in a phrase of the period, had to educate its masters; competition with better-schooled Germany; and a genuine reforming belief in opportunity. It also completed the removal of children from the workforce that the Factory Acts had begun, since a child in school is a child not in a mill.

Temperance. The Woman's Christian Temperance Union, founded in 1874 under Frances Willard, is the case to know, and it is more interesting than its subject. Heavy drinking was a real feature of industrial life, and its consequences fell hardest on women who had no legal claim on a husband's wages and few independent earnings. The WCTU became the largest women's organization in the United States, and under the slogan of home protection it moved from alcohol into suffrage, labor conditions, prison reform and public health, so a campaign that appeared to accept women's domestic role became a training ground for a generation of women organizers and a route into politics that the Topic 5.6 chapter's franchise arguments had left closed.

Common mistake to avoid

Do not write these as four separate good causes that happened to coincide, and do not write reform as something granted from above once people saw the problem. Each has an industrial cause: mills created the demand that expanded American slavery, unplanned cities created the sanitary crisis, industry and a widened franchise created the demand for mass schooling, and industrial conditions created the drinking that temperance attacked. And in each case pressure from below set the timing: the Baptist War forced the pace of abolition, working-class agitation drove the Factory Acts, and a Parliament that had ignored cholera for decades funded sewers when the smell reached its own windows.

Building an Answer That Scores

Each card is a full argument: the claim, the specific evidence, and the reason. The first explains most of the rest of the chapter, so start there.

Structure

A symmetrical rule between unequal parties is not neutral

The Combination Acts of 1799 and 1800 criminalized workers agreeing to seek higher wages, and American courts reached the same result through criminal conspiracy doctrine, both justified by freedom of contract: a wage is an individual bargain and a combination interferes with it. Each party could equally walk away, and an employer could wait weeks while a worker could not miss a week’s food, so the symmetrical rule handed the outcome to whoever could hold out. The same doctrine also struck down protective legislation, as in Lochner in 1905. With combination criminal and no arbitration available, the tactics left were machine-breaking, disguised friendly societies, mass petitioning and sudden strikes, which is why early labor looks disorganized.

Causation

The state supplied the force and called the dispute private

In 1877 wage cuts on the Baltimore and Ohio produced an unorganized strike that spread along the rail network, and federal troops reopened the lines after local militias proved unwilling to fire on neighbors, with about a hundred dead. In 1886 a bomb at Chicago’s Haymarket rally, thrower never identified, produced a trial built on the defendants’ published views; four were hanged and the survivors later pardoned by a governor who called the trial unfair, and the eight-hour movement and the Knights of Labor collapsed under the association with anarchism. In 1894 railroads attached mail cars to Pullman trains so that the boycott obstructed the mails, a federal injunction followed, troops broke the strike, and Debs went to prison for contempt and came out a socialist.

Comparison

The vote before industry, or after it, decides whether you build a party

British working-class men were excluded from the franchise until 1867 and 1884, so they spent decades organizing politically to win it, through Chartism and after, arriving with an independent political tradition that produced the Labour Representation Committee in 1900 and the Labour Party in 1906, with the Taff Vale judgment of 1901 convincing unions they needed their own MPs. Most American white men held the vote before industrialization and entered the industrial era already inside two parties. Add continuous immigration and legal racial hierarchy, which employers used in hiring and strikebreaking; routine suppression by troops, militias, Pinkertons and injunctions; and first-past-the-post districts that punish a third party. The AFL’s pure and simple unionism was a rational response to that environment.

Connection

The mill and the plantation were one system, and the enslaved forced the timing

Lancashire needed raw cotton in growing quantities and the American South supplied it, grown by an enslaved population that expanded because industrial demand made the crop worth expanding, so industrial capitalism and Atlantic slavery were contemporaneous and connected rather than sequential. Britain abolished the trade in 1807 and slavery by the act of 1833, compensating enslavers twenty million pounds and the enslaved nothing, with apprenticeship ended early in 1838. And the Baptist War in Jamaica over Christmas 1831, led by Samuel Sharpe and suppressed with hundreds killed and hundreds executed, is what persuaded Parliament that keeping slavery meant permanent war.

What Actually Separated Them

c. 1800 to 1900  ·  Middle and working

The Topic 5.6 chapter defines the two classes by their relationship to capital, which is correct and abstract. Lived from the inside, the line was drawn by something simpler: whether your income was predictable, and whether you could stop working without your household collapsing within a fortnight.

The middle class, and why it was new

The middle class ran from factory owners, merchants and bankers at the top through engineers, doctors, lawyers, managers, shopkeepers and, at the bottom edge, clerks and schoolteachers. What united a range that wide was the source of the income: business, qualification and salary rather than an inherited title or rents from land.

That is genuinely new and it is the point the success criteria want. In the societies of every earlier unit, the people at the top held their position by birth and by land, and status was largely fixed at birth. A cotton manufacturer richer than a duke, or a physician with no land at all and a professional income, did not fit that scheme, and the nineteenth century is partly the story of this class acquiring the political weight to match its money, which is what the Reform Act of 1832 in Topic 5.6 concedes.

Its markers were visible and deliberate: a house away from the works and eventually in a suburb reachable by railway, at least one domestic servant as the minimum sign of respectability, a parlor for receiving visitors, schooling for the children, church attendance, and a wife who did not earn. That last one is section 02, and it is the sharpest marker of all.

And a warning about the phrase. Middle class in nineteenth-century Britain does not mean the middle of the income distribution, which would be a well-paid manual worker. It means a position defined by the source of income and by a set of respectability markers, and a clerk earning less than a skilled engineer would count himself in it and the engineer out of it, which tells you the category is social as much as economic.

The working class, and what insecurity means

The working class sold labor for wages, in mills, mines, workshops, docks, building sites and, for the largest single group of women, other people's houses. The Topic 5.3 chapter has the hours and the danger; what belongs here is what the wage did and did not buy.

Insecurity is the defining feature, more than the level of the wage. Work was seasonal, trade was cyclical, and a downturn such as those of the 1840s put whole towns out of work at once. An injury ended earnings immediately, and the machine that caused it carried no liability. There was no sick pay and no pension, and after 1834 public relief was meant to run through the workhouse of Topic 5.6, deliberately made worse than the worst job. In practice most paupers went on being relieved in their own homes, because refusing outdoor relief to everyone would have cost more than the parishes could raise in a bad year, so what the New Poor Law reliably delivered was not the workhouse itself but the threat of it. So a household that was comfortable in a good year was destitute within weeks of a bad one, and this is why so much working-class organizing, described in Topic 5.8, went into friendly societies, burial clubs and mutual insurance rather than into politics.

Household economy followed from that. A working-class family needed multiple earners, which is why women and children worked, and the household budget went overwhelmingly on food, above all bread, with rent next, leaving nothing to absorb a shock. Diet was monotonous and often adulterated, since bread was bulked with alum and other additives before food regulation. Everyone in a family shared one or two rooms, and possessions could go to the pawnshop on Monday and be redeemed on payday.

And the class was internally graded in ways contemporaries took very seriously. A skilled engineer, printer or mule spinner in steady work, with a union and a friendly society, lived a life closer to a clerk's than to a casual dock laborer's, and that stratum, sometimes called the labor aristocracy, is where the unions of Topic 5.8 were built.

An Ideology You Had to Be Able to Afford

c. 1820 to 1900  ·  Gender by class

The nineteenth century held, loudly, that a woman’s place was the home. The same century employed enormous numbers of women in mills, mines and other people’s kitchens. Both are true because the doctrine described the lives of households that could afford it and was used to judge the households that could not.

What the doctrine said, and what it required

Separate spheres held that men and women were suited by nature to different domains: the man to the public world of work, commerce and politics, the woman to the private world of household, children and moral guardianship. The home was described as a refuge from a competitive commercial world, and the woman was its keeper, which gave her genuine authority over household management, spending, the servants and the children's early education while removing her from anything outside the door.

Now the economics, which is what the success criteria are asking for when they demand the why. This arrangement requires a household that can live on one income. That is the whole explanation. A family that can survive on a man's earnings can withdraw a woman's from the market, and doing so becomes the visible proof that it can, which converts a non-earning wife into the single most legible marker of middle-class status. It is expensive, and being expensive is the point.

A working-class household could not afford it. It needed every earner, so women worked in textile mills, in workshops and the sweated trades, in agriculture at harvest, underground in mines until the ban of 1842, and above all in domestic service, which was the largest single occupation for women in Britain across the century. Married women often withdrew from full-time factory work when children arrived and took in laundry, lodgers, sewing or childminding instead, which is invisible in the census and was real income.

So the same ideology produced opposite lives, and produced a judgment on top of that. Working-class women were criticized for neglecting a domestic role their household economy made impossible, and the criticism came from reformers, clergy and inspectors who often sincerely wanted to help. Naming that double bind is a strong move in an essay: the doctrine did not merely fail to describe working-class life, it supplied the vocabulary in which working-class life was found wanting.

What it cost, and what it built

The costs to middle-class women were concrete. Girls' education emphasized accomplishments, music, drawing, French, over the classics and mathematics that qualified a boy for university and a profession. Professions were closed: medicine, law, the church, the universities, and admission came only late in the century and against organized resistance. Under coverture, described in Topic 5.1, a married woman's property and earnings were her husband's until the Acts of 1870 and 1882. Economic dependence was total, which made a bad marriage inescapable, since divorce was practically unavailable and custody went to fathers.

And what it built, which the flat version of this story leaves out. Domestic authority was real authority, over a household budget and a staff. Philanthropy, charity visiting, Sunday schools and moral reform were defined as extensions of the domestic sphere and were therefore permitted, and they took middle-class women into slums, workhouses and prisons, gave them organizational experience, and produced the campaigning networks that the Topic 5.8 chapter's temperance and abolition movements ran on. The WCTU's slogan of home protection is exactly this move: accept the sphere and argue that protecting the home requires acting outside it. That is how a doctrine of confinement generated a generation of organizers.

Children divide the same way and for the same reason. Middle-class childhood became a protected stage with schooling, toys and sentiment attached to it, which is affordable when a child does not need to earn. Working-class children worked, in mills and mines as Topic 5.3 describes, and then in street trades, workshops and domestic help, and their earnings were a necessary part of the household budget rather than pocket money. The compulsory schooling of Topic 5.8 changed that by making it illegal, which cost poor families income and was resented for that reason before it was welcomed.

Common mistake to avoid

Do not write that industrialization pushed women out of work and into the home. Most women worked before, during and after it, and what changed was the location and terms. In the household economy that preceded the factory, described in Topic 5.3, women’s work was productive, took place at home and was compatible with childcare. The factory separated the workplace from the home and put fixed hours around it, which made those two things routinely incompatible for the first time and forced the choice on ordinary households rather than on a few. Middle-class households answered it by removing women from paid work; working-class households answered it by combining wage work with domestic work, or by taking in work that could be done at home for worse pay. One economic change, two answers, and the difference is what the household could afford.

The Arithmetic of an Unplanned City

c. 1800 to 1875  ·  Housing, filth, crime

The problems of the industrial city were not caused by the character of the people in it, which is what contemporaries mostly said. They were caused by a rate of growth that no existing infrastructure or government could absorb, and each named challenge follows from that arithmetic.

The numbers, and what they make impossible

Manchester went from roughly 25,000 to about 300,000 between 1772 and 1850; Britain passed from a majority rural to a majority urban population across the same century, the first country in history to do so. Migration came from the countryside, pushed by enclosure and by agricultural change and pulled by wages, and from Ireland in enormous numbers after the famine of the 1840s.

Now take the challenges the success criteria name and derive each one, which is what they are asking for.

Housing shortage. Population grew far faster than dwellings could be built, so rents rose, and what got built was built to the cheapest specification the market would bear, since there were no building regulations to set a floor. Hence back-to-backs, houses sharing three walls with neighbors so that only one side has windows and no through ventilation is possible; hence courts and cellars, with families living below street level in rooms that flooded; hence overcrowding measured in families per room rather than rooms per family. Overcrowding is not a preference, it is what happens when the number of people divided by the number of rooms produces that answer.

Insufficient infrastructure. A town of 25,000 has pipes, drains, wells and streets sized for 25,000. Multiply the population by twelve and there is no mechanism by which the pipes multiply too, because water was supplied by private companies to customers who could pay, drainage was a parish matter, and the municipal government was a corporation designed for a market town, in some cases with no authority to levy for such works at all. The reformed municipal corporations from 1835 and the public health legislation from 1848 are the beginning of building a government capable of the problem, and they arrive decades after the problem.

Pollution and filth. The coal smoke is in Topic 5.5. On the ground it was human and animal waste: privies shared by dozens of households, cesspools under floors, middens in courts, horse dung in the streets in enormous daily volumes, slaughterhouses and tanneries among the dwellings, and no collection service. This is the immediate setting for section 04.

Crime, and how to handle the claim. Recorded crime rose, and so did the number of things counted as crime, the number of police counting them, and the population being counted, so the statistics are measuring several changes at once. What is solid is that a city of strangers works differently from a village where everyone is known, since anonymity weakens informal control, and that a population with no savings and no relief but the workhouse will produce theft in a downturn. The response was institutional: the Metropolitan Police from 1829 and forces elsewhere after, plus prisons built on new principles. Contemporaries mostly read urban crime as moral degeneration among the poor, and that reading is itself evidence about the observers, which is the sort of distinction worth drawing in an essay.

Against the great cities of Topic 1.1 and 2.1: Song Kaifeng, Baghdad and Constantinople reached comparable sizes with functioning water supply, markets and sanitation, because they grew over centuries under states that built and maintained them. 04

Why the Water Killed People

1831 to 1866  ·  Cholera and typhoid

The success criteria ask specifically why overcrowded tenements without clean water or sewers produced cholera and typhoid, and the answer is a mechanism you can state in two sentences. Both diseases spread by the fecal-oral route, and a city with no sewers puts its waste into the water it drinks.

The mechanism

Cholera is caused by a bacterium ingested in contaminated water or food. It causes catastrophic diarrhea, and death follows from dehydration, sometimes within hours, which is why it terrified people in a way that endemic tuberculosis did not: a person could be well at breakfast and dead by evening. The infected material passes into the environment, and where sanitation is absent it reaches the water supply, which infects the next person. Typhoid works the same way, through contaminated water and food, more slowly.

Now put that mechanism in the city of section 03. Waste goes into cesspools and privies. Cesspools leak into the ground, and courts drain into ditches and streams. Water is drawn from shallow wells in the same ground, or piped by private companies from rivers into which the city's sewage flows, sometimes downstream of the outfall. Households share a standpipe running for a few hours a day, so water is stored in open vessels in crowded rooms. Every one of those steps closes the loop between what leaves a body and what enters the next one, and the density means one case reaches hundreds.

Then the perverse detail that makes this a good teaching case. Building proper sewers to flush waste out of the streets made the epidemics worse in the short term, because the sewers discharged into the Thames, and the Thames was where the water companies drew from. A sanitary improvement carried out under a wrong theory of disease moved the contamination from the street to the drinking supply, which is why the mechanism and not merely the effort is what has to be understood.

Britain had major cholera epidemics in 1831 to 1832, 1848 to 1849, 1853 to 1854 and 1866, with tens of thousands of deaths each time, and the disease had arrived from South Asia along the trade and troop routes of an expanding empire, which is a connection to Unit 6 worth noting: the same integration that moved cotton moved pathogens, exactly as the Topic 2.6 chapter describes for an earlier network.

How it was worked out, and what fixed it

The prevailing explanation was miasma theory, that disease arose from foul air given off by filth. It fitted the observation that disease was worst where the smell was worst, and it was wrong about the route while being right that filth was the problem, which is why sanitary reformers acting on it still did enormous good. The Topic 5.8 chapter has Chadwick, the reform movement and Bazalgette's sewers; what belongs here is how the mechanism itself was established.

John Snow argued that cholera was water-borne and demonstrated it in 1854 in Soho by mapping deaths against the pump they drew from, and, more powerfully, in a study comparing households in the same streets supplied by two different water companies, one drawing from a sewage-contaminated stretch of the Thames and one from further upstream, which found death rates several times higher among the first company's customers. That second study is the stronger evidence, because the households were otherwise similar in air, income and neighborhood, so the water was the only variable that differed. Snow did not persuade the profession immediately; germ theory, developed by Pasteur and Koch, who identified the cholera bacterium in 1883, supplied the explanation that made his result inevitable.

What actually ended the epidemics was engineering: intercepting sewers that carried waste far downstream, filtered water supplied under pressure, and eventually municipal ownership of the supply. Bazalgette's London system, funded after the Great Stink of 1858 made the river intolerable to Parliament itself, is the monument. Mortality in British cities improved substantially in the last third of the century, and clean water and sanitation are the largest identifiable reason.

How we know: two water companies on the same street

Snow’s South London study is worth knowing as a model of historical and scientific reasoning at once, because it is a natural experiment. In many streets, two competing companies had laid pipes house by house years earlier, so neighboring households, alike in air, income, diet and neighborhood, received water from different sources without anyone having chosen it for a reason connected to health. When one company moved its intake upstream of the sewage outfall and the other did not, the difference in cholera mortality between customers was enormous. That is why it convinces where the Broad Street map alone does not: the map shows an association, and the water companies supply a comparison in which everything except the water is held constant.

Building an Answer That Scores

Each card is a full argument: the claim, the specific evidence, and the reason. The second and third answer the two success criteria that ask you to explain why rather than to describe.

Structure

Middle-class status came from a source of income, not an amount

The middle class ran from factory owners and bankers through engineers, doctors and managers down to clerks and schoolteachers, united by income drawn from business, qualification or salary rather than inherited title or rent from land, which is a position the older order of birth and land had no category for. Its markers were deliberate and visible: a house away from the works, a servant, a parlor, schooling, and a wife who did not earn. Note that a clerk might earn less than a skilled engineer and count himself in it while placing the engineer out of it, which shows the category was social as much as economic. The working class sold labor for wages, and what defined its life was less the size of the wage than its insecurity, since injury or a downturn meant destitution within weeks and after 1834 relief was governed by a workhouse built to be worse than the worst job, held over applicants even where, as usually happened, they were relieved at home instead.

Causation

Separate spheres describes the households that could afford it

The doctrine assigned men the public world and women the home, and withdrawing a woman’s earnings requires a household able to live on one income, so a non-earning wife became the clearest visible proof that a family had reached that level. Working-class households needed every earner, so women worked in mills, workshops, agriculture, mines until the 1842 ban and above all in domestic service, the largest single female occupation of the century, and married women took in laundry, lodgers and sewing that the census never recorded. The same ideology therefore produced opposite lives and then supplied the vocabulary in which working-class women were criticized for neglecting a domestic role their household economy made impossible.

Mechanism

Every urban challenge follows from one rate of growth

Manchester went from about 25,000 in 1772 to about 300,000 by 1850, and pipes, drains, streets and government do not multiply because people arrive. Housing shortage follows because dwellings cannot be built that fast, so rents rise and what gets built meets the cheapest specification a market with no building regulation will bear, which is what back-to-backs and inhabited cellars are. Infrastructure shortfall follows because water was sold privately to those who could pay, drainage was a parish matter, and the corporation was designed for a market town. Filth follows from privies shared by dozens of households with no collection service. And crime statistics rose alongside the number of police counting them, so read them as measuring several changes at once.

Evidence

Two water companies on one street settle the question

Cholera and typhoid pass from one person to the next through contaminated water, so a city storing waste in leaking cesspools and drawing water from the same ground, or from a river below its own sewage outfall, connects every household’s waste to every other household’s cup, and density turns one case into an epidemic. The proof is Snow’s South London study of 1854: competing companies had laid pipes house by house years earlier, so neighboring households alike in air, income and diet received water from different sources for no health-related reason, and when one company moved its intake upstream of the sewage and the other did not, mortality among the two sets of customers diverged enormously. Building sewers under miasma theory had first made things worse by discharging into the Thames the water companies drew from.

01

Three Changes, Each With Its Mechanism

c. 1750 to 1900  ·  What actually broke

The success criteria ask you to name changes and tie each to a key concept, and tying means showing the causal link rather than filing the fact under a heading. Three will do more work than ten, if each one is written with the mechanism attached.

Energy, and everything downstream of it

The change: production stopped being limited by muscle, water and wind and began running on stored fossil energy.

Why it matters, mechanically: every economy before this one had a ceiling set by how much work living things and moving water could do in a year, and that ceiling is why output per person had risen so slowly for so long. Coal removes it, because a seam represents millions of years of accumulated energy available all at once. That is the single fact under everything else in Unit 5: the factory of Topic 5.3, the railway and telegraph of Topic 5.5, the cities of Topic 5.9 and the military gap that opens in Unit 6 are all consequences of an energy source that does not run out at the end of the season.

Tie it: this is the industrial economy key concept in its strongest form, and the environmental key concept as well, since a species burning fossil carbon at scale begins here, as the Topic 5.5 chapter records.

The wage relationship, and the political ideas it produced

The change: the typical relationship between a worker and the means of production inverted. Before, most people had access to some productive property, a strip of land, a workshop, a loom in the parlor; after, most people in industrial societies owned none and sold hours instead.

Why it matters, mechanically: as the Topic 5.6 chapter sets out, a person with no productive property has exactly one thing to withhold, which makes collective action the characteristic form of industrial politics and makes the legal treatment of combination, in Topic 5.8, the decisive question of the century. It also produced ideologies that had not existed before: socialism and Marxism are answers to a question that could not have been asked of a society of peasants and craftsmen.

Tie it: social structures and governance both, since this is the origin of class as the organizing political category and of the state's new responsibility for welfare, from the Factory Acts to Bismarck's pensions.

The collapse of distance, and a genuinely global economy

The change: the cost of moving goods and the time taken to move information both fell by orders of magnitude, through railways, steamships, the Suez Canal and the telegraph.

Why it matters, mechanically: the Topic 4.8 chapter argues that the world became connected in the sixteenth century in the sense that a closed circuit existed. This is different and it is worth stating as the difference: connection became cheap, which means ordinary bulk goods rather than luxuries, and it became fast, which means prices in Bombay and Liverpool move together in a day. That is what turns a network of trade into a single market, and it is why an Argentine harvest could lower a German farmer's income.

Tie it: economic systems and technology, and it is also the mechanism that made the imperialism of Unit 6 practicable, since an empire is only governable at the speed its orders travel.

Common mistake to avoid

Do not write "industrialization changed everything," and do not answer a change question by listing inventions. A change claim scores when it names what became possible that was not possible before and for whom. Compare two sentences. "The railroad was a major change because it made travel faster" names a fact. "Falling freight costs meant a farmer three hundred miles inland could sell into a national market, which rewarded production at scale and destroyed local producers that distance had protected" names a mechanism and its winners and losers. The second is an argument and the first is a caption.

The gains were distributed unevenly by region and by class, and the first generations of industrial workers, as Topic 5.3 records, largely did not receive them.

02

Three Continuities, and Why They Count

c. 1750 to 1900  ·  What held

A continuity is not a thing that failed to change. It is a structure that persists while its content changes completely, and saying which is the structure and which is the content is the whole of this part of the answer.

Steep hierarchy, with new parties to it

The continuity: a small group controlled most of the productive wealth and most of the political power, and a large majority worked for them with little of either.

Why it counts: compare a landlord and tenant in 1750 with a factory owner and worker in 1880. The names, the legal basis and the setting are entirely different, and the shape is the same: a propertied few, a working many, an enormous gap in wealth and security, and political institutions weighted toward the top. The Topic 5.6 chapter describes a new elite of capital displacing an elite of land, and displacing an elite is not the same as removing a hierarchy. That is the sentence a grader is looking for.

The honest complication, which strengthens the answer rather than weakening it: the terms did change in ways that mattered. Legal serfdom ended across Europe, Russia included in 1861; slavery was abolished across the Atlantic world; hereditary status gave way to a hierarchy that was formally open. So the correct claim is precise: hierarchy persisted as a structure and became economic and formally open rather than legal and closed, which is continuity of shape with change of mechanism.

The rights argument, and the position of most of the world

The continuity of the rights struggle. The Topic 5.1 chapter identifies the move, holding a general principle to its own terms, and every campaign in this unit runs it: revolutionaries against monarchs, the enslaved against enslavers, Chartists and unionists against property qualifications and combination law, women against a franchise defended in universal language. The parties change constantly and the argument does not, which is exactly what a continuity is. And it is a continuity that continues past this unit, since the anticolonial movements of Units 6 and 8 use the identical move against the empires that taught it to them.

The continuity of most people's lives. This one is worth naming because it corrects the unit's center of gravity. In 1900, the great majority of people on earth still farmed, still lived in villages, and still worked with tools their grandparents would have recognized. Industrialization was concentrated in a handful of regions, for the reasons the Topic 5.4 chapter gives, and outside them the nineteenth century did not transform daily labor. The changes reached those regions in a different form, as demand for cotton, rubber, guano and grain, as railways built to the coast, and as debt, all of which reshaped economies without industrializing them.

Writing that continuity is one of the most reliable ways to demonstrate a global perspective rather than a European one, and it is defensible with the evidence already in this unit.

How we know: measure it in wealth and in bodies

Two kinds of evidence support continuity claims about hierarchy, and they are worth knowing because assertions about inequality are easy to make and hard to test. The first is wealth and income distribution, reconstructed from tax records, probate inventories and estate duty returns, which for nineteenth-century Britain and France show wealth concentrated at the top through the whole period, with the concentration not falling before the twentieth century. The second is anthropometric: average heights, recorded in military recruitment and prison registers, track childhood nutrition, and they show working-class heights stagnating or falling in the early industrial decades and rising later. Both are records kept for other purposes, which is what makes them useful, since nobody was measuring recruits in order to prove a point about class.

Pitched too high, any structure persists and the claim becomes unfalsifiable, so a continuity is only worth writing if you can also state what would have counted as its ending.

Four Questions That Decide Which Side a Fact Belongs On

A method  ·  For any extent-of-change prompt

Most facts in this unit can be argued as continuity or as change, and a student who notices that usually panics. It is the opposite of a problem: it is the opening, because specifying which reading you mean and why is the analytical move the prompt is actually testing.

The four questions

1. At what level? Slavery is the clearest case. At the level of the institution, abolition across the Atlantic world is an enormous change. At the level of coerced labor, indentured servitude, debt peonage, the Congo rubber system of Topic 5.5 and the mita's descendants continue, so the practice persists in new legal forms. Both are true, and the sentence that scores says which level it is pitched at.

2. For whom? A Lancashire mill worker, a Bengali handloom weaver, an American plantation laborer, a Japanese farmer under the land tax and a Congolese rubber tapper are all in this unit, and the extent of change is wildly different for each. A claim that names its population is stronger than a claim about "society."

3. Over what span? From 1750 to 1800, very little has happened outside a few British counties. From 1750 to 1900, the world economy is unrecognizable. Prompts carry dates and the dates are part of the question.

4. Compared with what? Change is a comparison and is meaningless without a baseline. Compared with the century before, the nineteenth is a transformation. Compared with the twentieth, its technologies look continuous with what preceded them. Say what your baseline is.

Running the questions on one fact

Take a single fact and watch the four questions produce four different defensible answers, which is what makes them a method rather than a checklist.

The fact: women entered factory work in large numbers.

At what level? At the level of whether women worked, this is continuity, and the Topic 5.9 chapter says so: women had always worked. At the level of where and on whose terms, it is a genuine change, since the workplace separated from the home for the first time.

For whom? For working-class women it meant wage work away from home. For middle-class women it meant the opposite, withdrawal from paid work as a marker of status. One economic change, two opposite outcomes, decided by what a household could afford.

Over what span? Across the whole century it is change, since married women's property rights, education and eventually the vote follow. Across the first fifty years it is mostly a change of setting without a change of position.

Compared with what? Compared with the household economy immediately before, the factory is a break. Compared with the position of women in the societies of Units 1 to 4, the fundamentals of legal subordination and domestic responsibility are strikingly persistent until very late in this period.

Four answers, all supportable, and none of them contradicts the others because each is a different question. Writing one of them explicitly, with the qualifier attached, is what an extent-of-change prompt rewards.

A Thesis, Written Out

The third criterion  ·  A position, both sides, the reasoning

The success criteria ask for a defensible thesis that takes a position on the extent of change, supports both sides with specific evidence, and explains the reasoning. Here is one, written out, so you can see the shape rather than infer it.

The prompt, and a thesis that answers it

Prompt: Evaluate the extent to which industrialization transformed societies in the period from 1750 to 1900.

A weak thesis: "Industrialization changed society a lot, but some things stayed the same." It takes no position, names nothing, and could preface an essay about any period in the course.

A defensible thesis: "Industrialization transformed how societies produced and where their people lived more completely than any change since agriculture, but it left the basic structure of social hierarchy intact, replacing an elite of land with an elite of capital while the majority continued to work for others with little property or political power, and outside a small number of industrializing regions it altered what the world's farmers grew and sold without changing that they farmed."

Notice what that sentence does. It takes a position, that the change was profound in one dimension and limited in another. It specifies the dimensions, production and settlement against social structure. It names its scope, distinguishing industrializing regions from everywhere else. And it gives the grader a map of the essay, because each clause is a paragraph.

Supporting both sides, and stating the reasoning

The change side, with evidence. Britain passed from a majority rural to a majority urban society within a century, and Manchester grew from about 25,000 to about 300,000 between 1772 and 1850. Production moved from households working at their own pace to factories running on the clock, after Arkwright concentrated workers and machines at Cromford in 1771. Watt's rotary engine of 1781 and Bessemer's converter of 1856 removed the energy and materials ceilings, and the telegraph from 1844 separated the speed of information from the speed of transport for the first time in history. Entirely new political ideologies, socialism and Marxism, appeared to explain conditions that had not previously existed.

The continuity side, with evidence. A new elite displaced an old one rather than dissolving the hierarchy: the Reform Act of 1832 admitted the middle class to the political nation and left the working class outside, and Chartism was rejected. Wealth stayed concentrated at the top throughout, on the tax and probate evidence. Coerced labor persisted in new forms after abolition, in the Congo rubber system and in indenture and debt peonage. And in 1900 the great majority of the world's people still farmed, since industrialization reached a short list of regions and reached the rest as demand for raw materials, as railways built to the coast and as debt.

The reasoning, which is the part most answers omit. These two sides do not cancel, and the reason is that they describe different levels. Industrialization changed the means by which wealth was produced and the places where people lived, and it did so with a completeness that has few parallels. It did not change the relationship between those who own productive property and those who work it, and in the regions it did not industrialize it changed what was produced rather than how. A transformation of means with a persistence of relations is a coherent position, and it is why the nineteenth century produced both the greatest increase in output in history and the largest movements demanding that its benefits be shared.

Common mistake to avoid

Do not confuse acknowledging the other side with taking no position. A thesis that says change was extensive and then spends a paragraph on real continuities is stronger than one that hedges, because it has explained why the counterevidence does not defeat it. What loses marks is a thesis that lists both and never decides, or that decides and then ignores everything on the other side. The structure to imitate is: state the position, concede the strongest evidence against it, and then explain why your position survives it, usually because the two claims are pitched at different levels, cover different populations, or measure different spans.

Recognizing that a question has defensible answers on more than one side, and resolving it rather than balancing it.

Building an Answer That Scores

Each card is a full argument: the claim, the specific evidence, and the reason. The last one is a complete thesis with its concession attached, which is the deliverable this topic is asking for.

Change

Coal removed a ceiling every previous economy had lived under

Every economy in this course before the nineteenth century was limited to the work that muscle, water and wind could do in a year, which is why output per person had risen so slowly for so long. A coal seam is stored energy accumulated over geological time and spendable at whatever rate the machinery allows, so the constraint disappears. Everything else in Unit 5 sits downstream of that: the factory at Cromford in 1771, Watt’s rotary motion in 1781, Bessemer steel in 1856, railways, the telegraph, and cities that grew twelvefold in two generations. Note the contrast with Champa rice and the potato, which raised calories per acre and therefore the number of people supported; coal raised output per person, which is a change in kind rather than in scale.

Continuity

Displacing an elite is not removing a hierarchy

Compare a landlord and tenant in 1750 with a factory owner and worker in 1880: the names, the legal basis and the setting are entirely different and the shape is identical, a propertied few, a working many, a large gap in wealth and security, and political institutions weighted to the top. The Reform Act of 1832 admitted the middle class to the political nation and left the working class outside, Chartism was rejected, and tax and probate evidence shows wealth concentrated at the top throughout. The honest qualification strengthens the claim: legal serfdom and slavery ended and status became formally open, so hierarchy persisted as a structure while its basis moved from birth and land to capital.

Method

A fact has no answer until you say which comparison you mean

Women entering factory work reads as continuity at the level of whether women worked, since they always had, and as change at the level of where and on whose terms, since the workplace separated from the home. It meant wage work away from home for working-class women and withdrawal from paid work as a status marker for middle-class women, so the population named decides the answer. Across the whole century it is change, since property rights, education and the vote follow; across the first fifty years it is mostly a change of setting. And measured against the household economy immediately before it is a break, while measured against Units 1 to 4 the legal subordination is strikingly persistent. Four defensible answers, four different questions, and naming which one you mean is the analytical move.

Thesis

A transformation of means with a persistence of relations

Industrialization transformed how societies produced and where their people lived more completely than any change since agriculture, and left the basic structure of social hierarchy intact, replacing an elite of land with an elite of capital while the majority continued working for others with little property or power, and outside a small number of regions it altered what the world’s farmers grew and sold without changing that they farmed. The two halves do not cancel because they are pitched at different levels: the means of production and the places people lived changed almost completely, the relationship between owners and workers did not, and where industry never arrived the change was in the crop rather than the work. That is also why the same century produced the largest rise in output in history and the largest movements demanding it be shared.
