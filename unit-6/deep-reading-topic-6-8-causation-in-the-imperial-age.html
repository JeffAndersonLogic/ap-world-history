Topic 6.8  ·  Deep Reading  ·  AP World History: Modern

Which One Mattered Most

By now you have four effects of imperialism, a dozen mechanisms and about sixty datable facts, and an essay has room for perhaps a third of them. The question this topic asks is not what happened. It is which of the things that happened mattered most, and why, and that is a question you cannot answer at all until you decide what "mattered" is being measured in. This chapter is about making that decision and defending it.

How to Use This

This chapter is a method rather than a narrative, so read it with a specific essay question in mind if you have one. Section 02 is the part that changes how you write: four tests for significance that rank the same four effects in different orders, which is why a thesis has to name its measure before it can defend a ranking.

  1. 01 Four effects: each stated with its mechanism, so they can be weighed instead of listed.
  2. 02 Four tests: scale, duration, reversibility and the counterfactual, and how each one changes the ranking.
  3. 03 The causes: necessary, permissive and accelerating, plus the argument historians actually have about this.
  4. 04 A worked thesis: one written out in full, with its evidence structure underneath it.
  5. Then the closing section, which turns all of it into comparison sentences you can write.
01

Four Effects, Each With Its Working Part

c. 1750 to 1900  ·  Extraction, states, movement, hierarchy

You cannot rank things you can only name. Each of the four effects below is stated as a mechanism, because a mechanism can be compared against another mechanism on scale, on duration and on how hard it was to undo, and a label cannot be compared against anything.

The four, stated so they can be weighed

1. Economic restructuring and its infrastructure. Whole regions were reorganized to supply one commodity to industrial markets: Egyptian and Indian cotton, Congolese and Amazonian rubber, West African palm oil, Peruvian guano, Malayan tin and rubber, southern African gold and diamonds. The mechanism, from Topic 6.4, is specialization without linkages: the processing, machine-building and financial industries that a commodity generates grew in the importing country, and the transport built to move it ran from a producing region to a port rather than between producing regions. The result is an economy that could grow for fifty years and end with the same range of jobs it started with, plus a state whose revenue and borrowing rested on a price set abroad.

2. State expansion, and the states remade by resisting it. Between 1870 and 1914 nearly the whole of Africa, most of Southeast Asia and much of the Pacific passed under foreign rule, and Japan, Russia and the United States built their own empires with the same tools. The mechanism, from Topic 6.2, is a collapse in the cost of conquest, quinine, steamers, breech-loaders, telegraph, and colonies made to pay for their own occupation through hut taxes and locally recruited regiments. The corresponding mechanism, from Topic 6.3, is that resistance reshaped the states it failed against: 1857 abolished the East India Company and rebuilt the Indian Army and the whole logic of British rule.

3. Mass migration and the societies it composed. Something on the order of a hundred and fifty million people moved in three streams of comparable size. The mechanism, from Topic 6.6, is that four conditions had to hold at once and that the last two, an affordable route and a legal status, are what changed; and from Topic 6.7, that where recruitment brought in populations comparable in size to those already present, it composed plural societies whose administrative categories became their political ones.

4. Racial hierarchy written into law and institutions. The ideologies of Topic 6.1 did not stay in speeches. They became the subject-citizen distinction and the code de l'indigenat in French Africa, the martial-races recruitment doctrine in India, pass laws and closed compounds in southern Africa, the census categories of Topic 6.7, the dictation tests and exclusion acts, and school curricula on three continents. The mechanism is that a classification becomes self-confirming once rights, wages, land and offices are distributed by it, because the resulting inequality is then produced by the classification and cited as evidence for it.

Why the four are not independent

Before ranking them, notice that they interlock, and saying how is one of the fastest ways to show complexity in an essay.

Extraction required labor, which required migration, which required legal categories to control it, which were supplied by racial hierarchy. State expansion made extraction enforceable and was in several cases caused by extraction gone wrong, as in Egypt in 1882, where a debt produced an occupation. Migration was organized by states and restricted by them the moment its results became politically inconvenient. And racial hierarchy was the vocabulary in which all three were explained to electorates at home and administered to populations abroad.

So an answer that ranks them has to be careful not to rank a cause above its own effect. The strong move is to identify which one, if removed, would have changed the most about the others, and that is the counterfactual test in section 02.

Use this in your answer
Strongest tool, with its mechanism
State an effect as a mechanism so it can be compared. The mechanism is that a label, "economic exploitation", has no properties you can measure against another label, while a stated mechanism, "processing industries grow at the importing end and transport is built toward a port", has a scale, a duration and a reversibility, which are exactly the dimensions on which one effect can be argued to outweigh another.
Best evidence of exclusion or limit
The four are analytic categories rather than separate phenomena, and any real case sits in several at once, so the ranking is a claim about emphasis rather than about separable causes.
Comparison hook
Against Topic 5.10, the Unit 5 synthesis: there the task was change against continuity and the method was to ask what a person’s daily life would have registered. Here the task is relative significance and the method is a stated measure, but the underlying discipline is identical, which is deciding what your claim is about before making it.

Terms to use precisely

Relative significance
A judgment about which effect mattered most, which is meaningless until the measure of mattering is named.
Mechanism
A statement of how one thing produced another, which is what makes an effect comparable rather than merely nameable.
Interlocking effects
The way extraction, expansion, migration and hierarchy each required and reinforced the others.
Self-confirming classification
A category that produces the inequality later cited as evidence for it, once rights and wages are distributed by it.
Ranking a cause above its effect
The error of weighing two items when one produced the other, avoided by using a counterfactual.
02

Four Tests, and They Do Not Agree

The method  ·  Scale, duration, reversibility, counterfactual

"Most significant" is not a fact waiting to be found, it is a claim relative to a measure. Below are four measures that a historian would actually accept, and the useful discovery is that they rank the same four effects in different orders, which is why naming your measure is not a formality but the substance of the argument.

The four measures

Scale: how many people were affected, and how deeply. On this measure migration and economic restructuring lead, because they touched the daily lives of hundreds of millions who never saw a colonial official: a Bengali spinner losing her income to machine yarn, a Sicilian family living on remittances, an Igbo household paying a tax in cash it had to earn by selling palm oil. Formal political control, by contrast, changed who signed a document at the top of a system whose lower levels went on being run by the same people.

Duration: how long the effect outlasted the empire. On this measure the economic structures and the racial-legal categories lead, and political control comes last, because political control was the part that ended. Colonial rule was gone across most of Asia and Africa within twenty years of 1945. Single-commodity export economies, transport networks pointed at ports, external debt, and the population categories written into censuses and electoral rolls were still there afterward, and in many cases still are.

Reversibility: how hard was it to undo. Related to duration and not the same. A tariff can be changed by a new government in a session, and a rail network built to serve a port takes fifty years and capital nobody has to rebuild; a law can be repealed, and a demography cannot be. This test is the one that most often reorders a student's instinctive ranking, because the effects that look most dramatic, conquest, war, occupation, tend to be the reversible ones.

The counterfactual: what changes if this one is removed? Take away the industrial demand for raw materials and the whole structure loses its motive. Take away the cost collapse of Topic 6.2 and the same demand is met by trade rather than by conquest, roughly as it had been for the previous century. Take away the ideologies of Topic 6.1 and the annexations are probably still made and are harder to sell at home and are administered differently. Ranked by counterfactual weight, industrial demand and the cost collapse come first and ideology comes later, which is a different order from the previous three tests and is exactly why you must say which test you are using.

Using them, and the mistakes they prevent

A thesis built on these does something a list cannot: it says what it is claiming and admits what it is not. "Measured by what outlasted the empire, the economic restructuring of colonized regions was the most significant effect, because political control ended within two decades of 1945 while single-commodity dependence, port-facing infrastructure and external debt persisted for generations." That sentence takes a position, names its measure, and concedes the strongest rival case in its own second clause, which is what a complex-understanding rubric is asking for.

Two errors these tests prevent. The first is the list disguised as an argument: naming four effects, describing each, and concluding that all were important, which takes no position and therefore cannot be defended or attacked. The second is the unstated measure: asserting that one effect was most significant without saying on what dimension, which usually means scale is being assumed while the evidence offered is about duration.

Common mistake to avoid

Do not conclude that because significance depends on the measure, every ranking is equally good and the honest answer is that everything mattered. That is the same non-answer arrived at by a more sophisticated route. The measures are not arbitrary: they are stated in advance, they are applied to evidence, and a ranking that gets its evidence wrong is simply wrong on any of them. A claim that political control was the most significant effect measured by duration is refutable, and it is refuted, by the fact that formal empire ended across most of Asia and Africa within twenty years of 1945 while the economic and legal structures did not. Pick the measure, apply it honestly, and let the evidence decide the ranking. Relativism about the measure is not the same as relativism about the answer.

Use this in your answer
Strongest tool, with its mechanism
Name the measure before the ranking. The mechanism is that "most significant" has no truth value on its own, so an argument becomes testable only when it specifies the dimension, how many people, how long it lasted, how hard it was to undo, what changes if it is removed, at which point evidence can support or refute it and a grader can see you making a defensible claim rather than an assertion.
Best evidence of exclusion or limit
The tests conflict by design, and no procedure resolves the conflict for you: choosing which measure the question is really about is a judgment, and defending that choice is part of the essay.
Comparison hook
Against the periodization arguments in Topics 4.8 and 5.10: the same discipline again, since "when does the modern era begin" is also undecidable until you say what you are measuring. Learn the move once and it works on every reasoning-skill prompt in the course.

Terms to use precisely

Scale test
Ranking by how many people were affected and how deeply, which favors migration and economic restructuring.
Duration test
Ranking by what outlasted the empire, which favors economic structures and legal categories over political control.
Reversibility test
Ranking by how hard an effect was to undo, which often reorders the dramatic above the durable.
Counterfactual test
Asking what changes if one factor is removed, which is what identifies a necessary condition.
List disguised as argument
Naming several effects and concluding all were important, which takes no position and cannot be defended.
03

Necessary, Permissive, Accelerating

The historians' argument  ·  Hobson, Lenin, and the evidence against them

Causes of imperialism are usually taught as a list of five with equal billing, which is the same failure as a list of effects. Sorting them into necessary conditions, permissive conditions and accelerants produces an argument, and it also lets you use the dispute historians actually have about this, which is worth real credit.

Sorting them

Necessary: industrial demand. Without factories requiring cotton, rubber, palm oil, tin and nitrate, and populations requiring tea, sugar and grain, there is no motive for any of it. Remove this and the whole structure loses its purpose. It is necessary and it is nowhere near sufficient, because the demand existed for decades before the annexations.

Permissive: the collapse in the cost of conquest. Quinine, steamers, breech-loaders and telegraph, and above all the fiscal arrangement by which colonies paid for their own occupation. This does not cause anything by itself; it removes the obstacle that had been holding the motive in check, which is exactly what "permissive" means and is the category students most often miss.

Accelerating: great-power rivalry and popular nationalism. The positional-good mechanism of Topic 6.1 and the effective-occupation rule of Topic 6.2 explain the speed and much of the target selection, and cannot explain why it happened at all, since rivalry between European states was not new in 1880.

Enabling and shaping: ideology. The four rationales made the decisions acceptable at home and determined the form of rule, what was taught, whose law was recognized, which land was recorded as empty. Not the engine, not decoration.

Contingent: the crisis on the spot. Several major annexations were responses to a local breakdown rather than to a plan in a capital: Egypt in 1882 because a debt regime produced a nationalist movement, and a chain of West African advances because a treaty with one ruler committed a power against his neighbor. This category is why the map does not look like anybody's design.

The argument historians have, and what would settle it

In 1902 the English economist J. A. Hobson argued that imperialism was driven by surplus capital: an unequal domestic distribution of income meant investors could not find profitable outlets at home, so they sought them abroad and pushed their governments into acquiring the territories to protect them. Lenin took the argument in 1916 and made it structural, calling imperialism the highest stage of capitalism and tying it to monopoly and finance capital. This is still the most widely known explanation of the period.

The evidence against it, developed by later economic historians, is specific and is worth knowing precisely because it is a good example of a theory meeting data. Most British overseas investment in this period went not to the new tropical colonies but to the United States, Canada, Australia and Argentina, and the new African possessions attracted very little capital and generated very little trade for decades. If empire was built to place surplus capital, the capital did not go where the empire went.

The alternative developed by Ronald Robinson and John Gallagher shifts the causation outward: annexations were frequently improvised responses by an "official mind" in London to crises on the periphery, above all to threats to the route to India, which is a strategic and reactive explanation rather than an economic and planned one. Their account has its own critics, who point out that the crises on the periphery were themselves generated by prior economic penetration, which is exactly what happened in Egypt.

Here is how to use this in an essay without pretending to settle it. Say that the economic explanation is strongest for the informal empire of Topic 6.5, China's treaty ports, Argentine railways, Egyptian debt, where the money and the control are demonstrably in the same place, and weakest for the formal partition of tropical Africa, where they are demonstrably not; and that the strategic and contingent explanations are strongest exactly where the economic one is weakest. That is a genuine synthesis rather than a fence-sit, because it specifies which explanation applies where and says why.

How we know: this is a dispute about numbers, which is why it is resolvable

The Hobson and Lenin argument is unusual among grand historical theories in that it makes a claim that can be checked: capital went abroad because it could not profit at home, and it went where the flag went. Investment is recorded, in stock exchange listings, company registrations, bond issues and balance-of-payments estimates, and when economic historians assembled those series in the twentieth century the geography did not match, since the great destinations of British capital were the settler economies and the United States rather than the new tropical possessions. That is what a testable theory looks like when it meets its evidence. Two cautions before you use the refutation too confidently: the data are reconstructions with real margins of error, and a defender can reply that the argument is about the system rather than about matching investment to territory, which is a weaker but not empty claim. Say which version you are addressing.

Use this in your answer
Strongest tool, with its mechanism
Sorting causes by their logical role. The mechanism is that a necessary condition supplies the motive without which nothing happens, a permissive condition removes an obstacle without supplying any motive, and an accelerant changes only the speed and the targets, so labeling each cause by its role produces a ranked argument out of the same five items that would otherwise be an unranked list.
Best evidence of exclusion or limit
The categories are not always clean, and a condition can move between them over time: the cost collapse was permissive in the 1870s and became an accelerant once rivals could see how cheap conquest had become.
Comparison hook
Against the causes of industrialization in Topic 5.3: there the chapter’s whole argument was that no single factor is sufficient because each existed elsewhere without the result, and the same test applies here. Industrial demand existed before the annexations, and rivalry existed for centuries, so the interesting question in both cases is what combination arrived at once.

Terms to use precisely

Necessary condition
A cause without which the outcome does not occur, here the industrial demand for raw materials.
Permissive condition
A change that removes an obstacle without supplying a motive, here the collapse in the cost of conquest.
Hobson-Lenin thesis
The argument that surplus capital seeking outlets abroad drove imperial expansion.
The investment objection
The finding that most British capital went to settler economies and the United States rather than to new tropical colonies.
Peripheral crisis
Robinson and Gallagher's account of annexation as improvised response to local breakdowns and threats to the route to India.
04

A Thesis, Written Out

The work  ·  One position, three paragraphs of evidence, one concession

Everything above is preparation for one paragraph. Here is a thesis that would score, with the reasoning that produced it and the evidence that would carry it, and then a second thesis taking the opposite position on a different measure, so you can see that the structure rather than the conclusion is what is being rewarded.

The thesis, and why each clause is there

The prompt: Evaluate the relative significance of the effects of imperialism on the world between 1750 and 1900.

The thesis: Measured by what survived the end of empire, the economic restructuring of colonized and semi-colonized regions was the most significant effect of imperialism, because the export monocultures, port-facing infrastructure and external debts established between 1850 and 1900 outlasted colonial rule by generations, while formal political control, the most visible effect at the time, ended across most of Asia and Africa within two decades of 1945; the demographic and legal structures created by mass labor migration are the strongest rival case, and they are best understood as the social form the same economic reorganization took.

Four things are doing work in that sentence and each is worth copying as a habit. It names its measure in the first four words, so every later paragraph can be checked against it. It takes one position rather than four. It concedes the strongest rival explicitly, which is what a complexity point looks like. And its last clause makes a relationship claim, that migration was the social form of economic restructuring, which is more sophisticated than ranking two independent items and is defensible from Topics 6.4, 6.6 and 6.7.

The evidence, and the opposite thesis

Paragraph one, the claim. Egypt built an export crop from the 1820s, borrowed against the boom of the American cotton famine, defaulted in 1876, accepted the Caisse de la Dette and dual control, and was occupied in 1882; the cotton, the debt and the single-commodity structure were still shaping Egyptian politics long after 1954. India ended the century exporting raw cotton and importing cloth under a tariff regime it did not control. Argentina, never colonized, ended it with foreign-owned railways radiating to one port and an economy resting on one market.

Paragraph two, the mechanism. Specialization without linkages: processing, machine-building and finance grew at the importing end, transport was built to move a commodity out rather than to connect regions to each other, and government borrowing rested on prices set abroad. That structure is expensive and slow to reverse in a way that a change of flag is not, which is why it is the one that persisted.

Paragraph three, the concession made into an argument. Migration has the better claim on the scale test, since it touched a hundred and fifty million people directly and composed entire societies in Fiji, Trinidad, Guyana, Mauritius and Malaya, and the categories it produced still organize politics there. But the recruitment followed the plantations and the mines, the exclusion laws followed the labor market, and the legal statuses were designed to serve production, so on the measure named in the thesis the demographic effect is the social expression of the economic one rather than a competitor to it.

And the opposite thesis, equally scoreable: Measured by how many people were directly affected, the mass migrations of the period were the most significant effect, because roughly a hundred and fifty million people moved and both the societies they left and those they entered were reconstituted around that movement, whereas the economic restructuring, though more durable, operated through markets that many rural producers experienced only intermittently. Same evidence base, different measure, different ranking, and a grader can follow both. That is the point of the chapter: the discipline is in stating the measure and applying it honestly, not in reaching a particular verdict.

Use this in your answer
Strongest tool, with its mechanism
Concede the rival by absorbing it. The mechanism is that acknowledging the strongest opposing case and then showing it stands in a specific relationship to your own claim, that migration was the social form the economic reorganization took, is stronger than either ignoring the rival or listing it as an equal, because it explains the rival evidence rather than merely surviving it.
Best evidence of exclusion or limit
The absorbing move fails if the relationship is asserted rather than shown, so it has to be earned with specifics: recruitment following plantations, exclusion laws following labor demand, legal status designed to serve production.
Comparison hook
Against the worked thesis in Topic 5.10: the same architecture, measure first, one position, the rival absorbed, applied to change and continuity rather than to relative significance. Two prompts, one paragraph shape, which is why the shape is worth learning rather than the sentence.

Terms to use precisely

Stated measure
The dimension on which significance is claimed, named in the thesis so every paragraph can be checked against it.
Concession
Explicit acknowledgment of the strongest rival case, which is what a complexity rubric is looking for.
Relationship claim
Asserting how two effects stand to one another rather than ranking them as independent items.
Defensible claim
A position that could in principle be refuted by evidence, which is what makes it worth arguing.
Evidence structure
Claim, mechanism, then the rival absorbed, the order that keeps a long answer under control.

Building an Answer That Scores

Each card is a full argument: the claim, the specific evidence, and the reason. This chapter's cards are about method, so treat them as sentence patterns to reuse rather than as content to recall.

Method

Significance is relative to a measure, and the measures disagree

On scale, migration and economic restructuring lead, because they reached hundreds of millions who never met a colonial official: a spinner losing her income to machine yarn, a family living on remittances, a household paying a cash tax it had to earn by selling palm oil. On duration, economic structures and racial-legal categories lead and political control comes last, because political control is the part that ended, gone across most of Asia and Africa within twenty years of 1945. On reversibility, a tariff changes in a session and a rail network built toward a port does not. On the counterfactual, industrial demand and the collapse in the cost of conquest come first and ideology later. Four honest measures, four different orders, which is why a thesis must name its measure in its first clause.

Causation

Sort the causes by role and the list becomes an argument

Industrial demand for cotton, rubber, palm oil, tin and nitrate is necessary: remove it and nothing has a motive. The collapse in the cost of conquest is permissive: quinine, steamers, breech-loaders, telegraph, and colonies paying for their own occupation removed the obstacle without supplying a motive, which is why the same demand produced trade before 1870 and annexation after. Great-power rivalry and popular nationalism are accelerants that explain the speed and the targets and not the phenomenon, since rivalry was not new in 1880. Ideology supplied permission and shape. And a set of major annexations were contingent responses to crises on the spot, which is why the map looks like nobody’s design.

Evidence

The best-known explanation of imperialism has a data problem

Hobson argued in 1902 that surplus capital, unable to find profitable outlets at home because of unequal income distribution, was invested abroad and pulled governments into protecting it; Lenin made the argument structural in 1916. It is testable, which is unusual for a grand theory, and the test goes badly: most British overseas investment went to the United States, Canada, Australia and Argentina, while the new African possessions attracted very little capital and little trade for decades. Robinson and Gallagher’s alternative makes annexation an improvised response by an official mind to peripheral crises and threats to the route to India. The synthesis worth writing is that the economic explanation is strongest for informal empire, treaty ports, Argentine railways, Egyptian debt, where money and control sit in the same place, and weakest for the partition of tropical Africa, where they do not.

Thesis

One position, a named measure, and the rival absorbed

"Measured by what survived the end of empire, the economic restructuring of colonized and semi-colonized regions was the most significant effect, because the export monocultures, port-facing infrastructure and external debts established between 1850 and 1900 outlasted colonial rule by generations, while formal political control ended across most of Asia and Africa within two decades of 1945; the demographic and legal structures created by mass labor migration are the strongest rival case, and they are best understood as the social form the same reorganization took." Measure named first, one position taken, rival conceded and then explained rather than dismissed, with the relationship earned by specifics: recruitment followed the plantations, exclusion laws followed the labor market, and legal status was designed to serve production. Change the measure to scale and the opposite thesis scores just as well, which is the point.
