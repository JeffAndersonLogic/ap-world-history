Topic 8.8  ·  Deep Reading  ·  AP World History: Modern

What the System Could No Longer Pay For

The Soviet Union did not lose a war. It ran out of the ability to pay for the things holding it together, and the three causes this topic names are versions of that one problem: an economy that had exhausted the kind of growth it knew how to produce, an arms race that moved into technologies it could not make, and a war on its border it could neither win nor leave cheaply. Then a reform program removed the fear the system had been using in place of consent.

01

An Economy That Ran Out of the Growth It Knew How to Make

1928 to 1986  ·  The first five-year plan to the oil price collapse

Soviet planning was very good at one kind of growth, which is throwing more workers, more land, more coal and more steel at a defined target, and structurally bad at the other kind, which is getting more out of the same inputs. By the 1970s the first kind had run out of cheap inputs, so the growth the system still needed had to come from the kind it was built badly for.

Two kinds of growth, and three mechanisms

Economists distinguish extensive growth, which comes from adding inputs, from intensive growth, which comes from using the same inputs better. Soviet industrialization from 1928 was extensive on an enormous scale: peasants moved into factories, new coalfields and ore bodies were opened, and output of the things the plan measured rose fast enough to win a war. The problem with extensive growth is that it ends. By the 1970s the labor force was growing slowly, the accessible resources had been used and the new ones were in Siberia at high cost, and the war's demographic hole was still working its way through the population.

Three mechanisms explain why the second kind of growth was so hard to produce, and each is worth a sentence in an essay. The soft budget constraint. A Soviet enterprise could not go bankrupt: if it lost money the ministry covered it. An organization that cannot fail has no reason to economize on anything, so it hoards labor, materials and machinery against the day the plan changes, and the economist Janos Kornai built an account of chronic shortage on exactly that point.

The ratchet. A factory that beat its target this year received a higher target next year, so the rational manager hid capacity and negotiated the plan downward, which means the planners were bargaining with people paid to misinform them. The measurement problem. A target set in tons produces heavy goods, a target set in units produces small ones, and prices fixed administratively carry no information about what is scarce, so a planner cannot tell an efficient factory from a wasteful one by looking at the accounts.

Put together, these produce a system that hits its targets and misses the point. Shortage was not a series of mistakes, it was what the arrangement produced when working normally, and Soviet citizens experienced it as queues, waiting lists for apartments and cars, and a large informal economy of favors and connections that people relied on to obtain ordinary things. That is the ground the public discontent in the third criterion grows out of, and it long predates 1985.

Oil bought twenty years

A country with those problems still needs to eat and to import machinery, and from the late 1960s the Soviet Union found a way to do both. The great West Siberian oil fields came into production, output rose steeply, and then the world price of oil rose sharply after 1973 and again after 1979. The hard currency that produced paid for imported grain, which the country had begun buying in quantity from the early 1970s, and for Western machinery and technology.

The mechanism to carry out of this is not that oil made the Soviet Union rich. It is that oil made reform postponable. Revenue from a commodity whose price is set elsewhere let the leadership fund consumption and defense at the same time without confronting the structural problem, and a windfall used that way buys time at the cost of the reason to change. In 1986 the world oil price fell by roughly half, in Gorbachev's second year, which removed the cushion at the exact moment a reform program needed room to fail in. The economist and later Russian prime minister Yegor Gaidar made this the center of his account of the collapse. It is an influential reading rather than a settled one, and other historians give more weight to the political decisions of the reform years and less to the commodity cycle.

One more number that a good answer handles carefully. The share of Soviet output going to defense was published officially at around five or six percent. American intelligence estimated it at something closer to fifteen to seventeen percent of a smaller economy, and figures higher still have been given by Soviet and Russian officials since. Whichever estimate you use, the comparison to make is not with the American percentage but with what the same resources would have done in a civilian sector that was already failing to supply its population.

How we know: everyone was estimating, and the estimates are now the subject

There was no independent measurement of the Soviet economy. Official statistics were produced by the system being measured, in prices that carried no scarcity information, and Western figures were reconstructions built by intelligence agencies and academic economists from physical output data, defector reports and published fragments. After 1991 those reconstructions became a controversy in their own right, with critics arguing that Western estimates of Soviet output had been too high for years and defenders replying that the estimates tracked the direction of travel correctly even when the levels were wrong. Two lessons for your writing. Attribute the figure, so "American intelligence estimated" rather than a bare percentage. And notice what the argument implies: almost nobody, in either country, predicted the collapse, which is evidence about the limits of the sources rather than about the intelligence of the people using them.

Use this in your answer
Strongest tool, with its mechanism
The exhaustion of extensive growth. The mechanism is that Soviet planning produced growth by adding inputs to measured targets, which works while labor and accessible resources are cheap and stops when they are not, and the system had no way to switch to productivity growth because an enterprise that cannot fail will not economize, a manager who beats a target is punished with a higher one, and administered prices tell a planner nothing about what is scarce.
Best evidence of exclusion or limit
Structural weakness explains a long decline and not a date. The system generated shortage for decades without collapsing, so an answer needs what changed in the 1980s: the oil cushion going, a reform program that disrupted supply before improving it, and the political decisions in sections 02 and 04.
Comparison hook
Against Topic 8.4 on the Great Leap Forward: both are cases where the information a planning system ran on was produced by people with reasons to distort it. In China the falsified harvest reports turned failure into famine within two years; in the Soviet Union the ratchet and the soft budget produced slow, survivable misallocation over thirty. Same mechanism, opposite tempo, and the comparison is a strong one to make.

Terms to use precisely

Extensive growth
Output growth produced by adding inputs, labor, land and raw materials, which ends when cheap inputs run out.
Soft budget constraint
Kornai’s term for an enterprise that cannot go bankrupt because the state covers its losses, and therefore has no reason to economize.
The ratchet effect
The planning problem in which exceeding a target raises next year’s target, so managers conceal capacity and bargain the plan down.
Chronic shortage
Persistent unavailability of ordinary goods, produced by the system operating normally rather than by particular failures.
The oil cushion
Hard currency from West Siberian oil after the 1973 and 1979 price rises, which paid for grain and machinery and made reform postponable until the 1986 price fall.
02

A Competition Moved Into Terrain One Side Could Not Enter

1981 to 1987  ·  The defense buildup to the INF Treaty

The mechanism the criteria are asking for is not that the United States spent more money. It is that the competition moved into microelectronics, computing and precision guidance, where the Soviet disadvantage was structural rather than budgetary, so matching it would have required changing the system rather than reallocating within it.

Why the Soviets could build tanks and not microprocessors

American defense spending roughly doubled in nominal dollars between 1980 and the mid-1980s, rising from about five percent of national output to a little over six. Those numbers matter less than where the money went, which was disproportionately into guidance systems, sensors, stealth, communications and computing.

A command economy is well suited to producing a large quantity of a standardized object to a specification, which is why Soviet tank and artillery production was formidable. Computing is a different kind of problem. It advances through wide diffusion, constant lateral communication between people who are not in the same chain of command, cheap copying of documents, and tolerance of failed attempts. Soviet security practice restricted precisely those things: photocopiers were controlled and access to them logged, contact with foreign researchers was supervised, and the general-purpose computers that a Western engineer used casually were scarce and allocated. On top of that, a decision taken in the late 1960s to base the main Soviet mainframe series on a copy of an American architecture locked Soviet computing into reverse engineering, which by construction keeps you behind whoever you are copying.

That is the background against which Reagan announced the Strategic Defense Initiative on 23 March 1983: a research program toward defenses that would intercept incoming missiles, described by its critics as unbuildable and by the press as Star Wars. Judge it as a piece of pressure rather than as a weapon system, because it was never deployed and much of the physics community doubted it could be. What it presented to Soviet planners was a demand for very large investment in exactly the sector where they were weakest, on a timetable set by somebody else, at a moment when the oil revenue in section 01 was about to disappear. Soviet responses were argued out internally, and several participants later described the initiative as a serious concern precisely because they doubted their ability to compete in that domain.

And notice that the same administration negotiated. Reagan met Gorbachev at Geneva in 1985 and at Reykjavik in 1986, where the two came close to an agreement on eliminating far more than either side's officials had expected, and in December 1987 they signed the treaty that eliminated an entire class of intermediate-range missiles, with intrusive verification. A story about pressure alone cannot explain that, and a story about goodwill alone cannot explain the buildup.

The dispute, and what evidence would settle it

How much the arms race mattered relative to internal failure is a genuine and unresolved argument, and it is worth naming the positions rather than choosing between them in your first sentence.

The pressure account holds that sustained American investment, and SDI in particular, raised the cost of continuing the confrontation until the Soviet leadership concluded it had to be wound down, so the buildup contributed directly to the outcome. The internal failure account holds that the Soviet system was declining on its own terms for structural reasons that had nothing to do with Washington, that the buildup affected timing at most, and that it may even have delayed reform by strengthening the argument of Soviet officials who wanted to answer pressure with pressure. A third strand, associated with historians who have worked in the Soviet archives, gives independent weight to individual decision: Gorbachev and a small group around him held beliefs about the confrontation that other plausible successors did not hold, and a different general secretary in 1985 plausibly produces a different decade.

What would distinguish them is evidence, so say what you would look for. Whether Soviet military spending actually rose in response to SDI, which several post-Soviet accounts suggest it largely did not in the way the pressure thesis predicts. Whether the internal documents of the reform years track external pressure or domestic indicators. And whether the concessions came where the pressure was applied. A student who writes that the United States spent the Soviet Union into the ground has taken a side in this argument without knowing there was one.

Common mistake to avoid

Do not write that America outspent the Soviet Union and won, and do not swing to the opposite error of saying the buildup made no difference. The first treats a contested interpretation as a fact and ignores that Soviet decline was well advanced before 1981; the second ignores that Soviet officials themselves described the technological competition as unwinnable. Write the narrower claim, which is stronger than either: the competition moved into a sector where the Soviet disadvantage was structural rather than budgetary, which meant matching it would have required changing the system rather than reallocating inside it, and that raised the perceived cost of continuing at the moment the oil revenue that had paid for everything was disappearing. Then say what evidence would test it.

Use this in your answer
Strongest tool, with its mechanism
Competing where the opponent cannot follow. The mechanism is that a rivalry's cost depends on the terrain it is fought over, so shifting the competition from mass production of standardized hardware, where a command economy performs well, into computing and precision guidance, where it performs badly for reasons built into how it controls information, imposes a cost the other side cannot answer by spending more within its existing arrangements.
Best evidence of exclusion or limit
It is one cause among three named by the criteria, and the weighting is disputed. Several post-Soviet accounts find that Soviet military spending did not surge in response to SDI, which is evidence against the strongest version of the pressure argument.
Comparison hook
Against Topic 8.2 on deterrence: that chapter argues nuclear weapons made a direct clash too expensive and pushed the competition outward. This is the same logic applied to technology rather than to geography, and SDI is disruptive precisely because a working defense would have unpicked the mutual vulnerability the whole system rested on.

Terms to use precisely

Strategic Defense Initiative
The missile-defense research program announced on 23 March 1983, never deployed, whose effect was on Soviet calculations rather than on the battlefield.
Structural disadvantage
A weakness built into how a system works rather than into how much it spends, which more money cannot fix.
Reverse engineering lock-in
Basing a technology on a copy of somebody else’s, which guarantees a permanent lag behind the originator.
INF Treaty
The December 1987 agreement eliminating an entire class of intermediate-range missiles with intrusive verification, signed by the same administration that had led the buildup.
New thinking
The Gorbachev-era doctrine reframing security as something to be achieved with an adversary rather than against one, and the reason individual decision is part of the argument.
03

Afghanistan: A Bill, and a Signal That Cost More

1979 to 1989  ·  The December intervention to the Friendship Bridge

The war had two effects and they are separable, which is what the criteria are getting at with "reflected and compounded". The direct bill was real and, by itself, not large enough to break a superpower. The signal was much larger, because the Brezhnev Doctrine was a promise, and a promise that has visibly not been kept stops deterring anybody.

What the intervention was for, and why it did not work

A communist party had taken power in Kabul in 1978 and immediately began losing control of the country, through its own factional killings and through rural revolts against land and marriage reforms imposed on communities that had not asked for them. A rising in Herat in March 1979 was put down with heavy loss of life. Soviet leaders were asked repeatedly that year to intervene and repeatedly refused, which is documented in their own meeting records. In December they changed their minds, sent forces in on 25 December 1979, killed the Afghan leader they had come to support and installed a replacement.

The military problem was the standard one. Soviet forces could hold the cities, the airfields and the main roads and could not hold the countryside, and the resistance was not one organization but many, based on region, ethnicity and religious network, which meant there was no headquarters to defeat and no leadership to negotiate a general surrender with. The mujahideen were supplied through Pakistan, largely with American and Saudi money channelled by Pakistan's intelligence service, which also decided which Afghan parties received what. From 1986 the supply included shoulder-fired anti-aircraft missiles that made low-flying helicopter operations far more dangerous.

Now measure the bill honestly, because the honesty is what makes the rest of the argument land. Roughly 15,000 Soviet soldiers were killed over nine years and tens of thousands wounded, out of something over half a million who served. That is a serious loss and it is not the kind of loss that breaks a state which had absorbed the Second World War. The financial cost was a few billion rubles a year against an economy of a very different order. Afghan losses were on another scale entirely, with estimates commonly in the range of one to two million dead, most of them civilians, and five to six million people leaving the country as refugees, mainly into Pakistan and Iran. So if you want to argue that Afghanistan helped end the Cold War, the direct cost is the weakest part of your case, and you should say so and then make the strong part.

How we know: the Politburo argued about it on the record, and the record survived

Transcripts and working notes of Soviet leadership meetings from 1979 were released after 1991 and circulated in translation by archival projects, and they show something no later memoir could establish on its own: the leadership discussing intervention in the spring, listing the reasons against it, including the certainty of international condemnation and the difficulty of the terrain, and declining. Those documents are why historians can say the December decision was a reversal rather than a plan. Handle them with two cautions. Minutes record what participants were willing to say in the room, which in that room was shaped by who was present. And the opening of these archives was uneven and partly reversed later, so the documentary base for the Soviet side of this topic is better for some years and some subjects than for others.

The signal, and one thing not to write

Gorbachev inherited the war and wanted out of it. An agreement was signed at Geneva in April 1988, withdrawal began in May, and the last Soviet units crossed the bridge at the Uzbek border on 15 February 1989, filmed doing it.

Here is the mechanism that matters. Since 1968 Soviet policy had rested on a stated commitment to intervene militarily to preserve communist government in an allied state, the doctrine that had put tanks into Prague and had stood behind the imposition of martial law in Poland in 1981. A commitment of that kind works by being believed. Afghanistan demonstrated over nine public years that Soviet forces could be fought to a standstill, and the withdrawal demonstrated that the Soviet leadership would accept a political defeat rather than keep paying. Gorbachev then said the quiet part out loud, telling the United Nations General Assembly in December 1988 that states must be free to choose, and announcing large unilateral troop reductions. Nine months later the sequence in section 04 begins.

One complication worth keeping, because it prevents a lazy sentence. The Afghan communist government did not fall when Soviet troops left. It survived until 1992, outlasting the Soviet Union itself, and collapsed when Soviet successor aid stopped. So this was a failed war in the sense that the intervention did not produce a stable friendly state at an acceptable price, and not in the sense that the army was driven out and its client immediately swept away.

Common mistake to avoid

Do not write that the United States funded Osama bin Laden, or that the CIA created Al-Qaeda. It is a widespread claim and the evidence does not support it. American money went to Afghan parties, chosen and distributed by Pakistan's intelligence service, and the Arab volunteers who came to the war were a separate and much smaller phenomenon financed largely from private Gulf sources. What the war did supply to that network is real and is narrower: a place to organize, combat experience, contacts across several countries, and a demonstration that a superpower could be fought. That is the defensible claim, it connects Topic 8.8 to the second half of the Topic 8.7 chapter, and it does not require an unsupported one.

Use this in your answer
Strongest tool, with its mechanism
A guarantee that stops being believed. The mechanism is that a commitment to intervene deters by being credible rather than by being used, so a nine-year war that ends in withdrawal removes the credibility for every other state the guarantee covered. The cost is therefore not paid where the war was fought; it is paid wherever somebody had previously been deterred, which in 1989 was Eastern Europe.
Best evidence of exclusion or limit
The direct costs were absorbable. About 15,000 dead over nine years and a few billion rubles a year did not break a state that had survived 1941 to 1945, so an answer resting on the bill rather than on the signal is arguing from the weaker half of the evidence.
Comparison hook
Against Topic 8.3 on Vietnam: both are superpower interventions defeated by a resistance supplied from outside, and the instructive difference is what each did to the intervening state. The United States absorbed its defeat and remained a superpower with its alliances intact; the Soviet withdrawal removed the credibility of the guarantee its bloc was built on. The variable is what else was holding the alliance together.

Terms to use precisely

Brezhnev Doctrine
The commitment stated after 1968 to intervene militarily to preserve communist government in an allied state, and the promise the Afghan withdrawal falsified.
Mujahideen
The plural Afghan resistance forces, organized by region, ethnicity and religious network, supplied through Pakistan with American and Saudi funding.
Operation Cyclone
The American covert program that armed the Afghan resistance through Pakistan’s intelligence service, which chose the recipients.
Signal effect
The consequence of an action for what other actors now believe, which here mattered more than the direct cost of the war.
Geneva Accords 1988
The agreement under which Soviet forces withdrew between May 1988 and 15 February 1989, leaving the Kabul government in place until 1992.
04

Nineteen Eighty-Nine, and Who Was Driving

1980 to 1991  ·  Gdansk to the dissolution of the Union

1989 is where the two halves of the explanation meet. Movements that had existed for a decade acted at the moment the guarantee of Soviet intervention was withdrawn, and the cleanest evidence that the withdrawal mattered is that the one communist government which used decisive force that year is still in power, and the cleanest evidence that force was not sufficient is that another government used it and fell within a week.

The sequence, and Gorbachev in the middle of it

Start earlier than the criteria do. In August 1980 a strike at the Lenin shipyard in Gdansk produced an agreement recognizing an independent trade union, and Solidarity grew to roughly ten million members inside a year, which in a country of about 36 million is a fact about legitimacy and not only about labor. In December 1981 the Polish government declared martial law and suspended it, under Soviet pressure and without Soviet troops, with a formal ban following the next year, and the movement survived underground for the rest of the decade.

Gorbachev became general secretary in March 1985. Glasnost, openness, was meant to expose incompetence and corruption so that reform could get past the officials it threatened, and perestroika, restructuring, was meant to make the planned economy work. Both had unintended consequences that the criteria ask you to explain, and each has a mechanism. Openness applied to the present cannot be confined to the present: once the Chernobyl reactor accident of April 1986 and the Afghan casualty lists could be discussed, so could the Stalinist terror, the annexation of the Baltic states in 1940 and the secret protocol that had arranged it. A state whose authority rested partly on its account of its own history had opened that account for examination. And restructuring broke the existing supply chains before the new ones worked, so shortages got worse while people were newly free to say so.

Then 1989, in order. Round-table talks in Poland produced partly free elections on 4 June, in which Solidarity won essentially every seat it was allowed to contest, and by late August Poland had a non-communist prime minister. Hungary had begun dismantling its border fence with Austria in May and opened the crossing to East Germans in September, which turned a hole in one border into an emigration crisis for another country.

Weekly demonstrations in Leipzig grew through the autumn, and on 9 October a crowd of tens of thousands marched and was not fired on. Honecker resigned nine days later. On 9 November a press conference misstatement about travel rules sent Berliners to the crossing points and the wall was opened. Czechoslovakia's government gave way in the weeks after 17 November, and in Romania, the exception, Ceausescu was overthrown and shot on 25 December. Germany was reunified on 3 October 1990 under a treaty signed that September by the two German states and the four wartime occupying powers.

From below or from above, the control case, and then 1991

Here is the dispute, and it is the one an examiner most wants to see handled. The from below account credits the movements: Solidarity's ten million, Charter 77, the churches, the Leipzig crowds, and the Baltic Way of 23 August 1989, when roughly two million people formed a human chain across three republics on the fiftieth anniversary of the pact that had delivered them to Moscow. The from above account replies that all of those movements, or their predecessors, had existed before and had been crushed, in East Berlin in 1953, Hungary in 1956, Czechoslovakia in 1968 and Poland in 1981, and that the variable which changed in 1989 was Gorbachev's decision not to send the army.

The control case is on the same date. On the night of 3 to 4 June 1989, while Poles were voting, the Chinese leadership used the army to clear the demonstrations in Beijing that had occupied the central square for seven weeks. Estimates of the dead range from several hundred to a few thousand and no full accounting has been published. The Communist Party of China is in power today. That pairing is the strongest single piece of evidence that the willingness to use force was decisive, and it should be stated carefully, because Romania cuts the other way: Ceausescu also ordered force, and his security forces and army split, and he was dead within a week. So the honest formulation is a combination. The movements supplied the pressure, the withdrawal of the intervention guarantee changed what local leaders calculated, and whether force was ordered and obeyed decided the rest.

Then the Union itself. Two facts have to sit together. In March 1991 a referendum in the nine republics that took part produced a large majority for preserving a renewed union, so the picture of a population unanimously demanding dissolution is wrong; six republics, including the three Baltic states, refused to hold it at all, which tells you where the pressure was concentrated.

Then in August 1991 a group of senior officials attempted a coup against Gorbachev to stop a new union treaty. It collapsed in three days against public resistance in Moscow and the refusal of key units to act, and its failure destroyed what was left of the central institutions, since the people who had run them had just tried to seize power. Ukraine voted overwhelmingly for independence on 1 December, the leaders of Russia, Ukraine and Belarus declared the Union at an end on 8 December, most of the remaining republics joined that agreement on 21 December, and Gorbachev resigned on 25 December 1991.

Close the volume's argument here. The Topic 8.1 chapter said a rivalry that cannot be settled where it starts is conducted where the outcome is still open. This is where it came home: the confrontation ended at its origin, in Europe, and it ended by one participant losing the capacity to hold its own arrangement together rather than by any settlement of the line. What it did not do was end the wars it had paid for. Angola's fighting continued into the next century, Afghanistan's continued after both patrons stopped paying, and Cuba lost the Soviet subsidy that had sustained its economy and entered years of severe shortage. That is the sentence that hands the rest to Unit 9.

Common mistake to avoid

Two claims to avoid, both common and both checkable. The first is that everyone in the Soviet Union wanted it dissolved: the March 1991 referendum returned a large majority for a renewed union in the nine republics that voted, and the strongest independence movements were concentrated in the republics that refused to hold it. Write about which populations, where, and when. The second is writing as though the collapse was visible in advance. It was not, by either side's analysts, which is why the interesting question for a historian is not why people failed to see it but which of the causes in this chapter were legible at the time and which became legible only afterward. That distinction is the difference between explaining an event and narrating it backwards from the ending.

Use this in your answer
Strongest tool, with its mechanism
A permissive cause and a mobilizing cause together. The mechanism is that mass movements supplied the pressure and had done so for decades, while the removal of the Soviet intervention guarantee changed what local party leaderships expected to happen if they refused to concede, so the same pressure that produced tanks in 1968 produced a round table in 1989. Neither half explains the year on its own.
Best evidence of exclusion or limit
The permissive cause is not sufficient either, because whether force was ordered and obeyed still decided outcomes. Beijing on 4 June 1989 and Bucharest on 25 December 1989 are the two ends of that, and any general claim has to survive both.
Comparison hook
Against Topic 8.5 on decolonization: the same combination reasoning, in a different setting. There, organized nationalism raised the cost, metropolitan exhaustion lowered the tolerance for cost, and a changed international system removed the diplomatic cover for reconquest. Here, organized opposition raised the cost, economic failure lowered the tolerance, and the abandoned guarantee removed the military cover. If you can write one of those sentences you can write the other.

Terms to use precisely

Solidarity
The Polish independent trade union recognized in 1980 with about ten million members, banned under martial law in 1981, and legalized again in 1989.
Glasnost
Openness, the relaxation of censorship intended to expose obstruction to reform, which could not be confined to the present once it began.
Perestroika
Restructuring, the attempt to make the planned economy work, which disrupted existing supply relationships before replacements existed.
Permissive cause
A condition whose removal allows an outcome without producing it, here the withdrawal of the guarantee of Soviet military intervention.
August coup
The failed 1991 attempt by senior officials to seize power and stop a new union treaty, whose collapse destroyed the central institutions it was meant to save.

Building an Answer That Scores

The three criteria are three causes, and the first three cards are them, each with the mechanism attached and each honest about its own weakest point. The fourth is the year everything arrives, with the dispute named.

Economy

The Soviet system was good at one kind of growth and it ran out of it

Planning after 1928 produced extensive growth, more workers, more coalfields, more steel toward measured targets, and it won a war doing so. Intensive growth is a different problem and the system was built against it: an enterprise that cannot go bankrupt has no reason to economize, a manager who beats a target gets a higher target next year and so hides capacity, and administered prices carry no information about scarcity, so a planner cannot tell an efficient factory from a wasteful one. By the 1970s labor growth had slowed and the accessible resources were gone. What postponed the reckoning was oil: West Siberian production plus the price rises of 1973 and 1979 bought imported grain and Western machinery for a decade and a half, and the price fell by roughly half in 1986, in Gorbachev's second year. Attribute the numbers: Soviet defense burden estimates run from an official five or six percent to American intelligence figures of fifteen to seventeen.

Technology

The arms race moved into a sector, and the sector was the point

American defense spending roughly doubled in nominal dollars in the early 1980s, and the mechanism is not the total. The money went into guidance, sensors, communications and computing, and a command economy that could mass-produce standardized hardware performed badly there for reasons built into how it controlled information: copiers logged, foreign contact supervised, general-purpose computers scarce, and the main mainframe series based since the late 1960s on a copy of an American architecture, which guarantees a lag. SDI, announced 23 March 1983 and never deployed, demanded exactly that kind of investment on somebody else's timetable. Then name the dispute, because it is real: one account credits the pressure, another holds the system was failing anyway and the buildup changed timing at most, and a third gives independent weight to Gorbachev's own beliefs. Several post-Soviet accounts find Soviet military spending did not surge in response, which is evidence the strongest pressure claim has to answer.

Causation

Afghanistan cost less than people think and signaled more

Soviet forces entered on 25 December 1979 after the leadership had refused intervention repeatedly that year, which their own meeting records show. Nine years later, roughly 15,000 Soviet dead and a few billion rubles a year is a serious loss and not one that breaks a state which absorbed 1941 to 1945; Afghan losses were on a different scale, with estimates commonly of one to two million dead and five to six million refugees. So make the signal argument instead. Since 1968 Soviet policy had rested on a commitment to intervene militarily to save an allied communist government, and a commitment of that kind deters by being believed. The withdrawal completed on 15 February 1989 showed the leadership would accept political defeat rather than keep paying, and Gorbachev told the United Nations in December 1988 that states must be free to choose. Nine months later, Eastern Europe tested it.

Comparison

1989 needs both halves, and Beijing and Bucharest are why

From below: Solidarity's ten million members from 1980, Charter 77, the churches, the Leipzig marches, and roughly two million people in the Baltic Way on 23 August 1989. From above: those movements or their predecessors had been crushed in 1953, 1956, 1968 and 1981, and what changed was the decision not to send the army. The control case is on the same date, since Poland voted on 4 June 1989 and the Chinese leadership cleared Beijing on the night of 3 to 4 June and remains in power. But Romania cuts the other way, because Ceausescu also ordered force and was dead by 25 December. So the defensible claim is a combination: movements supplied the pressure, the withdrawn guarantee changed what party leaderships calculated, and whether force was ordered and obeyed decided the rest. Then 1991: a March referendum for a renewed union in the nine republics that voted, a failed August coup that destroyed the center, and dissolution by December.
