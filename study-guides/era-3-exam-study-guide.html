BeHistorical Study System

Study for explanation, not recognition.

01 · KNOW IT

Name the evidence.

For every topic, be able to identify at least 2–3 specific historical examples without looking them up.

02 · EXPLAIN IT

Connect evidence to process.

Be able to explain how the evidence demonstrates expansion, legitimacy, exchange, labor systems, continuity/change, or causation.

03 · COMPARE IT

Know what makes it different.

Strong answers identify a meaningful similarity or difference and then explain why that difference existed.

When You See a Stimulus
WHO / WHAT?Identify the person, empire, institution, map, image, or trade route represented.
WHEN / WHERE?Use the time period and geography to eliminate answers that do not fit the historical context.
WHAT PROCESS?Ask whether the evidence is about expansion, administration, belief, exchange, labor, or social hierarchy.
SO WHAT?Connect the evidence to cause/effect, continuity/change, or comparison before choosing an answer.

Unit 3 · Topics 3.1 to 3.4

Land-Based Empires

c. 1450 to c. 1750

How did rulers expand, hold, pay for, and justify their empires? Topic 3.4 asks you to compare the job, not the empire.

Topic 3.1

Empires Expand

Know
  • Gunpowder, cannons, and armed trade as tools of imperial expansion
  • The land empires: the Manchu (Qing) in Central and East Asia, the Mughal in South and Central Asia, the Ottoman in Southern Europe, the Middle East, and North Africa, and the Safavid in the Middle East
  • The Ottoman conquest of Constantinople (1453) and Babur’s founding of the Mughal Empire
  • Rivalries driven by political and religious disputes, such as the Safavid-Mughal conflict and Morocco’s conflict with the Songhai Empire
Be able to explain
  • How gunpowder weapons gave empires an advantage over rivals and fortified cities
  • How and why land-based empires developed and expanded from 1450 to 1750
  • How political and religious disputes led to rivalries and conflict between states
Evidence anchors: gunpowder · cannons · fall of Constantinople · janissaries · Babur · Manchu · Safavid-Mughal conflict · Songhai and Morocco
Topic 3.2

Empires: Administration

Know
  • Bureaucratic elites and military professionals, such as the Ottoman devshirme and salaried samurai
  • Religious ideas used to legitimize rule, such as European divine right and Songhai promotion of Islam
  • Art and monumental architecture, such as Mughal mausolea and mosques, Qing imperial portraits, and Versailles
  • Revenue systems: Ottoman tax farming, Mughal zamindar tax collection, Mexica tribute lists, and Ming taxes collected in hard currency
Be able to explain
  • Why rulers recruited officials and soldiers whose loyalty depended on the ruler
  • How religion, art, and buildings made a ruler’s authority look legitimate
  • How tribute and tax systems turned local wealth into state power and paid for expansion
Evidence anchors: devshirme · salaried samurai · divine right · Taj Mahal · Versailles · Qing imperial portraits · tax farming · zamindars · Mexica tribute
Topic 3.3

Empires: Belief Systems

Know
  • Martin Luther’s challenge to Church authority and the Protestant Reformation as a break with existing Christian traditions
  • The Catholic Reformation, including the Council of Trent and the Jesuits
  • The Ottoman-Safavid rivalry, the Battle of Chaldiran (1514), and the split between Sunni and Shi’a
  • Guru Nanak and the development of Sikhism in South Asia
Be able to explain
  • How the Protestant and Catholic reformations both contributed to the growth of Christianity
  • How political rivalry between the Ottoman and Safavid empires intensified the split within Islam
  • How Sikhism developed in a context of interactions between Hinduism and Islam
  • What changed and what continued within each belief system
Evidence anchors: Martin Luther · Protestant Reformation · Council of Trent · Jesuits · Battle of Chaldiran · Sunni and Shi’a · Guru Nanak · Sikhism
Topic 3.4

Comparison in Land-Based Empires

You should be ready to compare
  • Expand: how the Ottomans and Mughals used gunpowder armies to take and widen territory
  • Hold: the Ottoman devshirme and the Mughal mansabdars as two ways to build loyal officials and soldiers
  • Pay: Ottoman tax farming and Mughal zamindar land-revenue collection as two ways to turn local wealth into state power
  • Justify: Mughal monumental architecture and Qing imperial portraits as two ways to make authority visible
  • Qualify it: how the Ottoman-Safavid rivalry shaped imperial influence while deepening an older religious divide
Evidence anchors: expand · hold · pay · justify · gunpowder · devshirme · mansabdars · tax farming · zamindars · monumental architecture

Unit 4 · Topics 4.1 to 4.8

Transoceanic Interconnections

c. 1450 to c. 1750

The oceans were joined, and the new trade reached into people’s lives. Topic 4.8 asks you to follow the trade to the people.

Topic 4.1

Technological Innovations

Know
  • Knowledge borrowed from the Classical, Islamic, and Asian worlds, including the compass, the lateen sail, the astrolabe, and astronomical charts
  • New ship designs: the caravel, the carrack, and the fluyt
  • An improved understanding of regional wind and current patterns, such as the Portuguese volta do mar
Be able to explain
  • How cross-cultural interactions spread the knowledge and technology behind European ocean voyages
  • What problem each tool or ship design solved at sea
  • Why new tools, ship designs, and knowledge of winds and currents together made transoceanic travel and trade possible
Evidence anchors: compass · lateen sail · astrolabe · astronomical charts · caravel · carrack · fluyt · volta do mar
Topic 4.2

Exploration: Causes and Events

Know
  • State-supported voyages: rulers supplied ships, permission to claim land, titles, or a share of the profits
  • Portuguese voyages around Africa to Asia, such as Vasco da Gama’s, and the Portuguese trading-post empire
  • Spanish sponsorship of Columbus and later voyages across the Atlantic and Pacific
  • English, French, and Dutch crossings of the northern Atlantic, often in search of another route to Asia
Be able to explain
  • Why states backed maritime exploration, including the hope of cutting out the middlemen in the spice trade
  • How Portuguese maritime technology and navigation led to trade with Africa and Asia
  • How Spanish-sponsored voyages increased European interest in transoceanic travel and trade
  • Why Portugal’s and Spain’s hold on the southern routes pushed England, France, and the Dutch north
Evidence anchors: state sponsorship · spice trade · Vasco da Gama · trading-post empire · Columbus · Magellan · Cabot · Cartier · Hudson
Topic 4.3

Columbian Exchange

Know
  • The cause: new connections between the Eastern and Western Hemispheres
  • Diseases such as smallpox, measles, and malaria, and disease vectors such as mosquitoes and rats
  • American foods such as maize, potatoes, and cassava becoming staple crops in Europe, Asia, and Africa
  • Afro-Eurasian animals, grains, fruit trees, and sugar carried to the Americas, and foods such as okra and rice brought by enslaved Africans
  • Cash crops grown on plantations with coerced labor and exported mostly to Europe and the Middle East
Be able to explain
  • What crossed the ocean on purpose and what crossed by accident
  • Why diseases were so deadly in the Americas, including the effects of war, forced labor, and hunger
  • How American food crops improved nutrition for populations in Afro-Eurasia
  • How cash crops tied the exchange to plantations and coerced labor
Evidence anchors: smallpox · mosquitoes and rats · maize · potatoes · cassava · horses, pigs, and cattle · sugar · okra and rice
Topic 4.4

Maritime Empires Established

Know
  • European trading posts in Africa and Asia, such as Goa and Malacca, and the Portuguese cartaz
  • New maritime empires (Portuguese, Spanish, Dutch, French, and British) driven by political, religious, and economic rivalries
  • Ming China and Tokugawa Japan restricting trade with Europeans; Asante and Kongo gaining influence through trade
  • Asian merchants, such as Gujaratis, Swahili Arabs, Omanis, and Javanese, keeping Indian Ocean trade going
  • Colonial labor: the Inca mit’a put to new work, plus encomienda, hacienda, indentured servitude, and chattel slavery
  • Slavery in Africa continuing in traditional forms, while plantations increased the demand for enslaved labor in the Americas
Be able to explain
  • How trading posts made profits for rulers and merchants, and why some Asian states limited European trade
  • How taking part in maritime trade increased the influence of states such as Asante and Kongo
  • What continued and what changed in Indian Ocean trade after the Portuguese arrived
  • Which colonial labor systems were older systems in a new form and which were new
  • How the plantation economy changed slavery in the Americas, with major demographic, social, and cultural effects
Evidence anchors: trading posts · cartaz · Ming and Tokugawa trade limits · Asante · Kongo · Gujarati merchants · mit’a · encomienda · hacienda · chattel slavery
Topic 4.5

Maritime Empires Maintained and Developed

Know
  • Mercantilism and joint-stock companies, such as the Dutch VOC and the English East India Company
  • Economic disputes that led to rivalry and war, such as the Anglo-Dutch Wars
  • The Atlantic trading system, which moved goods, wealth, and enslaved people
  • Silver from Spanish America, such as Potosí, used to buy Asian goods and to meet Chinese demand for silver
  • Peasant and artisan labor intensifying: wool and linen in western Europe, cotton in India, and silk in China
  • Changes in African families caused by the slave trade, and cultural and religious mixing in the Atlantic world, such as Vodou
Be able to explain
  • How rulers used mercantilism and joint-stock companies to make empires pay and to compete with rivals
  • How the global flow of silver connected the Americas, Europe, and Asia
  • What changed and what continued in networks of exchange
  • How the trade in enslaved people changed families in parts of Africa
  • How Africans, Indigenous Americans, and Europeans all contributed to cultural mixing in the Atlantic world
  • How religions spread, clashed, or blended as the hemispheres were connected
Evidence anchors: mercantilism · joint-stock company · VOC · Anglo-Dutch Wars · Atlantic trading system · Potosí silver · peasant and artisan labor · syncretism · Vodou
Topic 4.6

Internal and External Challenges to State Power

Know
  • State expansion and centralization producing resistance from local social, political, and economic groups
  • Cases from Africa, Asia, and Europe: the Fronde, Razin’s revolt, the Marathas against the Mughals, and Njinga of Ndongo and Matamba
  • Cases from the Americas: the Pueblo Revolt and Metacom’s War
  • Organized resistance by enslaved people: maroon communities such as Palmares and the Jamaica Maroons, and revolts such as the Stono Rebellion
Be able to explain
  • What a state pushed for, which group pushed back, and how
  • What a case of resistance changed, and one way two cases were alike
  • How enslaved people challenged authorities in the Americas, and why authorities responded differently to different challenges
Evidence anchors: Fronde · Razin’s revolt · Marathas · Njinga · Pueblo Revolt · Metacom’s War · Palmares · Jamaica Maroons · Stono Rebellion
Topic 4.7

Changing Social Hierarchies

Know
  • States that made room for or made use of diverse groups, such as the Ottoman Empire and Akbar’s Mughal Empire
  • States that pushed out or limited groups, such as Spain’s expulsion of Jews and Qing limits on Han Chinese
  • New elites formed by conquest and trade: the Qing banners, and the peninsulares and creoles at the top of the casta system
  • Old elites whose power rose and fell with stronger rulers: Ottoman sipahis who held timars, Russian boyars, and European nobles
Be able to explain
  • Why a state made room for or limited a group, and what the state gained
  • How imperial conquest and new economic opportunities created new elites
  • What older elites lost and kept as monarchs grew more powerful
  • What stayed the same at the top of society
Evidence anchors: Akbar · jizya · expulsion of Jews from Spain · Qing banners · casta system · peninsulares · creoles · timars · boyars

Must-Master Cross-Topic Comparisons

Answer each prompt aloud or on paper before you check your notes.

ComparisonYour task
Ottoman vs. MughalBoth empires needed loyal officials and steady money. Compare how each one found the people who ran the empire and how each collected taxes. Why could different tools do the same job?
The Reformations vs. the Sunni-Shi’a splitBoth divided an existing religion. What caused each split, and what part did rulers and rivalries play? What stayed the same in each faith?
Portuguese vs. Spanish empiresPortugal built trading posts, and Spain conquered land. Compare what each empire wanted, how it got it, and who did the work.
Slavery in Africa vs. slavery in the AmericasSlavery existed in Africa before 1450. How was Atlantic plantation slavery different, and what changed in Africa because of it?
Ming China and Tokugawa Japan vs. Asante and KongoAll four met European traders. Why did some states limit contact while others joined the trade, and what did each choice bring them?
