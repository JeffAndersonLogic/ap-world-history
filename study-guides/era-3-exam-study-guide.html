Empires of Land & Sea

Study Guide · c. 1450–1750 · AP Units 3–4

How to use this guide: Do not study this era as a list of empires. The exam emphasizes causation, continuity and change, and comparison: how empires grew, how rulers held and paid for power, what joining the hemispheres changed, and what it did not change. For every topic below, be able to explain why a development mattered and connect evidence to a broader historical claim.
How the Era 3 Exam Works
Expect to reason, not just recognize. Most questions begin with a source: a document excerpt, a description of an image, a map, or a table of numbers. Several questions share each source. Many answer choices will be true statements about this era that do not answer the question being asked. The right answer is the one that explains the source and fits its time and place. Practice by reading the source first, saying in your own words what it shows, and only then reading the choices.

BeHistorical Study System

Study for explanation, not recognition.

01 · KNOW IT

Name the evidence.

For every topic, be able to identify at least 2–3 specific historical examples without looking them up.

02 · EXPLAIN IT

Connect evidence to process.

Be able to explain how the evidence shows expansion, legitimacy, revenue, exchange, labor, resistance, or continuity and change.

03 · COMPARE IT

Know what makes it different.

Strong answers identify a meaningful similarity or difference and then explain why that difference existed.

When You See a Stimulus
WHO / WHAT?Identify the person, empire, institution, map, image, or table represented, and who made the source.
WHEN / WHERE?Use the date and place to eliminate answers that belong to another region or another era.
WHAT PROCESS?Ask whether the evidence is about expansion, administration, belief, exchange, labor, hierarchy, or resistance.
SO WHAT?Connect the evidence to cause and effect, continuity and change, or comparison before choosing an answer.
Common Traps · Answer in Your Own Words
Gunpowder & empire
Did gunpowder create empires, or did it help big states get bigger? Why could only a large state that collected taxes afford cannons and trained gunners?
1453 & exploration
Did the fall of Constantinople cause European exploration? What were the stronger reasons rulers paid for ocean voyages?
Legitimacy vs. belief change
A ruler building a great mosque and a religion splitting in two are different stories. Which one is about holding power, and which is about religion itself changing?
Disease & slavery
Is "disease killed Indigenous workers, so Europeans enslaved Africans" a complete explanation? What else drove the demand for enslaved labor?
The old networks
Did Europeans replace Indian Ocean trade after 1498? What evidence shows Asian merchants kept trading and profiting?
Slavery, old and new
How was slavery inside Africa different from chattel slavery on American plantations? Which one continued older practice?
Silver
Why did so much silver mined in Spanish America end up in China? What did Chinese demand have to do with it?
Accommodation & equality
The Ottomans let Jews and Christians keep their own religion and courts. Did that make them equal to Muslims? What did the state gain?

Unit 3 · Topics 3.1 to 3.4

Land-Based Empires

c. 1450 to c. 1750 · 25 questions

Gunpowder won the land. Holding it was the hard part. Every empire had the same jobs (expand, hold, pay, and justify), and the exam asks how each one did them.

On this exam50 pts
Topic 3.1

Empires Expand

Know
  • Gunpowder weapons, cannons, and armed trade as tools of imperial expansion
  • The four land empires the College Board names: Ottoman, Safavid, Mughal, and Manchu (Qing), and the regions each ruled
  • The Ottoman siege of Constantinople in 1453 and what artillery did to its walls
  • Rivalries from political and religious disputes: the Safavid and Mughal fight over Kandahar, and Morocco's defeat of Songhai at Tondibi in 1591
Be able to explain
  • How gunpowder weapons helped land-based empires conquer territory and defeat rivals
  • Why only large states that collected taxes could afford cannons and trained gunners, so big states tended to get bigger
  • How political and religious disputes led to rivalries and wars between states
  • Why Tondibi shows gunpowder deciding a battle between two states, not only a siege
Evidence anchors: gunpowder · cannons · Constantinople 1453 · Ottoman · Safavid · Mughal · Manchu (Qing) · Kandahar · Tondibi 1591
Topic 3.2

Empires: Administration

Know
  • People who serve: the Ottoman devshirme and Janissaries, Mughal mansabdars, and salaried samurai in Tokugawa Japan
  • Symbols that justify: divine right in Europe, Versailles, Qing imperial portraits, Mughal mausolea and mosques, the Inca sun temple at Cuzco, and Songhai promotion of Islam
  • Systems that pay: Ottoman tax farming, Mughal zamindar tax collection, Mexica tribute lists, and Ming taxes collected in silver
  • The Ottoman millet system as a way to govern diverse religious communities
Be able to explain
  • How rulers used bureaucratic elites and military professionals to keep centralized control
  • How religious ideas, art, and monumental architecture made a ruler's power look rightful
  • How tribute, tax farming, and new tax systems paid for state power and expansion
  • How two empires solved the same problem of holding conquered land in different ways
Evidence anchors: devshirme · Janissaries · mansabdars · salaried samurai · divine right · Versailles · Taj Mahal · tax farming · zamindars · tribute · silver taxes
Topic 3.3

Empires: Belief Systems

Know
  • Martin Luther, the Ninety-Five Theses (1517), and the Protestant Reformation's break with the Catholic Church
  • The Catholic Reformation: the Council of Trent, the Jesuits, and missionary work
  • The Sunni Ottomans and the Shi'a Safavids, Shah Ismail making Shi'a Islam the state religion, and the Battle of Chaldiran (1514)
  • Guru Nanak and the growth of Sikhism in South Asia
Be able to explain
  • Why the Protestant Reformation was a break with existing Christian traditions, and how both reformations contributed to the growth of Christianity
  • How political rivalry between the Ottoman and Safavid empires intensified the split between Sunni and Shi'a Islam
  • How Sikhism developed in a context of interaction between Hinduism and Islam
  • For each case, what continued (the core faith) and what changed (a split, a reform, or a new tradition)
Evidence anchors: Luther · Ninety-Five Theses · indulgences · Council of Trent · Jesuits · Sunni · Shi'a · Shah Ismail · Chaldiran 1514 · Guru Nanak · Sikhism
Topic 3.4

Comparison in Land-Based Empires

You should be ready to compare
  • Devshirme vs. mansabdars: two ways to build a service elite loyal to the ruler rather than to a family or region
  • Ottoman vs. Mughal religious policy: how each empire governed subjects who did not share the ruler's faith, and how policy could change from one ruler to the next
  • Qing banners vs. Ottoman Janissaries: two professional armies tied directly to the dynasty
  • Divine right vs. Islamic legitimacy: how European kings and rulers such as the Mughals or Songhai used religion to justify their power
  • Gunpowder conquest: the Ottomans at Constantinople and Morocco at Tondibi as two cases of firearms deciding who ruled
Evidence anchors: expansion · service elites · legitimacy · revenue · religious policy · rivalry · Empire A and Empire B increased influence by ___, but differed because ___.

Unit 4 · Topics 4.1 to 4.8

Transoceanic Interconnections

c. 1450 to c. 1750 · 25 questions

The ocean became a road. What traveled it (crops, germs, silver, and enslaved people) changed everyone at both ends. But the old world did not stop: much of it kept its own rules.

On this exam50 pts
Topic 4.1

Technological Innovations

Know
  • Tools borrowed from older worlds: the magnetic compass, the astrolabe, astronomical charts, and the lateen sail
  • New ship designs: the caravel, the carrack, and the Dutch fluyt
  • Better knowledge of Atlantic winds and currents, including the volta do mar, the wide loop that brought ships home
Be able to explain
  • How knowledge and technology from the Classical, Islamic, and Asian worlds spread and made European innovation possible
  • How new ships and wind knowledge made a return voyage across an ocean possible
  • How these tools together changed patterns of trade and travel
Evidence anchors: compass · astrolabe · astronomical charts · lateen sail · caravel · carrack · fluyt · volta do mar
Topic 4.2

Exploration: Causes and Events

Know
  • State sponsorship: rulers paid for voyages hoping for trade, wealth, and power, especially direct access to Asian spices
  • Portugal: Prince Henry's sponsored voyages, Dias rounding the tip of Africa (1488), da Gama reaching India (1498), and a trading-post empire
  • Spain: sponsorship of Columbus (1492) and the Atlantic and Pacific voyages that followed, including Magellan's fleet, completed by Elcano
  • Northern crossings sponsored by England, France, and the Dutch (Cabot, Cartier, Hudson) looking for another sea route to Asia
Be able to explain
  • The role of states in the expansion of maritime exploration
  • The economic causes of exploration and its economic effects, such as Portugal's trading-post empire
  • Why the northern crossings were attempts to find alternative routes to Asia
  • Why the fall of Constantinople was at most background, not the direct cause, of European exploration
Evidence anchors: state sponsorship · spices · Prince Henry · Dias · da Gama · Columbus · Magellan and Elcano · Cabot · Cartier · Hudson · trading-post empire
Topic 4.3

Columbian Exchange

Know
  • Diseases from the Eastern Hemisphere (smallpox, measles, malaria) and disease vectors (mosquitoes, rats), and the catastrophic decline of many Indigenous populations
  • American foods (maize, potatoes, cassava) that became staple crops in Europe, Asia, and Africa
  • Cash crops such as sugar grown on plantations with coerced labor and exported mostly to Europe and the Middle East
  • Afro-Eurasian grains, fruit trees, sugar, and animals (horses, pigs, cattle) brought to the Americas, and okra and rice brought by enslaved Africans
Be able to explain
  • How new connections between the hemispheres caused the exchange of plants, animals, and diseases
  • Why the transfer of disease was unintentional, and why its effects were so catastrophic in many areas
  • How the increased variety of American food crops benefited Afro-Eurasian populations nutritionally
  • Why "disease emptied the Americas, so Europeans enslaved Africans" is an incomplete explanation
Evidence anchors: smallpox · measles · malaria · mosquitoes · maize · potatoes · cassava · sugar · horses · cattle · okra · rice
Topic 4.4

Maritime Empires Established

Know
  • European trading posts in Africa and Asia, such as Elmina, Goa, and Malacca, and the new Portuguese, Spanish, Dutch, French, and British maritime empires
  • Asian states that restricted European trade: Ming China and Tokugawa Japan (the Dutch confined to Dejima)
  • African states that grew through the trade: Asante and the Kingdom of Kongo
  • Indian Ocean trade that kept flourishing with Asian merchants, such as Gujaratis, Omanis, Swahili Arabs, and Javanese
  • Colonial labor: the existing Inca mit'a, and new systems of chattel slavery, indentured servitude, encomienda, and hacienda
  • Slavery in Africa continuing in older forms, and plantations raising demand for enslaved labor in the Americas
Be able to explain
  • How political, religious, and economic rivalries drove European states to build maritime empires
  • Why some Asian states adopted restrictive trade policies, and how Asante and Kongo gained influence through trade
  • What continued and what changed in Indian Ocean trade after Europeans arrived
  • What continued and what changed in labor systems and in systems of slavery
Evidence anchors: trading posts · Elmina · Ming · Tokugawa · Dejima · Asante · Kongo · Gujarati merchants · mit'a · encomienda · hacienda · indentured servitude · chattel slavery
Topic 4.5

Maritime Empires Maintained and Developed

Know
  • Mercantilism: rulers built national wealth by exporting more than they imported and using colonies for raw materials and markets
  • Joint-stock companies such as the Dutch East India Company (VOC) and the English East India Company
  • Economic disputes that became rivalries: Muslim and European rivalry in the Indian Ocean, and Morocco's war with Songhai over the gold trade
  • The Atlantic trading system, and the global flow of silver from Potosí and Mexico to China, including through Manila
  • More peasant and artisan labor: wool and linen in western Europe, cotton in India, and silk in China
  • Gender and family change in Africa from the slave trade, cultural mixing in the Atlantic world, and religions that spread, clashed, and blended (the Virgin of Guadalupe)
Be able to explain
  • How rulers used mercantilism and joint-stock companies to consolidate power and compete in global trade
  • Why so much American silver ended up in China, and what that shows about continuity and change in networks of exchange
  • How growing demand for goods intensified peasant and artisan labor
  • How the Atlantic slave trade changed gender balance and family life in parts of Africa
  • How belief systems spread, caused conflict, or blended when hemispheres connected
Evidence anchors: mercantilism · joint-stock companies · VOC · Potosí · Manila galleons · Chinese demand for silver · cotton · silk · cultural synthesis · syncretism · Virgin of Guadalupe
Topic 4.6

Internal and External Challenges to State Power

Know
  • Local resistance to centralizing states: the Fronde in France, Cossack revolts in Russia (Stenka Razin), the Pueblo Revolt of 1680, and Metacom's War
  • Challenges from peoples empires tried to conquer: the Marathas against the Mughals, and Njinga (Ana Nzinga) of Ndongo and Matamba against Portugal
  • Organized resistance by enslaved people: Palmares in Brazil, the Maroons of Jamaica, and the Stono Rebellion (1739)
Be able to explain
  • How state expansion and centralization led to resistance from social, political, and economic groups at the local level
  • How enslaved people challenged authorities in the Americas through organized resistance
  • Why resistance grew in the same era that states were becoming stronger
  • How an Afro-Eurasian case and an American case of resistance were similar and different
Evidence anchors: Fronde · Cossacks · Stenka Razin · Pueblo Revolt · Popé · Metacom's War · Marathas · Njinga · Palmares · Maroons · Stono Rebellion
Topic 4.7

Changing Social Hierarchies

Know
  • Accommodation: Ottoman acceptance of Jews expelled from Spain (1492), communities that ran their own affairs and paid the jizya, and Akbar's end of the jizya and use of Rajput commanders
  • Suppression: the expulsion of Jews from Spain and Portugal, Aurangzeb's return of the jizya (1679), and Qing restrictions on Han Chinese, such as the queue
  • New elites: the Manchu rulers of the Qing, and the casta system in Spanish America
  • Existing elites under pressure from stronger rulers: Ottoman timar holders, Russian boyars under Peter the Great, and the French nobility at Versailles
  • The varying status of different classes of women within the Ottoman Empire
Be able to explain
  • Why some states accommodated ethnic and religious diversity while others suppressed it or limited certain groups
  • How conquest and new economic opportunities created new political and economic elites
  • How the power of existing elites rose or fell as monarchs grew more powerful
  • Why accommodation was not the same as equality
Evidence anchors: jizya · Rajputs · Jewish refugees · Qing queue · Manchu elite · casta system · peninsulares · creoles · mestizos · timars · boyars · Table of Ranks · Versailles
Topic 4.8

Continuity and Change from 1450 to 1750

Know
  • Rising global demand for raw materials and finished goods increased the demand for labor
  • Plantations expanded, and the Atlantic slave trade developed and intensified
  • Most economies stayed centered on agriculture, and traditional peasant agriculture increased and changed
  • Empires grew, shaping and being shaped by the diverse peoples they ruled
Be able to explain
  • How economic developments from 1450 to 1750 affected social structures over time
  • Where economic change remade society most, such as plantation colonies, and where older structures adapted and lasted
  • How to support a claim about how much changed with evidence of both change and continuity

Must-Master Cross-Topic Comparisons

Answer each prompt aloud or on paper before you check your notes.

ComparisonYour task
Ottoman vs. Mughal religious policyCompare how each empire governed subjects who did not share the ruler's religion. What did the rulers gain, and how could the policy change over time?
