# Module 05 — AP Skill Builder Scaffolding Standard

## Purpose

Module 05 exists to teach students **how to perform an AP historical thinking move**, not simply to give them another content question. A student should be able to open the module independently and answer four questions:

1. What skill am I practicing?
2. What does that skill require me to do?
3. What evidence can I use?
4. What should a successful response accomplish?

The module should scaffold the reasoning without turning the response into a fill-in-the-blank exercise.

## Audit criteria

Every Module 05 should be reviewed for the following ten qualities.

1. **Skill clarity** — Names the historical thinking skill and explains it in student-friendly language.
2. **Task decomposition** — Breaks the skill into a small number of visible reasoning moves.
3. **Evidence accessibility** — Gives students access to appropriate evidence through the lesson, a short evidence menu, a comparison matrix, or clearly named cases when retrieval load would otherwise interfere with skill practice.
4. **Decision support** — Helps students make the hidden decisions required by the skill: what to compare, which category to hold constant, which cause to trace, which baseline to use, or which evidence supports a claim.
5. **AP-authentic reasoning** — Requires explanation of how, why, significance, change, continuity, relationship, or qualification rather than fact listing.
6. **Response-construction support** — Provides a response pattern or sentence frame when students are still learning the move; removes that support as the course progresses.
7. **Independent usability** — A student who missed the teacher's verbal directions should still understand what to do.
8. **Cognitive-load control** — Adds support only when it makes the thinking clearer. More reading is not automatically better scaffolding.
9. **Topic-specific alignment** — Uses the lesson's actual learning objective, cases, and evidence rather than a generic skill worksheet.
10. **Progressive release** — Support deliberately fades as students become more experienced historical thinkers.

## Progressive release by course stage

### Foundations and Units 1–2 — High support

Students are learning the moves for the first time. Module 05 may include:

- explicit numbered steps;
- small evidence banks;
- comparison/category matrices;
- examples of appropriate evidence choices;
- causal or CCOT connectors;
- response frames;
- explicit warnings about common errors such as listing facts, comparing different categories, or confusing context with the thesis.

The purpose is to reduce **extraneous retrieval and decision load** so students can practice the historical reasoning itself.

### Units 3–5 — Moderate support

Students should receive the reasoning architecture but make more decisions themselves. A typical Module 05 should:

- name the skill;
- provide 3–4 reasoning moves;
- identify useful cases or categories without supplying a complete answer bank;
- require students to select and explain their own evidence;
- use sentence frames only when a new or unusually difficult move is being introduced;
- increasingly require qualification, significance, or mechanism.

### Units 6–9 — Increasing independence

Students should be approaching exam-ready reasoning. Module 05 should usually provide:

- the historical objective or question;
- a concise reasoning sequence such as claim → evidence mechanism → qualification;
- named cases already learned in the lesson;
- an expectation that students choose, organize, weigh, and qualify evidence themselves.

Do **not** add Unit 1-style tables, evidence menus, or sentence frames simply for consistency. At this stage, independent evidence selection and organization are part of the skill being assessed.

## Skill-specific minimums

### Contextualization

A strong scaffold teaches students to:

1. identify a broader historical condition that predates or surrounds the topic;
2. explain that condition rather than merely name it;
3. bridge the broader setting to the specific development;
4. keep contextualization distinct from the thesis or main evidence.

Early-course support may include a short menu of broader settings and a bridge frame. Later-course modules should expect students to generate the context themselves.

### Causation

A strong scaffold teaches students to:

1. identify a specific cause or effect;
2. explain the **mechanism** connecting cause to outcome;
3. use specific historical evidence;
4. distinguish background/structural causes from immediate triggers when appropriate;
5. weigh relative significance or interaction among causes as students advance.

Avoid prompts that allow `A caused B` without explaining how.

### Comparison

A strong scaffold teaches students to:

1. choose two historical cases;
2. hold one analytical category constant across both cases;
3. provide balanced evidence from both sides;
4. state a meaningful similarity or difference;
5. explain why the pattern existed or why it mattered.

Early-course comparison matrices are appropriate. Later-course students should create the categories and evidence pairings themselves.

### Continuity and Change Over Time (CCOT)

A strong scaffold teaches students to:

1. establish a baseline;
2. track the same category across the time period;
3. identify a meaningful continuity;
4. identify a meaningful change;
5. explain why the change occurred or why the continuity persisted.

Avoid treating `before / during / after` as sufficient unless the student explicitly identifies and explains continuity/change.

### Claims and Evidence

A strong scaffold teaches students to:

1. state or identify the claim;
2. select a precise source detail or historical fact;
3. explain how that detail supports the claim;
4. distinguish evidence use from description.

The key reasoning sentence is the bridge: **This supports the claim because...**

### Sourcing

A strong scaffold teaches students to:

1. identify a relevant sourcing feature (historical situation, audience, purpose, point of view);
2. use specific information about that feature;
3. explain how it affects the meaning, usefulness, emphasis, or limitation of the source.

Do not reward merely labeling HIPP categories.

### Argumentation / Qualification

A strong scaffold teaches students to:

1. answer the historical question with a defensible claim;
2. organize specific evidence into a line of reasoning;
3. explain how the evidence proves the claim;
4. address variation, limitation, counterevidence, or relative significance;
5. qualify the claim without abandoning it.

By Units 6–9, this pattern should increasingly replace sentence-level scaffolds.

## What not to do

- Do not enlarge every Module 05 simply because another topic has a chart.
- Do not turn evidence banks into answer keys.
- Do not provide a sentence frame when selecting and organizing the argument is the intended late-course challenge.
- Do not change a correct `skillBuilder.label` merely to make it match a different checkpoint skill. Module 05's skill and a later checkpoint's assessed skill can legitimately differ.
- Do not make students retrieve large amounts of content from memory when the actual instructional goal is learning a brand-new reasoning move.
- Do not remove all support in later units: the objective, reasoning sequence, and expectations should remain explicit even when evidence selection becomes independent.

## Course-wide audit decision rule

For each topic, ask:

> Can a student independently identify the skill, execute its reasoning moves, access or select appropriate evidence at the level expected for this point in the course, and produce an AP-authentic response without the teacher having to restate the assignment orally?

If yes, leave the module alone. **Consistency does not require identical scaffolding.**

If no, add the smallest amount of support that fixes the reasoning bottleneck while preserving the course's progressive release.
