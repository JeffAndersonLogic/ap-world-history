## The unit in one sentence

**Gunpowder won the land. Holding it took loyal people, steady money, and a
believable claim to rule, and the beliefs inside those empires did not stay still.**

The shorter version for the wall: **Gunpowder won the land. Holding it was the hard
part.**

Why this spine: it is already the argument the course's own best material makes. The
3.1 eBook chapter's thesis is that artillery's real consequence was political: only a
big, taxing state could afford it. That hands directly to 3.2 (how you keep and pay for
the state). 3.3 is the turn: religion is one of the tools of holding power, but belief
also changes on its own terms, sometimes because of empires (Chaldiran) and sometimes
beyond their control (Luther, Guru Nanak). 3.4 asks students to use all of it to
compare.

## How it connects to what comes before and after

- **From Unit 2 (bridge in, for 3.1's BeReady):** Topic 2.5 taught that gunpowder
  *traveled* the networks of exchange. Unit 3 starts from the question: once states had
  it, what did they do with it? Topic 2.2's Mongols are the other useful retrieval: the
  last huge land empire, which conquered fast and fragmented fast. That is the "holding
  is hard" problem in the form students already know.
- **Into Unit 4 (hand-off from 3.4):** Unit 3 is empires that grew over land with armies.
  Unit 4 opens (Topic 4.1) with the technology that let states grow over water. Keep the
  1453 link to European exploration **qualified**, as the audit requires: Constantinople
  is not the direct cause of the Age of Exploration.

## Topic by topic

### 3.1 Empires Expand

- **Story spine:** Cannons made old walls useless, and only big states could afford
  cannons, so big states got bigger.
- **Reasoning move:** causation (explain how and why empires expanded).
- **This topic owns:** gunpowder and cannons as the cause of expansion; the four land
  empires the CED names (Ottoman, Safavid, Mughal, Manchu/Qing); interstate rivalry from
  political and religious disputes, with the CED's two examples: **Safavid–Mughal
  (Kandahar)** and **Songhai–Morocco (Tondibi, 1591)**.
- **Must-have evidence and its "so what":**
  - Constantinople 1453: proves what artillery could do to the strongest walls in the
    world, and why only a state could field it. Not a trade-route cause story.
  - Tondibi 1591: proves gunpowder decided wars between states, not just sieges, and
    carries the Songhai–Morocco rivalry.
  - Kandahar: proves that rival empires fought over frontiers for political and religious
    reasons, carrying the Safavid–Mughal rivalry.
- **Mention only as a bridge:** devshirme and Janissaries (owned by 3.2; use only to
  show *who* fired the guns); Russia (real, but not one of the CED's four named empires
  in this key concept; keep it brief).
- **Hands to 3.2:** "Mehmed took Constantinople in seven weeks. Now he has to run it."

### 3.2 Empires: Administration

- **Story spine:** Conquest wins land; people, legitimacy and money hold it.
  Three parts, in this order: **people who serve, symbols that justify, systems that
  pay.**
- **Reasoning move:** causation (how rulers legitimized and consolidated power).
- **This topic owns** all three branches, with equal weight (the audit found the
  legitimacy branch was being crowded out):
  - People: devshirme, salaried samurai, mansabdars.
  - Legitimacy: divine right, Versailles, Qing imperial portraits, Mughal mausolea and
    mosques, the Inca sun temple at Cuzco, Songhai promotion of Islam.
  - Money: Ottoman tax farming, Mughal zamindar collection, Mexica tribute lists, Ming
    taxes in silver.
- **Must-have "so what":** every example answers one question: *how did this help a
  ruler keep control of people who had no reason to obey?*
- **Mention only as a bridge:** religious *change* (owned by 3.3). Here religion appears
  only as a ruler's tool for legitimacy.
- **Hands to 3.3:** "Rulers used religion to justify their power. But what happened to
  religion itself in these same centuries?"

### 3.3 Empires: Belief Systems

- **Story spine:** Religions changed as much as empires did.
  The three changes behind it, in the audit's words: **Christianity changes, Islam's
  divide hardens through imperial rivalry, Sikhism grows from Hindu-Muslim contact.**
- **Reasoning move:** continuity and change within belief systems. Not "how rulers used
  religion", which is 3.2's job.
- **This topic owns:** the Protestant *and* Catholic Reformations (both grew
  Christianity); the Ottoman-Safavid rivalry (Chaldiran, 1514) hardening the Sunni-Shia
  divide; Sikhism and Guru Nanak.
- **Must-have "so what":** each case names what *continued* (the core faith) and what
  *changed* (a split, a reform, a new tradition), and why.
- **Mention only as a bridge:** Akbar and Aurangzeb (useful context for Sikhism's
  setting, but they cannot replace Sikhism, which the old checkpoint let them do).
- **Hands to 3.4:** "You now have four tools empires used to grow and hold power:
  force, people, legitimacy and money. Which empires leaned on which?"

### 3.4 Comparison in Land-Based Empires

- **Story spine:** Every empire had the same jobs: expand, hold, pay, and justify.
  Comparing *how* they did those jobs explains why they turned out differently.
- **Reasoning move:** comparison, anchored to the CED's exact question: the methods
  empires used to **increase their influence**.
- **This topic owns:** the comparison itself. It introduces no new required content; it
  reuses 3.1 to 3.3 evidence.
- **The sentence every answer completes:** *Empire A and Empire B increased influence by
  ___, but differed because ___; this mattered because ___.*
- **Hands to Unit 4:** "All of these empires grew over land. The next story is about
  states that grew over water."

## Conflicts found while drafting (the constraint check)

These were found by reading the current pages against the spine above. None has been
changed.

1. **The 3.3 eBook chapter opens on the wrong question.** Its first section is "The Four
   Jobs Religion Did for a Ruler" (`scripts/lib/deep-reading-content/topic-3-3.js`),
   which is 3.2's legitimacy story. The audit repair fixed the 3.3 lesson, First & 10
   and checkpoints but not this chapter. Recommendation: reframe that opening section
   around continuity and change when 3.3 is built.
2. **3.1's BeSurreal is a Janissary's letter**, which centers devshirme, a 3.2 topic.
   It works as a bridge, but a gunpowder or siege prompt would fit the 3.1 spine better.
   This is a teaching call.
3. **3.1's third lecture card, "Why Constantinople Mattered", shows a Mughal map.** The
   caption is accurate, but it sits under the wrong card. A picture problem for the
   presentation build, not a history problem.
4. **Unit 3 has no class presentations, no Teaching OS pages and no Run of Show yet.**
   Every topic still needs the full production line. The lesson pages, readings, eBook
   chapters and scenarios exist and pass the CED lock.
