## Sources leaned on, and in what role

Original texts, read in full and quoted from them word for word:
- **Adam Smith, *An Inquiry into the Nature and Causes of the Wealth of Nations*** (1776), Book I,
  chapter 1, Project Gutenberg e-text 3300: "One man draws out the wire; another straights it; a third
  cuts it; a fourth points it; a fifth grinds it at the top for receiving the head ... the important
  business of making a pin is, in this manner, divided into about eighteen distinct operations ... I
  have seen a small manufactory of this kind, where ten men only were employed ... Those ten persons,
  therefore, could make among them upwards of forty-eight thousand pins in a day ... But if they had all
  wrought separately and independently ... they certainly could not each of them have made twenty,
  perhaps not one pin in a day"; and "a workman not educated to this business ... could scarce, perhaps,
  with his utmost industry, make one pin in a day, and certainly could not make twenty."
- **Andrew Ure, *The Philosophy of Manufactures*** (London: Charles Knight, 1835), pp. 13 to 16,
  Internet Archive scan `philosophyofmanu00urea`: "The term Factory, in technology, designates the
  combined operation of many orders of work-people, adult and young, in tending with assiduous skill a
  system of productive machines continuously impelled by a central power." On Lombe's Derby silk mill
  (1718): it "contained indeed certain elements of a factory." On Cromford: "When the first
  water-frames for spinning cotton were erected at Cromford, in the romantic valley of the Derwent,
  about sixty years ago." On Arkwright: the difficulty lay "above all, in training human beings to
  renounce their desultory habits of work, and to identify themselves with the unvarying regularity of
  the complex automaton."
- **UNESCO World Heritage Centre, Derwent Valley Mills** (inscribed 2001; statement of value endorsed
  2010): "The Derwent Valley saw the birth of the factory system, when new types of building were erected
  to house the new technology for spinning cotton developed by Richard Arkwright in the late 18th
  century."

Scholarship and institutions, each in its role:
- **Gregory Clark, "The British Industrial Revolution, 1760-1860,"** chapter 2 of *World Economic
  History* (UC Davis, 2002), read in full: cotton "did not grow in western Europe"; raw cotton
  consumption 2.6 million lb in 1760, 51.6 million in 1800, 621 million in 1850 (Table 1); "Britain in
  the seventeenth century was very poorly endowed with woodland. Population pressure on the land had led
  to the destruction of most of the original forest. Thus wood was relatively expensive ... much of the
  iron used in Britain was imported from Sweden and Russia"; Arkwright's water frame patented 1769;
  "By 1771 a water powered mill was established. In 1774, after the expenditure of about £12,000,
  profits began to be made. Note that in this period the average carpenter would earn no more than £36
  per year, so the capital sums invested were substantial"; Arkwright's "spinning and carding patents"
  struck down by the courts in 1785, "partly on the grounds that he had stolen the spinning invention
  from Highs and the carding invention from another innovator, Hargreaves"; earlier cotton mills of the
  1740s ("The first factory was powered by two asses, and employed ten girls"); "a constant search for
  means by which to reduce the skill content of labor"; Britain's population 6.5 m (1701), 7.8 m
  (1761), 23.1 m (1861); agricultural productivity estimates "all find productivity more than doubled
  between 1700 and 1850," with the caution that "none of these estimates ... are derived from direct
  estimates of outputs and inputs"; the Newcomen engine "used to drain the coal mines of northeast
  England and the tin mines of Cornwall"; Manchester 17,000 in 1760 and 180,000 by 1830.
- **E. A. Wrigley, *Energy and the English Industrial Revolution*** (Cambridge, 2010), ch. 2: "By 1700
  about half of the total energy consumption of England came from coal. At the end of the eighteenth
  century the proportion exceeded three-quarters, and by 1850 was over 90 per cent."
- **Robert C. Allen, *The British Industrial Revolution in Global Perspective*** (Cambridge, 2009), as
  summarized in his own NBER paper and the CORE reading: Britain's high wages and cheap energy made
  labor-saving, energy-using machines pay there. Used in teacher notes only, attributed to Allen.
- **Kenneth Pomeranz, *The Great Divergence*** (Princeton, 2000): China's main coal deposits lay far from
  its most commercial region, the Yangzi delta. Used in teacher notes only, attributed to Pomeranz.
- **Encyclopaedia Britannica**: "Bridgewater Canal" (the canal cut the cost of coal in Manchester in
  half); "Abraham Darby" (marketable iron from a coke-fired furnace); "Domestic system" (merchants gave
  materials to rural workers, usually at home; "finished products were returned to the employers for
  payment on a piecework or wage basis"); "Norfolk four-course system" (wheat, turnips, barley, then
  clover and ryegrass, instead of a fallow year).
- **Historic England, the Old Furnace, Coalbrookdale** (List entry / Heritage Gateway MSA453) and
  **English Heritage, History of Iron Bridge**: Darby smelted iron with coke at Coalbrookdale in 1709,
  "using coke made from local coal to fuel furnaces rather than charcoal."
- **Bridgewater Canal Company, history** and **Archives Hub (Jisc), The Duke of Bridgewater and the
  Bridgewater Canal**: Act of Parliament 1759 (Royal Assent 23 March 1759); the canal reached Manchester
  in 1761; the duke bore the cost and "had to borrow money to pay his workmen."
- **UK Parliament, Living Heritage, "Enclosing the land"**: "Parliament passed a total of 5,265
  enclosure Acts between 1604 and 1914, of which 3,828 were passed during 1750-1819"; enclosure
  "related to just over a fifth of the total area of England, amounting to some 6.8 million acres."
- **Manchester City Council, Public Intelligence A20, "Manchester's population over time"**: national
  census 1801, 70,409; local estimates for 1773 of about 23,000 (the local census of 1773 counted
  Manchester township at about 23,000).
- **Hansard, House of Commons, 18 January 1812, "Population of the several counties of Great
  Britain"**: 1801 total for England, Wales and Scotland, 10,472,048.
- **Cambridge Group for the History of Population and Social Structure (CAMPOP)**, occupational
  structure project: adult males in agriculture 43 percent in 1701; Allen's estimate of 35 percent by
  1800; Clark's census-based 36 percent in 1801.
- **Science and Industry Museum, Manchester, "Manchester, cotton and slavery"**: "By the 1780s, most of
  the cotton spun and woven in Manchester was grown by enslaved people on plantations in the Caribbean
  and South America"; "By 1860, plantations in the United States supplied almost 90 percent of the
  cotton spun and woven in Lancashire's mills."
- **Statute of Monopolies** (21 Jac. 1 c. 3, 1624), section 6, via copyrighthistory.org (Cambridge /
  Primary Sources on Copyright): letters patent for "the sole working or making of any manner of new
  manufactures within this realm to the true and first inventor" for "the term of fourteen years or
  under."
- **Cromford and the Derwent Valley:** the Derwent Valley Mills Partnership's key-site page and
  Cromford Mills (Arkwright Society) history: Cromford Mill built 1771; water from Bonsall Brook and the
  Cromford Sough, a lead-mine drain that "had the advantage of not freezing in winter"; Arkwright built
  houses for his workers (North Street); most early workers were women and children, the youngest about
  seven. Partners Samuel Need, "a wealthy Nottingham hosier," and Jedediah Strutt, "co-inventor of the
  Derby Rib stocking frame," introduced by the Nottingham banker Ichabod Wright (Belper Research; the
  Derwent Valley Mills page on Strutt).
