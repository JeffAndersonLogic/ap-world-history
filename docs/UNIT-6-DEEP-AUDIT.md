## Protected Unit 6 spine

- **6.1:** ideologies/rationales used to justify imperialism.
- **6.2:** compare processes by which state power shifted/expanded.
- **6.3:** explain how and why Indigenous peoples responded to state expansion.
- **6.4:** environmental/resource demand -> export economies/global economy.
- **6.5:** economic imperialism + commodity organization benefiting industrial powers.
- **6.6:** demographic/environmental/economic/transport factors -> voluntary and coerced migration.
- **6.7:** gender roles, ethnic enclaves, prejudice, and immigration regulation as effects of migration.
- **6.8:** relative significance of the effects of imperialism.

## Topic findings

### 6.1
The three learning targets divide the single required KC-5.2.III development into Social Darwinism, nationalism/civilizing mission, and religious conversion. This is a clean instructional decomposition of the official topic.

### 6.2
The runtime model includes transfer from non-state to state colonial control, European/American/Japanese territorial expansion, African conquest through war/diplomacy, settler colonialism, and neighboring-land expansion by the U.S., Russia, and Japan. The evidence/variation frame supports the official comparison objective.

### 6.3
The lesson preserves both anticolonial/nationalist resistance and varied forms of anti-imperial resistance, including religiously influenced rebellions. Yaa Asantewaa, the 1857 rebellion, and Samory Touré are appropriate evidence rather than substitutes for the broader process.

### 6.4
The lesson is tightly aligned to export-economy specialization driven by industrial demand for food/raw materials. Cotton, rubber, palm oil, guano, meat, and diamonds function as examples of the same mechanism.

### 6.5
The lesson correctly distinguishes economic imperialism from formal colonial rule and uses commodity organization/economic advantage as the second required branch.

### 6.6
The topic correctly includes demographic pressure, transportation-enabled urban/return migration, voluntary work migration, and coerced/semicoerced labor migration.

### 6.7
The topic correctly preserves all three social-effects branches: gender-role change in sending societies, ethnic enclaves/cultural transplantation, and prejudice/state regulation in receiving societies.

### 6.8
The synthesis objective is correctly stated as the relative significance of imperialism's effects. The main remaining task is assessment design: make sure the capstone actually **ranks/weights effects and defends relative significance**, rather than becoming a generic list of economic extraction, state expansion, and migration.

The structural scan also notes that this synthesis topic does not currently use a BeInTheRoom scenario. That is not itself a CED defect. Either add a capstone scenario because it improves reasoning, or document the synthesis topic as an intentional exception.

## Certification recommendation

Unit 6 is a strong candidate to become the **second reference-certified unit after Unit 2**, because it needs much less curricular reconstruction than Units 3–5.

Before certification:
1. human-review every checkpoint for exact target coverage;
2. tighten 6.8 to relative-significance argumentation;
3. run technical/browser contracts;
