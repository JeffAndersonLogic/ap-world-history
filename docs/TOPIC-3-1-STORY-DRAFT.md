## 3. The ninth-grade story

Last unit, gunpowder was just one more thing traveling the trade routes, a Chinese
invention moving west. This unit starts with what happened once states learned to build
it big.

For a very long time, the answer to an army was a wall. Constantinople is the famous
case: its land walls had stood for about a thousand years and no army had ever broken
through them. In 1453 the Ottoman sultan Mehmed II came with cannons so large that teams of
oxen had to drag them to the city. Seven weeks later the walls
were broken and the city was his.

Here is the part that matters most. A cannon like that is not just a weapon, it is a
bill. You need metal, a foundry, specialists who know how to cast and fire it, gunpowder
by the ton, animals and roads to move it, and soldiers paid to protect it. The engineer
who built Mehmed's biggest guns had, according to a Byzantine chronicler, offered his
services to the Byzantine emperor first. The emperor could not afford him. So guns
rewarded whoever already had the most money. A big state could buy guns, use them to take
more land, tax that land, and buy more guns. **Big states got bigger.**

That loop is how four great land empires grew in these centuries: the **Ottomans** across
three continents, the **Safavids** in Iran, the **Mughals** across India, and the
**Manchu Qing** across East and Central Asia. Each one has a moment where gunpowder did
the work.

The twist is that empires that keep growing eventually run into each other, or into a
neighbor with something they want. The **Safavids and the Mughals** fought for more than fifty
years over Kandahar, a fortress city on the road between Iran and India, which changed hands
four times. That fight was political, about a strategic road and who controlled it, and
it shows the limit of the loop too: in the 1650s the Mughals brought their guns three
times and could not take it back. **Morocco and Songhai** collided in 1591 over the
gold and salt of the Sahara, the same trade from Topic 2.4. Morocco's sultan claimed to
be the rightful ruler of all Muslims and demanded Songhai's salt revenue; when Songhai
refused, a small Moroccan army with guns crossed the desert and destroyed a far larger
Songhai army at Tondibi. Political and religious disputes turned neighbors into rivals,
and gunpowder often decided the fight.

That is why College Board cares: these empires are the biggest states on Earth for three
hundred years, and "how and why did they get so big" has a real answer.

## 4. The spine

**Approved in the story map:** "Cannons made old walls useless, and only big states could
afford cannons, so big states got bigger."

**Fact-check recommendation: change one word.** "Useless" is the kind of absolute a
teaching device carries into every slide (the 2.6 "year 900" case). Old walls were not
useless: Vienna held against the Ottomans twice, Kandahar held against Mughal guns three
times, and in Europe builders answered cannons with new low, angled forts. The mechanism
is right; the adjective is too strong.

**Proposed:** "Cannons could break the old walls, and only big, rich states could afford
cannons, so big states got bigger."

**Shorter for the wall:** "Guns take land. Land pays for guns."

## 7. The retelling slide, named

**The gunpowder loop.** One cycle a student can redraw from memory, with one arrow
leaving it:

> Cannons break walls -> only rich states can afford them -> conquest brings land and
> taxes -> taxes buy more guns -> (back to the start) ... and at the edge: borders meet
> rivals -> wars over land, wealth and the right to rule.

It carries all three Key Concepts at once (the mechanism, the empires that ran the loop,
and the rivalries at its edge), so it carries no single Key Concept band.

## 9. Fact-check notes on the teaching devices

- **"A thousand years unbroken"** is said of the *land* walls only (built in the 400s).
  The city fell in 1204 through the sea walls; say so if a student asks.
- **"The emperor could not afford him"** comes from the Byzantine historian Doukas.
  Attribute it ("according to a Byzantine chronicler") rather than stating it as fact.
- **Tondibi numbers** come from chronicles. Say "a few thousand" against "many times
  larger", not exact figures.
- **Kandahar is political, not religious.** Both courts were Muslim and Persianate; the
  deep reading says so explicitly. Do not tell students it was a Sunni and Shia war. The
  religious half of KC-4.3.III.i is carried by Tondibi (the Moroccan sultan's claim to
  rule all Muslims) and, in 3.3, by the Ottoman and Safavid rivalry.
- **"Gunpowder empires" is a debated label.** Guns were necessary, not sufficient:
  Mughal expansion also ran on cavalry, alliances and revenue. The loop says that
  (guns *and* money), which is why the spine names money.
