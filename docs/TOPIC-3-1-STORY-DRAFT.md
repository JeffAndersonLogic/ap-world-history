## 1. What the CED requires

- **Learning objective:** Explain how and why various land-based empires developed and
  expanded from 1450 to 1750.
- **KC-4.3.II:** Imperial expansion relied on the increased use of gunpowder, cannons, and
  armed trade to establish large empires in both hemispheres.
- **KC-4.3.II.B:** Land empires included the Manchu in Central and East Asia; the Mughal in
  South and Central Asia; the Ottoman in Southern Europe, the Middle East, and North
  Africa; and the Safavids in the Middle East.
- **KC-4.3.III.i:** Political and religious disputes led to rivalries and conflict between
  states. **CED examples:** the Safavid and Mughal conflict, and the Songhai Empire's
  conflict with Morocco.
- **Reasoning move:** causation. "How and why" asks for a mechanism, not a list of empires.

## 3. The ninth-grade story

For about a thousand years, the best defense a king, a city, or a lord had was a wall. If
you sat behind a thick stone wall with food and water, an army had two choices: wait
months for you to starve, or throw soldiers at the wall and lose thousands of them. Most
armies did neither, and so a noble in a castle could ignore a distant king, and a city
with good walls could stay independent. Walls were the reason power stayed local.

Then guns got big. Gunpowder had come out of China, and the networks you studied in Unit 2
carried it across Eurasia. By the 1400s, some rulers were casting huge bronze cannons. A
thin, tall wall built to stop ladders is the wrong shape to stop a heavy iron ball. The
ball does not climb the wall. It breaks it. In 1453 the Ottoman sultan Mehmed II, who was
21, aimed the biggest cannons anyone had seen at Constantinople, whose walls had turned
back attackers for centuries. It still took about seven weeks. But the walls fell.

Here is the part that matters. A cannon is not like a sword. You need a foundry to cast
it, saltpeter and sulfur for the powder, oxen and roads to drag it, engineers to aim it,
and trained soldiers to fire on command, year after year. Only a ruler with a steady tax
income could pay for all of that. So guns did not just help empires win fights. They moved
power away from local lords and toward whoever had the treasury. The Ottomans, Safavids,
Mughals, and Manchu (the Qing) all had that treasury, and they grew.

But there was a twist. Once these empires had swallowed the land between them, they ran
into each other. The Safavids and Mughals fought over Kandahar, a fortress city on the
road between Iran and India. Far to the west, Morocco sent a few thousand soldiers with
firearms across the Sahara in 1591 and destroyed the Songhai army at Tondibi, a fight over
the gold-and-salt trade you met in Topic 2.4. Guns decided Tondibi. At Kandahar, where
both sides had them, guns settled nothing. Guns took land. They did not, by themselves,
keep it.

That is why College Board cares. "Gunpowder empires" is not an explanation, because plenty
of states had guns and built no empire. The explanation is what guns required: money,
organization, and a rival next door to fight over the same ground.

## 4. Memorable spine

**Cannons made old walls useless, and only big states could afford cannons, so big states
got bigger.**

For the wall: **Gunpowder won the land. Holding it was the hard part.** (The approved unit
spine.) The second sentence is the hand-off to 3.2.

The spine must name the treasury, not just the gun. Otherwise the deck teaches the
circular answer the deep reading warns about.

## 6. Narrative beats

The story decides the count. This draft has 12 beats, plus the module handoff.

1. **Teacher Preflight.** Today's job, and what the exam just covered.
2. **BeReady, four minutes, no notes.** Because 3.1 follows the exam, the prompts draw on
   Unit 2 as a whole. Proposed: (a) "Gunpowder started in China. How did it get across
   Eurasia?" (2.5) (b) "What happened to the Mongol empire after its conquests?" (2.2)
   (c) "What did Mali get rich from?" (2.4). **Bridge:** "Gunpowder reached everybody. So
   what happens when rulers with huge treasuries, and rulers next door, all have it?"
3. **The topic question on screen.** "Why did a handful of empires get so big after 1450?"
   Over a real photograph of the Theodosian Walls.
4. **The old rule: walls win.** Why armies gave up on walls, and why that kept power local.
5. **1453: the wall stops working.** Mehmed II, the big guns, seven weeks. Include the
   honest limit: it was guns plus numbers plus a state, not guns alone.
6. **The price of a gun.** Foundry, powder, oxen, drilled soldiers, steady taxes. Only a
   state could pay. Local lords lose their strongholds.
7. **Organizing claim: guns made big states bigger.** The four CED empires on one map,
   with how each one used the same toolkit. Cases sit inside the claim, one slide, not
   four.
8. **The twist: empires collide.** Expansion ran out of empty ground, and empires ran into
   each other. The rivalry mechanism: overlapping claims, then a dispute, then what each
   side's military could do about it.
9. **Case 1, Kandahar.** A fortress on the road between Iran and India. Both sides had
   guns. The city changed hands, and neither empire could settle it.
10. **Case 2, Tondibi.** One side with firearms, one without, and a much larger army lost.
    Gold and salt, the Unit 2 link. Then the aftermath: Morocco won the battle and could
    not govern the conquest.
11. **Retelling slide** (below).
12. **AP synthesis.** Students answer the learning objective in a claim, evidence, and
    reasoning frame: how and why did land empires expand? Model one, then students write.
13. **Landing and hand-off.** "Mehmed took Constantinople in seven weeks. Now he has to run
    it." (Opens 3.2.) Then the module handoff slide.

Beats 9 and 10 are set side by side because the contrast between them is the analytic
point: same tool, opposite outcomes, and the reason is who else had it.

## 10. Traps to avoid

- **"They expanded because they were gunpowder empires."** That is circular. Name the
  treasury and the army the guns required.
- **Constantinople as a one-gun story.** It was seven weeks, a huge army, and a state.
- **A second 3.2 lesson.** Devshirme and the Janissaries appear only to say who fired the
  guns. The people-legitimacy-money story belongs to 3.2.
- **A second 3.3 lesson.** Chaldiran gets one sentence. The Sunni and Shi'a story belongs
  to 3.3.
- **The Constantinople to exploration shortcut.** The lecture already qualifies this. Keep
  it qualified, or leave it out.
- **Four one-slide empire tours.** The empires are evidence inside claim 7.
- **Every fight is about religion.** See decision 1.

## 11. Facts to verify before they reach a slide

The course text supports most of the story. These come from outside it, so they are
unverified until checked against a source, and per the authoring standard a hook is a
factual claim like any other.

- **Urban and the price of a gun.** A traditional account says the cannon founder Urban
  offered his guns to the Byzantines first, who could not pay, and then sold them to
  Mehmed II. If it holds up, it is the perfect hook for beat 6: the gun went to the buyer
  who could afford it. I have not confirmed it against a source, and it is reported by
  later writers. Use it only if verified, or label it "according to a chronicler."
- **Kandahar's dates.** The deep reading says only that it "passed between the two empires
  several times across the seventeenth century." I recall a Safavid capture in 1622, a
  Mughal recovery in 1638, a Safavid recapture in 1649, and failed Mughal sieges in 1649
  to 1653. Those specifics are from memory and are not in the course. Do not put dates on
  a slide until they are checked. "Several times in the 1600s" is safe.
- **Tondibi's date and size.** The course says 1591 and "a few thousand men" against a
  "many times larger" army. Do not add numbers beyond those.
- **The Qing and guns.** The deep reading says gunpowder is "a large part of the reason"
  the Qing and Russians expanded so far. That is a strong claim, and the Qing conquest also
  leaned heavily on mounted banner armies. Keep the Qing claim modest.

## What Jeff needs to decide

1. **Kandahar, religion, and the success criterion.** The course text says Kandahar was
   "a strategic corridor, not about faith" and Tondibi's motive was gold and salt, yet the
   CED sentence and success criterion say "political and religious disputes." Recommended:
   teach the mechanism honestly as "a dispute over land, routes, or wealth, sometimes
   sharpened by religious difference, backed by military power." The CED's "and" does not
   require both in every case. Present Kandahar as mostly political with a religious
   difference in the background. Note Chaldiran (3.3) as the clearest religious case.
   The alternative is to rewrite the deep reading, which is a bigger change.
2. **"Armed trade."** Recommended: name it in one teacher note as the sea counterpart of
   the cannon, hand it to Unit 4, and give it no slide. The KC-4.3.II band will show the
   words "armed trade" on the wall, so the note should say why.
3. **The spine's second sentence.** Approve "Gunpowder won the land. Holding it was the
   hard part," or edit it.
4. **BeSurreal.** The story map says to replace the Janissary letter. Recommended: a
   prompt from Urban the gun-founder's point of view, if the Urban account verifies, since
   it enacts "only a state can pay." If it does not verify, a prompt from inside
