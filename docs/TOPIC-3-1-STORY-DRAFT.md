## 3. The ninth-grade story

Think about a duke in a stone castle who will not listen to the king. The king could send an
army, but the walls are thick and tall. He would have to starve the duke out, which takes
months while his own army eats, or storm the wall and lose lots of soldiers. So most of the
time the king leaves him alone. Across much of the world, a castle, a walled city or a
mountain fort let a local lord say "no" to a faraway ruler, because making him say "yes"
cost too much.

Constantinople had some of the strongest walls anywhere. Its land walls had stopped
attackers for roughly a thousand years.

Then people got good at cannons. Gunpowder had started in China and traveled the networks of
exchange you studied in Unit 2. By the 1400s, rulers could cast huge bronze and iron guns. A
tall, thin stone wall is built to stop ladders and battering rams. A heavy iron ball does not
climb the wall, it breaks it. Sieges that took a year started taking weeks.

In 1453, the young Ottoman sultan Mehmed II brought giant cannons called bombards to
Constantinople. A Hungarian engineer named Urban helped build them. After about seven weeks
the walls gave way and the city fell. Everyone watching understood that the old rules had
changed.

Here is the catch. A cannon is not something a duke builds in his backyard. You need a lot of
metal, foundries, gunpowder, trained gunners, and an army to haul and guard it all. Only a
big state that can tax a lot of people can pay for that. So the new weapon did not just help
rulers win. It helped big, rich rulers beat small, local ones. And a castle wall could not
protect you from your own ruler anymore, so the same guns pushed outward against neighbors
and inward against rebel nobles.

That is how four empires got huge: the Ottomans in Southern Europe, the Middle East and North
Africa; the Safavids in Iran; the Mughals in India; and the Manchus, who founded the Qing in
China. When armies with guns met cavalry that charged, the guns won. At Chaldiran in 1514
Ottoman firearms and artillery broke a Safavid cavalry charge. At Panipat in 1526 the Mughal
founder Babur beat a much bigger army with cannons and musketeers. The Qing used firearms to
take over China and then pushed across the steppe grasslands where nomad horsemen had once
been nearly unbeatable.

But when you grow that big, you run into other big states. And that is when disputes over
power and religion turn into long rivalries, which is exactly what the CED asks you to see.

The Safavids in Iran and the Mughals in India shared a border, and Kandahar was the fortress
on it. Each empire wanted that frontier. Each also claimed to be a great Muslim power, with
different traditions of Islam behind them. So the fight was about land and about religion at
the same time, and Kandahar changed hands again and again in the 1600s.

Out in West Africa, the sultan of Morocco and the Songhai Empire were rivals. In 1591 Morocco
sent a few thousand soldiers across the Sahara with guns. At Tondibi they destroyed a Songhai
army many times their size, and Songhai broke apart as an empire. It is the cleanest test in
the course: same century, same region, one side with guns and one without.

And there is a twist. Morocco won the battle and then could not really govern what it won.
Kandahar kept changing hands even though both sides had cannons. Guns can win a place. They
do not run it.

Mehmed took Constantinople in seven weeks. Now he has to run it. That question is Topic 3.2.

## 4. The spine

**Cannons made old walls useless, and only big states could afford cannons, so big states
got bigger.**

Shorter for the wall: **Guns broke the walls. Only big states could buy guns.**

The unit spine, "Gunpowder won the land. Holding it was the hard part," is carried by the
twist near the end and by the hand-off to 3.2.

## 6. Narrative beats

1. **Teacher Preflight.**
2. **BeReady.** No notes. Three prompts from memory: (a) Where did gunpowder start, and how
   did it travel west? (b) The Mongols built the largest land empire. What happened to it?
   (c) Why is it hard to hold a huge empire together? **Bridge from 2.7:** "Gunpowder reached
   every empire in Eurasia. What did states do once they had it?"
3. **Topic question on screen:** Why did a few empires get huge after 1450, and what happened
   when they ran into each other?
4. **What was already true: the wall problem.** A wall let a local lord say no to a king.
   Constantinople's walls had held for about a thousand years.
5. **What changed: the cannon.** Why a wall is the wrong shape against an iron ball. Weeks,
   not a year.
6. **The proof: Constantinople, 1453.** Seven weeks. The Dardanelles Gun as physical evidence.
   One line on who fired the guns (the Janissaries; devshirme belongs to 3.2).
7. **The mechanism: only a state can pay.** Metal, foundries, powder, gunners, haulers. Big,
   taxing states win. Guns pushed outward and inward.
8. **Four winners:** the map of the Ottomans, Safavids, Mughals and Qing, with Chaldiran,
   Panipat and the Qing steppe campaigns as one-line proofs. One sentence names armed trade at
   sea as the other half of KC-4.3.II, which Unit 4 takes up.
9. **When big states meet: rivalries.** Expansion made neighbors. Disputes over power and
   religion became long rivalries (KC-4.3.III.i). Safavid-Mughal (Kandahar). Songhai-Morocco
   (Tondibi).
10. **The twist, and the hand-off:** guns win places, they do not run them. "Mehmed took
    Constantinople in seven weeks. Now he has to run it."
11. **Retelling slide** (proposed below).
12. **Reasoning:** model one causal chain using the Skill Builder's three parts (cause,
    mechanism, effect), then students write 2 to 3 sentences about ONE empire.
13. **AP synthesis:** answer the learning objective in two sentences, then one line on what
    the exam will ask (causation, and the two named rivalries).

## 7. Proposed retelling slide

A causal chain a student can redraw from memory, left to right:

**Cannon breaks the wall** -> **only a big treasury can pay** -> **big states grow outward and
inward** -> **empires meet** -> **rivalries over power and religion**

Each link carries one anchor the student can name: Constantinople 1453, the Dardanelles Gun,
the four empires, Kandahar and Tondibi.

Why a chain and not a map or a matrix: the learning objective is "how and why," and the
reasoning move is causation. A chain is the shape of a causal answer.
