## Sources leaned on, and in what role

Original texts, read in full-text editions and quoted from them word for word:
- **Locke, *Second Treatise of Government*** (1689; title page dated 1690), Project Gutenberg eBook
  #7370: §95, "MEN being, as has been said, by nature, all free, equal, and independent, no one can
  be put out of this estate, and subjected to the political power of another, without his own
  consent"; §123, "the mutual preservation of their lives, liberties and estates, which I call by the
  general name, property"; §222 on legislators who "endeavour to take away, and destroy the property
  of the people."
- **Locke, *An Essay Concerning Humane Understanding*** (1689; dated 1690), Gutenberg #10615, Book II,
  ch. 1: "Let us then suppose the mind to be, as we say, white paper, void of all characters."
- **Rousseau, *The Social Contract*** (1762), G. D. H. Cole's translation, Gutenberg #46333: "Man is
  born free; and everywhere he is in chains."
- **Rousseau, *Emile*** (1762), Barbara Foxley's translation, Gutenberg #5427, Book V: "A woman's
  education must therefore be planned in relation to man. To be pleasing in his sight, to win his
  respect and love ..."
- **Wollstonecraft, *A Vindication of the Rights of Woman*** (1792), Gutenberg #3420, chapter 4:
  "'Educate women like men,' says Rousseau, 'and the more they resemble our sex the less power will
  they have over us.' This is the very point I aim at. I do not wish them to have power over men;
  but over themselves."
- **De Gouges, *Déclaration des droits de la femme et de la citoyenne*** (September 1791), article
  premier, in the French text as reprinted by BAnQ and the académie d'Amiens: "La Femme naît libre
  et demeure égale à l'homme en droits." Translated on the page as "Woman is born free and remains
  equal to man in rights," a literal rendering, and said to be a translation. (A published English
  translation, Levy, Applewhite and Johnson, *Women in Revolutionary Paris* (1979), reads "lives
  equal to man in her rights"; the page uses the literal one because it keeps the parallel with the
  1789 article it rewrote.)
- **Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen** (1789), Avalon Project translation (Yale):
  Article 1, "Men are born and remain free and equal in rights"; Article 3, "The principle of all
  sovereignty resides essentially in the nation."
- **Declaration of Sentiments** (Seneca Falls, 1848), National Park Service text (Women's Rights
  National Historical Park): "We hold these truths to be self-evident; that all men and women are
  created equal"; "He has never permitted her to exercise her inalienable right to the elective
  franchise."
- **Kant, "An Answer to the Question: What Is Enlightenment?"** (*Berlinische Monatsschrift*,
  December 1784): the motto *Sapere aude*, rendered by Kant as "Have courage to use your own
  understanding" (CUNY Enlightenment Commons and Columbia College translations).

Scholarly and institutional sources, for facts about people and events:
- **National Park Service, Women's Rights National Historical Park**: the convention on 19 and 20 July
  1848 at the Wesleyan Chapel; one hundred women and men signed; Mott, a delegate of the Philadelphia
  Female Anti-Slavery Society to the 1840 World Anti-Slavery Convention in London, first met Stanton
  there. **Britannica, "Declaration of Sentiments"**: 68 women and 32 men signed; only the resolution
  on "the elective franchise" did not pass unanimously.
- **History Today; Britannica (Olympe de Gouges)**: the declaration published September 1791;
  executed 3 November 1793.
- **Britannica and the UK Parliament's Living Heritage pages; Wikipedia, "Society for Effecting the
  Abolition of the Slave Trade," citing Hochschild, *Bury the Chains* (2005)**: founded 22 May 1787 by
  twelve men, nine of them Quakers; the seal "Am I not a man and a brother?" and Wedgwood's medallion
  from it, 1787.
- **Yale Law Library, "Monuments of Imperial Russian Law: Emancipation of the Serfs"**: of 67 million
  people (1858 to 1859 census), "some 23 million were serfs"; the manifesto signed 19 February (3
  March) 1861. Alexander II's words to the Moscow nobility in March 1856, "it is better to abolish
  serfdom from above than to wait for the time when it will begin to abolish itself from below," are
  quoted in several histories (for example Daniel Field, *The End of Serfdom*, 1976) and given on the
  page as his reported words.
- **Habsburger.net, "The peasant is free"; Austria-Forum, "Bauernbefreiung"**: Kudlich's motion of 26
  July 1848 became law on 7 September 1848, ending peasants' subordination and labor dues.
- **Légifrance, décret du 27 avril 1848**: slavery abolished in French colonies; universal male
  suffrage decreed in March 1848.
- **Wikipedia, "Lei Áurea," and the Fundação Perseu Abramo timeline**: Law 3,353 of 13 May 1888;
  Brazil the last country in the Americas to abolish slavery.
- **NZHistory (Manatū Taonga)**: the Electoral Act signed 19 September 1893; "the first self-governing
  country in the world in which women had the right to vote in parliamentary elections."
- **National Portrait Gallery (John Locke)**: Locke "owned shares in the Royal African Company"; the
  shares were held in the 1670s (Glausser 1990, and the Nazarbayev University article on Locke's
  Royal African Company stock).
- **Musée protestant, "The Calas affair"; Universalis**: Jean Calas executed 10 March 1762; Voltaire's
  *Traité sur la tolérance* 1763; Calas rehabilitated 9 March 1765.
- **Encyclopedia.com and Wikipedia (Antonio Nariño)**: his printed Spanish translation of the 1789
  declaration (dated 1793 by most accounts, 1794 by some); arrested 29 August 1794; later a leader of
  independence in New Granada.
