## Sources leaned on, and in what role

The fact-check (2026-10-22) read every source below; a source that could not be opened is not
counted. Britannica was reached only through search summaries and is cited only where its wording was
seen.

Energy and engines:
- **Science Museum Group, "Rotative Steam Engine by Boulton and Watt, 1788"** (object co50948): the
  separate condenser; "Specific fuel consumption was reduced to less than one third that of a
  Newcomen type engine"; Boulton "wished to apply the steam engine to driving mill machinery."
- **Black Country Living Museum** (history trail): Newcomen's engine of 1712, "used in the Black
  Country to pump water out of ever deeper coal mines," at Coneygree, Tipton, near Dudley Castle.
- **Britannica, "Watt steam engine"** (search summary): the condenser idea, May 1765; the patent,
  1769. The 1911 *Britannica* on Watt: "the patent was not obtained till January 1769."
- Watt's 1782 patent, including the sun and planet gear (rotary motion); Papplewick, near Nottingham,
  1785, an early cotton mill driven directly by steam (sources differ on the first; the course claims
  no "first").
- Coal: **NBER Macrohistory Database, "Coal Production for United Kingdom"** (FRED A01211GBA418NNBR),
  millions of long tons: 1854, 64.7; 1900, 225.2.
- World energy: **Our World in Data, "Global energy 200 years"**: "By the turn of the 20th century,
  around half of the world's energy came from coal and half still came from biomass"; "Until the
  mid-19th century, traditional biomass" was the dominant source. **Vaclav Smil (2002)**: coals
  surpassed biomass fuels during the 1890s (solid fuels; not used, because OWID's own series,
  global-energy-substitution, puts coal at 5,728 TWh against biomass at 6,111 in 1900).
- Oil: **American Oil and Gas Historical Society**: Drake's well, Titusville, August 1859; the industry
  met demand for kerosene, lamp fuel.
- Internal combustion: **Deutz AG history** (via EBSCO and Encyclopedia.com): Otto's four-stroke engine
  of 1876, patented 1877. **Mercedes-Benz Group** history: Benz's application of 29 January 1886, DRP
  37435. **FHWA, Highway Statistics, Table MV-201** and **Historical Statistics of the United
  States**: 8,000 automobiles registered in 1900.

The second industrial revolution:
- Steel: **Philip W. Bishop, *The Beginnings of Cheap Steel* (Smithsonian, 1959)**: Bessemer's address
  at Cheltenham, August 1856. **NBER Macrohistory Database**: US steel ingots and castings (FRED
  A01208USA576NNBR), thousands of long tons, 1870, 69; 1900, 10,188; Great Britain (FRED
  A013BAGBA576NNBR), 1870, 215; 1900, 4,901.
- Chemicals: **Britannica, "Dye: Synthetic dyes"**: Perkin's mauve, 1856, "the first commercially
  successful synthetic dye"; about 90 percent of the world's dyes made in Germany by 1914. **BASF**
  (company history, via search): founded 6 April 1865; synthetic indigo sold from 1897.
- Electricity: **IEEE Milestone, Pearl Street Station, 1882** (ETHW): power from 4 September 1882;
  about 400 lamps on the first day. **Paul A. David, "The Dynamo and the Computer," *American Economic
  Review* 80 (1990)**: in 1899 "the horsepower capacity of all ... electric motors installed in
  manufacturing establishments in the country represented less than 5 percent of factory mechanical
  drive"; about two decades more to reach 50 percent.
- Precision machinery: **ASME Landmark 234, "The United States Standard Screw Threads"**: Whitworth's
  system "promulgated in 1841"; Sellers's system, April 1864; "machines with interchangeable parts,
  from typewriters to locomotives, flooded the national economy."

Reach, trade and migration:
- Rail: **Science and Industry Museum, Manchester** (Liverpool Road station): "the world's first
  inter-city railway opened, on 15 September 1830." **Fordham Modern History Sourcebook**, railway
  length table (from *The Spread of the Industrial Revolution*, Table 6): Russia 27 km (1840), 53,234
  (1900); Germany 469, 51,678. **Indian Railways** (via several sources): Bori Bunder to Thane, 16 April
  1853, 34 km. **National Park Service, Golden Spike NHP**: Promontory Summit, 10 May 1869.
  **History Today** and others: the Trans-Siberian begun 1891; through route 21 July 1904 (via
  Manchuria); the all-Russian Amur line 1916. Bairoch via Daudin, Morys and O'Rourke: world railways
  about 0.2 million km in 1870.
- Ships: **Norway Heritage, "By Steamship Across the Ocean"**: "The average crossing time on a sailing
  ship from Norway to America was 53 days"; "The ships of the White Star Line could cross the Atlantic
  in 7 to 8 days." **Daudin, Morys and O'Rourke, "Globalization, 1870-1914" (IIIS Discussion Paper
  250, 2008)**: "the opening of the Suez Canal in 1869 (which could only be used by steamships)."
  The **Pleiad**, 1854: Wikipedia's Baikie entry, the British Society for the History of Medicine and
  the Haslar Heritage Group: up the Niger and Benue, about 250 miles beyond earlier explorers, no
  deaths, quinine given daily.
- Telegraph: **Library of Congress**, Morse Papers: 24 May 1844, Washington to Baltimore, "What hath
  God wrought?" **IET Archives**: the Great Eastern landed the 1866 cable at Heart's Content, 27 July
  1866; the 1858 cable worked only weeks. Subtel Forum: the Red Sea line to Bombay, 1870.
- Trade: **Daudin, Morys and O'Rourke (2008)**: "Between 1870 and 1913, the wheat price gap between
  Liverpool and Chicago fell from 57.6% to 15.6%" (citing O'Rourke and Williamson 1994); shipping
  wheat from Chicago to New York fell from 17.2 to 5.5 percent of the Chicago price.
- Migration: **Timothy Hatton and Jeffrey Williamson, *The Age of Mass Migration* (Oxford, 1998)**:
  about 55 million Europeans to the Americas and Australia, 1850 to 1914 (publisher's description).
  **Adam McKeown, "Global Migration, 1846-1940," *Journal of World History* 15 (2004)**: Southeast Asia
  "consisted of over 29 million Indians and over 19 million Chinese"; "Between 28 and 33 million
  Chinese migrated into Manchuria and Siberia"; "At least 13 million Russians moved into central Asia
  and Siberia." Daudin, Morys and O'Rourke: "once the new steam technologies had lowered the cost of
  travel sufficiently, mass emigration became inevitable" (their word; the course says steam made
  journeys faster and cheaper).

Primary Source:
- **W. Stanley Jevons, *The Coal Question*, 2nd edition (London: Macmillan, 1866)**, Chapter I,
  "Introduction and Outline," as reprinted by the Library of Economics and Liberty; checked against the
  1865 first edition (Internet Archive scan, Introduction, pp. vii to viii), which differs in small
  words ("more obvious" for "more evident"; "As Fuel—or the source of fire—").
