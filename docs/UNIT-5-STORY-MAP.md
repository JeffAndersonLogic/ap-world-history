Like Unit 4, Unit 5 has a topic with two learning objectives: 5.1 carries A (the intellectual
and ideological context of the Atlantic revolutions) and B (how the Enlightenment affected
societies over time). A 5.1 story has to answer both, not the first.

## The unit in one sentence

**Between 1750 and 1900, two revolutions remade the world: one changed who had the right to
rule, and one changed how things were made, and each made new winners, new losers, and new
arguments about who deserved what.**

The shorter version for the wall: **New ideas changed who could rule. New machines changed who
did the work.**

Why this spine: the CED's unit title is "Revolutions," and its two big Key Concept families are
exactly these. KC-5.3 says "an intense period of revolution and rebellion against existing
governments" began in the 18th century, with the Enlightenment (KC-5.3.I) and nationalism
(KC-5.3.II) behind it; KC-5.1 says "the development of industrial capitalism" raised living
standards "for some" and changed what was made and how. The spine gives 5.1 the ideas, 5.2 the
political revolutions and nations, 5.3 to 5.5 the industrial revolution and its spread and
machines, 5.6 and 5.7 the states and the money, 5.8 and 5.9 the people who pushed back and the
society that formed, and 5.10 the argument about how much it all changed.

**One correction the spine has to carry: neither revolution happened everywhere, or all at
once.** Middle Eastern and Asian countries "continued to produce manufactured goods" while their
share fell (KC-5.1.II.B); only "a small number of states" sponsored industry (KC-5.1.V.C); reform
efforts in the Ottoman Empire and Qing China were "often resisted" (KC-5.1.V.B); the expansion of
rights came through long reform movements, not one revolution (KC-5.3.I.C). Unit 5 is about
change that was real, uneven, and argued over, which is why four of its ten topics are
Continuity and Change.

## How it connects to what comes before and after

- **From Unit 4 (bridge in, for 5.1's BeReady):** 4.8 hands off with "By 1750 the oceans were
  joined and states were stronger than ever. Next: who do those states belong to?" Topic 3.2 is
  the other retrieval: divine right, and Louis XIV ruling from Versailles. That is the claim the
  Enlightenment questioned.
- **Inside the unit:** 5.1 to 5.2 is the hinge from ideas to revolutions; 5.2 to 5.3 is the hinge
  from the political revolution to the industrial one (the CED puts both in the same years).
- **Into Unit 6 (hand-off from 5.10):** industrial states needed raw materials and markets and
  had new tools to take them. Unit 6 is the consequences: imperialism, resistance and migration.
  5.10 names that turn in one line and does not teach it.

## Topic by topic

### 5.1 The Enlightenment

- **Story spine:** Enlightenment thinkers asked every old authority a new question, "by what
  right?", and answered it with reason, natural rights and the consent of the people. The words
  reached further than the men who wrote them: over the next century, reformers and the people
  left out kept pressing them until rights reached further, while much of the old order held on.
- **Reasoning move:** Continuity and Change (CED p. 95), across both objectives: what changed in
  how Europeans and Americans thought about authority, and what the ideas did to societies over
  time, including what they did not change for decades. Suggested skill 3.A Claims and Evidence in
  Sources (identify and describe a claim or argument in a source): the topic's sources are
  arguments (Locke, Wollstonecraft, de Gouges, the Seneca Falls Declaration), so the skill is
  native to it.
- **This topic owns:** the Enlightenment's ideas and method: empiricism and reason applied to
  nature and to human relationships, religion's place in public life reexamined, the individual,
  natural rights and the social contract (KC-5.3.I.A); the rise and diffusion of that thought,
  which "often preceded" revolutions (KC-5.3.I); nationalism as a new and major force, introduced
  as the people becoming the nation (KC-5.3.II.i); reform movements driven by Enlightenment ideas
  **and religious ideals** that expanded rights: suffrage, the abolition of slavery, the end of
  serfdom (KC-5.3.I.C); women's suffrage demands and emergent feminism (KC-5.3.IV.B), with the
  CED's three examples: Wollstonecraft, de Gouges, Seneca Falls.
- **Must-have "so what":** both objectives get equal standing. Today the second (LO B, effects
  over time) has no target, criterion or checkpoint (the deep audit's finding).
- **Mention only as a bridge:** the revolutions themselves and their documents, the Declaration
  of Independence, the Declaration of the Rights of Man and Bolívar's "Letter from Jamaica"
  (KC-5.3.I.B, 5.2); the Haitian Revolution as an event (5.2); the new sense of national
  commonality and unification movements (KC-5.3.II.ii and II.iii, 5.2); Adam Smith and free
  trade (KC-5.1.III.A, 5.7); reforms that answered industrial capitalism (KC-5.1.V.D, 5.8).
- **Hands to 5.2:** "By 1789 the ideas were everywhere educated people read. In America, France,
  Haiti and Spanish America, people turned them into revolutions. What caused them, and what did
  they create?"

### 5.2 Nationalism and Revolutions in the Period from 1750 to 1900

- **Story spine:** Colonists, citizens and enslaved people turned the new ideas into
  revolutions, and a new loyalty to the nation made new states and broke or remade old ones.
- **Reasoning move:** Causation (CED p. 95): causes and effects of the revolutions. Suggested
  skill 3.C Compare the arguments or main ideas of two sources, which the CED's sample activity
  models with the three revolutionary documents.
- **This topic owns:** revolution and rebellion leading to new nation-states (KC-5.3); the
  American, Haitian and Latin American revolutions and independent states in the Americas
  (KC-5.3.III.B); the revolutionary documents (KC-5.3.I.B); democracy and liberalism (KC-5.3.IV.A.i);
  national commonality, harnessed by governments (KC-5.3.II.ii); nationalists challenging borders
  or unifying fragmented regions, with the CED's examples (KC-5.3.II.iii).
- **Mention only as a bridge:** the ideas themselves (5.1); abolition and the end of serfdom as
  long reform movements (5.1).
- **Hands to 5.3:** "While Paris argued about rights, a different revolution was starting in
  British workshops."

### 5.3 Industrial Revolution Begins

- **Story spine:** Britain had the right mix (coal and iron near water, more food, growing towns,
  protected property, capital and foreign resources), and the factory gathered the work under one
  roof and split it into pieces.
- **Reasoning move:** Causation (CED p. 95). Suggested skill 1.B.
- **This topic owns:** the factors behind industrial production (KC-5.1.I.A, all seven); the
  factory system and specialization of labor (KC-5.1.I.C).
- **Hands to 5.4:** "The new way of making things did not stay in Britain."

### 5.4 Industrialization Spreads in the Period from 1750 to 1900

- **Story spine:** Industry spread from northwestern Europe to other parts of Europe, the United
  States, Russia and Japan, and the world's share of manufacturing shifted: Middle Eastern and
  Asian producers kept making goods, but a falling share of them.
- **Reasoning move:** Continuity and Change (CED p. 95). Suggested skill 5.A.
- **This topic owns:** KC-5.1.II.B and KC-5.1.I.D, with the CED's examples (shipbuilding in India
  and Southeast Asia, iron works in India, textiles in India and Egypt).
- **Mention only as a bridge:** how states sponsored it (5.6).

### 5.5 Technology of the Industrial Age

- **Story spine:** Machines unlocked the energy stored in coal and oil, a second industrial
  revolution made steel, chemicals and electricity, and railroads, steamships and the telegraph
  carried trade and people into the interiors of continents.
- **Reasoning move:** Causation (CED p. 95). Suggested skill 1.B; the CED's sample activity ranks
  five technologies.
- **This topic owns:** KC-5.1.I.B, KC-5.1.I.E, KC-5.1.IV.
- **Mention only as a bridge:** colonial resource extraction (Unit 6); the deep audit's finding
  that today's Learning Target 2 and its checkpoint teach that instead.

### 5.6 Industrialization: Government's Role from 1750 to 1900

- **Story spine:** A few governments decided not to wait for industry and built it themselves:
  Muhammad Ali's Egypt and Meiji Japan.
- **Reasoning move:** Causation (CED p. 95). Suggested skill 5.A.
- **This topic owns:** state-sponsored industrialization (KC-5.1.V.C, with Muhammad Ali's cotton
  textile industry); Meiji reform, industry and regional power (KC-5.2.II.A).
- **Mention only as a bridge:** classes, ideologies and women's roles, which today's 5.6 teaches
  and which belong to 5.8 and 5.9 (the deep audit's priority rebuild).

### 5.7 Economic Developments and Innovations in the Industrial Age

- **Story spine:** Europe dropped mercantilism for free trade, big transnational businesses ran
  on new banking and finance, and industrial capitalism made more goods cheaper, raising living
  standards for some.
- **Reasoning move:** Continuity and Change (CED p. 95). Suggested skill 5.B.
- **This topic owns:** KC-5.1.III.A, KC-5.1.III.B (with HSBC, Unilever, stock markets,
  limited-liability corporations), KC-5.1.

### 5.8 Reactions to the Industrial Economy from 1750 to 1900

- **Story spine:** Workers organized, reformers pushed laws, Marx and the socialists imagined
  another society, and governments in the Ottoman Empire and Qing China tried to modernize
  against resistance at home.
- **Reasoning move:** Causation (CED p. 96). Suggested skill 2.B; the CED's sample activity pairs
  Engels on Manchester with a Manchester business directory.
- **This topic owns:** KC-5.1.V.D, KC-5.1.V.A, KC-5.3.IV.A.ii, KC-5.1.V.B.
- **Mention only as a bridge:** abolition, which today's 5.8 teaches as a main branch and which
  the CED gives to 5.1 (KC-5.3.I.C).

### 5.9 Society and the Industrial Age

- **Story spine:** Industry made a middle class and a working class, split women's lives by
  class, and packed cities faster than they could cope.
- **Reasoning move:** Continuity and Change (CED p. 96). Suggested skill 4.B.
- **This topic owns:** KC-5.1.VI.A, VI.B, VI.C.

### 5.10 Continuity and Change in the Industrial Age

- **Story spine:** Argue how much industrialization changed the world from 1750 to 1900, with
  evidence from the whole unit.
- **Reasoning move:** Continuity and Change (CED p. 96), answering LO K. Suggested skill 6.C.
- **This topic owns:** the argument, using Unit 5 evidence already taught; its six review Key
  Concepts (KC-5.1, KC-5.1.IV, KC-5.3, KC-5.3.I.A, KC-5.3.I, KC-5.3.II.i).
- **Hands to Unit 6:** "Industrial states now needed raw materials and markets, and had new tools
  to take them."
