## 3. The ninth-grade story

For three topics we visited three trade networks one at a time: the Silk Roads, the
Indian Ocean, the Sahara. Today we stop touring and ask why they look so alike.

All three were solving the **same problem.** People wanted things made far away: silk,
porcelain, pepper, gold, salt. Distance made those things expensive and dangerous to
get. Whoever made distance cheaper and safer got rich.

But each network faced a **different geography**, so each one needed different tools.
The desert needed camels, a saddle that let them carry more, and caravans that knew
the oases. The long overland routes needed caravanserais to sleep in safely and credit,
so a merchant did not have to carry a fortune. The ocean needed knowledge of the
monsoon winds, ships built to use them, and ports that welcomed foreign merchants.

Different tools led to the **same results.** Cities and states grew rich where routes
narrowed and goods had to pass through: Samarkand, Calicut, Malacca, Timbuktu, and
Mali. Workshops far from the routes made more because buyers kept asking: Chinese
porcelain and iron, and Indian cotton cloth. And the networks carried things nobody
was selling: religions, technologies, crops, and plague.

That is why College Board cares. A good comparison does not say "they were different."
It says **what was the same, what was different, and why.**

## 4. Memorable spine

**Same problem, different ground, different tools, same results.**

Shorter for the wall: **Geography picked the tools. Demand did the rest.**

## 5. Must-have evidence and what each one proves

| Evidence | Network | What it proves in the comparison |
|---|---|---|
| Caravanserai | Silk Roads | Commercial infrastructure lowered the risk of distance (similarity: all reduced risk). |
| Credit and paper money (flying cash, jiaozi) | Silk Roads | Money economies let trade grow without carrying coin (a commercial practice the CED names). |
| Monsoon knowledge, dhows and junks | Indian Ocean | The environment set the schedule and the technology (the difference is explained by geography). |
| Port states and merchant communities (Calicut, Malacca) | Indian Ocean | Trade built powerful nodes (similarity: cities grow at chokepoints). |
| Camel saddle and caravans | Sahara | Transportation technology made the desert crossable at scale (difference: animal, not wind). |
| Mali, Mansa Musa, Timbuktu | Sahara | A state that protected and taxed trade grew rich from it (similarity with the port states). |
| Chinese porcelain and iron, Indian textiles | All | Demand increased productive capacity (the KC-3.3 effect, shared by all three). |
| Diffusion recap (one line each from 2.5 and 2.6) | All | The networks carried more than goods (similarity; keep it brief, it was already taught). |

Named examples are evidence inside a claim. None of them earns its own slide.

## 6. Narrative beats

1. **Teacher Preflight.**
2. **BeReady (from 2.6):** "What did the same network carry that fed people? What did it
   carry that killed them?" Then one retrieval prompt per network: "Name one tool that
   made each network work." **Bridge:** "Three networks, three different toolkits. So
   why did all three end up producing rich cities and busy workshops?"
3. **Topic question on screen:** Why did three very different trade networks end up
   doing the same things?
4. **The shared problem:** distance is expensive and dangerous, and demand made it worth
   solving.
5. **Geography picks the tools:** desert, overland and ocean, with one toolkit each.
   This is the Big Difference.
6. **Same result 1, nodes:** cities and states grow where routes narrow.
7. **Same result 2, production:** demand far away makes workshops grow.
8. **Same result 3, diffusion:** a quick recap, not a reteach.
9. **Retelling slide:** the three-column matrix (proposed below).
10. **Reasoning:** model one comparison sentence using a single category, then students
    write their own. The rule: **same category on both sides.**
11. **AP synthesis:** answer the learning objective in two sentences, then a line on
    what the exam will ask.

## 8. Traps to avoid

- **Reteaching three topics.** This is synthesis. If a slide teaches a fact students
  did not meet in 2.1 to 2.4, cut it.
- **"They were different" answers.** Every comparison needs a *because*.
- **Unbalanced evidence:** comparing one network's tools with another's cities.
- **The Silk Road as one road.** It was many routes.
