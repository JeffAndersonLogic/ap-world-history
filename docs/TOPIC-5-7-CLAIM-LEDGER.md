## Sources leaned on, and in what role

The fact-check (2026-10-29) read every source below; a source that could not be opened is not
counted. Britannica, the UK Parliament's Living Heritage pages and Unilever's own history pages
refused this session (HTTP 403 or 404) and are not cited as read; where a search result quoted them,
the row says so.

Ideas and trade policy:
- **Adam Smith, *An Inquiry into the Nature and Causes of the Wealth of Nations* (1776)**, Project
  Gutenberg e-text 3300, read in full text 2026-10-29. Book IV, chapter 2, "Of Restraints upon
  Importation from Foreign Countries of such Goods as can be Produced at Home": "It is the maxim of
  every prudent master of a family, never to attempt to make at home what it will cost him more to make
  than to buy. The tailor does not attempt to make his own shoes, but buys them of the shoemaker ...";
  "What is prudence in the conduct of every private family, can scarce be folly in that of a great
  kingdom. If a foreign country can supply us with a commodity cheaper than we ourselves can make it,
  better buy it of them with some part of the produce of our own industry, employed in a way in which
  we have some advantage." Book IV, chapter 8, "Conclusion of the Mercantile System": "Consumption is
  the sole end and purpose of all production; and the interest of the producer ought to be attended
  to, only so far as it may be necessary for promoting that of the consumer"; "in the mercantile system,
  the interest of the consumer is almost constantly sacrificed to that of the producer." Book IV,
  chapter 9: the sovereign has "only three duties": defense, "an exact administration of justice," and
  "erecting and maintaining certain public works, and certain public institutions." The words
  "laissez-faire" and "laisser" do not occur in the text (searched 2026-10-29).
- **4.5 First & 10** (`scripts/lib/reading-content/unit-4.js`, reviewed in 4.5's build): mercantilism
  as "a kingdom grew strong by bringing in more gold and silver than flowed out ... sell more to other
  countries than it bought from them, and ... keep its colonies' trade for itself."
- **The Corn Laws and their repeal.** EBSCO Research Starters, "British Parliament Repeals the Corn
  Laws," and Encyclopedia.com, "Anti-Corn Law League" (as returned by search): the Manchester
  Anti-Corn Law Association founded in September 1838, leading in 1839 to a national League; Cobden and
  Bright its leaders; Peel's conversion to repeal split his party; the repeal passed the Commons in May
  1846. Charles Read, History and Policy, "Conservative splits: lessons from history": "failed
  harvests leading to the Irish potato famine contributed to Peel's decision that the Corn Laws should
  be repealed"; Peel argued "economic necessity in the face of famine." The Corn Law of 1815 is the
  common starting date in every account read.
- **Britain's tariff after 1846.** *Encyclopaedia Britannica* (1911), "Commercial Treaties" (vol. 9),
  and *The Spectator*, 11 and 18 February 1860, on Gladstone's budget: under the French treaty Britain
  would "sweep away the duties on all manufactured goods"; the budget "struck off every item which was
  not a matter of revenue"; the war duties on tea and sugar were kept; the wine duty cut from 5s. 10d.
  to 3s. a gallon.
- **The Cobden-Chevalier Treaty, 1860.** Encyclopedia.com, "Cobden-Chevalier Treaty"; Wikipedia,
  "Cobden-Chevalier Treaty" (citing Nye, Irwin, and O'Rourke and Williamson): signed 23 January 1860; it
  cut duties on wine, brandy and silk from France and coal, iron and manufactures from Britain; the
  first of a series of "most favoured nation" treaties Britain negotiated in the 1860s, copied across
  Europe.
- **Germany's tariff of 1879.** Wikipedia, "German tariff of 1879" and "Marriage of Iron and Rye":
  duties on grain, livestock and iron from 1879, after the 1870s agricultural crisis; Britannica (as
  quoted in search results) dates passage to 1879.
- **United States tariffs.** Wikipedia, "Morrill Tariff" and "McKinley Tariff": the Morrill Tariff of
  1861 "began a long period of protectionism that lasted until 1913"; the McKinley Tariff of 1890 raised
  the average duty to about 49 percent.

Business and finance:
- **The Bubble Act.** Wikipedia, "Bubble Act," and the UK Insolvency Service's *Technical Manual*,
  chapter 75, "History of Companies in the British Isles": from 1720 joint-stock companies could not be
  formed unless approved by royal charter (or an act of Parliament); repealed in 1825.
- **Limited liability.** Wikipedia, "Limited Liability Act 1855" and "Joint Stock Companies Act 1856":
  the 1855 Act "first expressly allowed limited liability for corporations that could be established by
  the general public"; the 1856 Act "provided a simple administrative procedure by which any group of
  seven people could register a limited liability company," banks and insurers excluded. Several
  accounts of the Companies Act 1862 call it a consolidation.
- **France.** Freedeman, *Joint-Stock Enterprise in France, 1807-1867* (UNC Press, catalogue
  description) and the Geneva working paper "Les origines de la société anonyme en France" (RePEc): laws
  of 1863 and 1867 removed the need for government authorization.
- **Stock exchanges.** The 1886 print "The World's Exchanges" (Library of Congress 98516796), read
  against its catalogue record: "Images of stock exchanges in New York, Paris, London, Chicago, Hamburg,
  Frankfort, Berlin, and Brussels." London's exchange: the Grub Street Project (from Wheatley and
  Cunningham) and the London Stock Exchange's own timeline as summarized in search results agree on
  organized trading at Jonathan's coffee house from 1698 and a building of the brokers' own from 1773;
  the course says only "since the 1700s."
- **The railway boom.** Wikipedia, "Railway Mania," and Focus Economics, "Railway Mania": a
  speculative boom in railway shares in Britain peaking in 1845 to 1846, drawing in many middle-class
  investors; 263 or 272 railway Acts in 1846 (the sources differ). The course gives no number.
- **HSBC.** HSBC, "History timeline" (hsbc.com, read 2026-10-29): founded by "Thomas Sutherland, a young
  Scotsman" working in Hong Kong for a shipping firm, who "recognised that businesses needed better
  local banking facilities"; opened "in Hong Kong on 3 March 1865, and in Shanghai a month later"; "In
  July 1865, the bank opened an office in London to facilitate foreign exchange services for clients in
  China and India"; "By the turn of the 20th century, we operated in 16 countries and territories."
  The provisional committee: Wikipedia, "Dent & Co." and "Francis Chomley" (Chomley, a Dent's partner,
  chaired the committee's first meeting on 6 August 1864); Dent & Co. was "one of two major
  opium-trading houses on the China coast"; *South China Morning Post*, "Bank was built on shipping and
  opium trade" (headline and summary).
- **Lever Brothers and Unilever.** Library of Congress trademark registrations 2020707577 (Lever Bros.,
  "Sunlight" the "Self-Washer" Tablet, registered 31 August 1886, Warrington, England) and 2020713351
  (Lever Brothers, Limited, "Sunlight" Soap, registered 5 May 1891, Port Sunlight, England), each read
  as an image. Unilever's history page "1800s" as quoted in search results: 1884, "William Lever and
  Lever & Co began selling a soap that contain copra or pine [palm] kernel oil"; the Jurgens family in
  Oss from 1860; the Van den Berghs "exporting to England by 1870"; the margarine patent bought by
  Jurgens in 1871. Wikipedia, "Lever Brothers" and "Unilever"; Grace's Guide, "Lever Brothers":
  Port Sunlight developed from 1888; Margarine Unie formed in 1927; Unilever founded 2 September 1929,
  "Unilever PLC ... London and Unilever NV ... Rotterdam"; Wikipedia, "Huileries du Congo Belge": the
  Belgian Congo concession of 1911, and forced recruitment of labor. MyLearning (Port Sunlight Museum
  partner), "The story of Port Sunlight" and "First bar of Sunlight Soap": Lever sold soap in wrapped
  packs bearing the Sunlight name, unusual at the time, where soap had been cut from long bars.

Goods and living standards:
- **C. Knick Harley, "Cotton Textile Prices Revisited" / "Was Technological Change in the Early
  Industrial Revolution Schumpeterian?" (Oxford Economic and Social History Working Paper 81)**, read in
  full: "cloth prices fell impressively in real terms before the end of the Napoleonic Wars because the
  price of yarn had fallen so much. Cloth prices continued to fall thereafter as technology improved in
  weaving and finishing."
- **Charles Feinstein, "Pessimism Perpetuated: Real Wages and the Standard of Living in Britain during
  and after the Industrial Revolution," *Journal of Economic History* 58 (1998)** (abstract, RePEc): the
  average working-class family's standard of living improved "by less than 15 percent between the 1780s
  and 1850s"; the working-paper version: "very modest until after 1861."
- **Roderick Floud and Paul Johnson, eds., *The Cambridge Economic History of Modern Britain*, vol. 2,
  "Living standards 1860-1939"** (chapter abstract, Cambridge Core): most historians accept that real
  wages grew significantly from 1851 to 1913, while height and infant mortality give a less optimistic
  picture up to 1900.
