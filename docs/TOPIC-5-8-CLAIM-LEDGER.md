## Sources leaned on, and in what role

The fact-check (2026-11-01) was done by four research passes, each reading the sources below; a source
that could not be opened is not counted. Britannica, Oxford Reference, the Fordham Sourcebook pages for
the 1839 and 1856 edicts, the British Museum, UNESCO and Project MUSE refused this session (HTTP 403,
404, 500 or a bot check) and are not cited as read; where a search result quoted them, the row says so.

Britain: unions, the Chartists and reform:
- **The Combination Acts**: the 1800 Act's text (Marxists Internet Archive) voided agreements "for
  obtaining an advance of wages ... or for lessening or altering their ... usual hours ... of working";
  Encyclopedia.com (Gale), "Combination Acts": "They prohibited all organized activity intended to improve
  working conditions or wages ... They were repealed in 1824"; historyhome.co.uk: "In 1825 ... an
  amendment to the repeal legislation was passed, limiting the purpose of combinations."
- **Tolpuddle**: Tolpuddle Martyrs Museum ("an oath of secrecy ... seven years' transportation"; an
  "800,000-strong petition"; the men home from June 1837 to August 1839); Encyclopedia.com (Oxford
  Companion): sentenced under an Act of 1797 against "unlawful oaths"; "Two years later the six were
  pardoned."
- **The Chartists**: London Museum, object 480931 (the Charter "launched in 1838" with six points:
  "universal male suffrage for over 21s, 300 constituencies of equal size, abolition of property
  qualifications for MPs, annual parliaments, elections by secret ballot and an annual salary of £500
  for MPs"; the 1839 petition of "over 1.2 million signatures ... rejected"; "Two subsequent petitions
  were made in 1842 and 1848 but both were rejected"); Mark Hovell, *The Chartist Movement* (1918),
  ch. 16 (1842: "It had 3,317,702 signatures, said the Northern Star"); History of Parliament blog
  (3,315,752; rejected "by 287 votes to 49"); Encyclopedia.com (Gale), "Chartist Movement": all points
  but annual parliaments "were enacted subsequently." The Royal Collection's record for RCIN 2932482:
  Kilburn's daguerreotype of 10 April 1848, "One of a pair of daguerreotypes of the Chartist Meeting on
  Kennington Common purchased by Prince Albert in 1848"; "the rally passed peacefully."
- **Unions' legal standing**: TUC Library blog (London Metropolitan University): the first Congress met
  "between 2nd – 6th June 1868 in the Mechanics' Institute ... Manchester"; the Trade Union Act 1871,
  s. 2: a union's purposes "shall not, by reason merely that they are in restraint of trade, be deemed to
  be unlawful."
- **The Labour Party**: the Labour Representation Committee founded 26 and 27 February 1900; 29 seats in
  1906; the name "The Labour Party" adopted in February 1906 (Wikipedia only; parliament.uk and the TUC
  refused this session). Routine chronology, standard in every account.
- **Factory and mines laws**: The National Archives, education resource on the 1833 Factory Act ("no
  child workers under nine years of age"; "children of 9-13 years to work no more than nine hours a day";
  "four factory inspectors appointed"); the Mines Act 1842, 5 & 6 Vict. c. 99 (enacted text: no "Female
  Person whatsoever within any Mine or Colliery"; no "Male Person under the Age of Ten Years"); the
  Factories Act 1847, 10 & 11 Vict. c. 29 (ten hours a day for young persons and women from 1 May 1848).
- **The vote**: Encyclopedia.com (Oxford Companion), "Reform Acts": 1867 added "some 938,000" urban
  working-class voters; 1884 raised the electorate "to 5 million, bringing in large numbers of county
  voters"; women first voted in national elections in 1918 ("restricted to women over 30").
- **Schools**: educationengland.org.uk, the Elementary Education Act 1870 (the "Forster Act"; school
  boards "may" require attendance from five to thirteen) and the 1880 Act ("obliged local authorities to
  make byelaws requiring school attendance").
- **London's sewers**: London Museum: "In the hot summer of 1858"; Bazalgette's plan of "1,100 miles of
  drains ... 82 miles of new brick-lined sewers ... completed in 1875."
- **Engels**: *The Condition of the Working Class in England in 1844*, translated by Florence Kelley
  Wischnewetzky (New York, 1887), archive.org, chapter "The Great Towns": "At the bottom flows, or rather
  stagnates, the Irk, a narrow, coal-black, foul-smelling stream, full of debris and refuse"; "The town
  itself is peculiarly built, so that a person may live in it for years, and go in and out daily without
  coming into contact with a working people's quarter or even with workers, that is, so long as he
  confines himself to his business or to pleasure walks." The book was "first issued in Germany in 1845"
  (Engels, 1892 preface). Grace's Guide, "Ermen and Engels": Engels's father a partner; the son "sent to
  England by his father to learn the business."
- **Whellan's directory**: *A New Alphabetical and Classified Directory of Manchester and Salford*, W.
  Whellan & Co., title page dated 1853, preface signed "MANCHESTER, February, 1853" (University of
  Leicester, Historical Directories). The preface does not describe the city; the opening chapter,
  "Sketches of Manchester and its Vicinity," does: "the innumerable large mills and factories whose tall
  chimneys shoot up in every direction, betoken the activity, enterprise, and wealth of its inhabitants";
  Manchester as "the 'workshop of the world'." The CED's "Wheelan and Co. business directory, 1852" is
  printed so in the source file; an 1852 issue appears in catalogues but was not opened. The course says
  "the early 1850s" and quotes the 1853 copy.

Workers, socialism and Germany:
- **Marx and Engels, *Manifesto of the Communist Party*,** English translation by Samuel Moore with
  Engels, 1888, read in the Marxists Internet Archive's text (chapters I, II, IV and the 1872 and 1888
  prefaces): "First Published: February 1848"; the manuscript "was sent to the printer in London"; the
  Communist League "commissioned us ... at the Congress held in London in November 1847." Section I
  opens: "The history of all hitherto existing society is the history of class struggles." Section II:
  "the theory of the Communists may be summed up in the single sentence: Abolition of private property."
  Section IV ends: "The proletarians have nothing to lose but their chains. They have a world to win.
  Working Men of All Countries, Unite!" The archive's editors note that "Workers of the World, Unite!" is
  a popular simplification that "does not appear in any official translation"; the German is
  "Proletarier aller Länder, vereinigt euch!" Engels, 1888 preface: "we could not have called it a
  socialist manifesto ... in 1847, socialism was a middle-class movement, communism a working-class
  movement," naming the "Owenites in England" among the socialists.
- **Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, "Socialism"**: socialism as an economy in which "the bulk of
  the means of production is under social, democratic control."
- **Robert Owen**: Open University OpenLearn, "Robert Owen and New Lanark" ("When he arrived at New
  Lanark in 1800"; "He reduced the adult working hours from 12 to 10"; the Institute opened "in January"
  1816; New Harmony bought "in 1825"); New Lanark Trust timeline; Indiana State Museum (New Harmony sold
  to Owen in 1825, dissolved by 1827); Victorian Web, "Socialism" (the word first appeared in English in
  1827 in the *Co-operative Magazine*; in the 1830s "socialism was used interchangeably with ...
  Owenism").
- ***Capital***: German History in Documents and Images (GHDI), cover of the first edition ("Hamburg:
  Otto Meissner, 1867"); English Heritage, "Karl Marx's London connections" ("From 1849 until his death
  in 1883, Karl Marx was based in London ... working mostly in the Round Reading Room at the British
  Museum").
- **German workers' parties**: GHDI, Gotha Program (the ADAV "founded in 1863 by Ferdinand Lassalle";
  the Eisenach party founded "In August 1869"; the merged party "renamed the Social Democratic Party of
  Germany" in 1890); GHDI, Anti-Socialist Law (passed "October 21" 1878; "the law's lapse on September
  30, 1890"); GHDI, Reichstag elections 1871 to 1890 (1890: SPD 19.7 percent of the vote and 35 seats,
  Centre 18.6 percent and 106 seats; the SPD "won more votes than any other party from 1890 onwards");
  Bundestag exhibition, "Elections in the Empire" (19.8 percent in 1890; the largest group in 1912).
  Erfurt Program, 1891: Marxists Internet Archive text and the Wikipedia and Britannica summaries as
  returned by search (Kautsky the principal author of its theoretical part; it committed the party to
  Marxism).
- **Bismarck's insurance laws**: bismarck-biografie.de, "Bismarck's social legislation 1881 to 1890"
  (sickness insurance "At the end of May 1883"; accident insurance "In July 1884"; invalidity and old-age
  insurance "In June 1889"; to "win over the working classes"); GHDI, the Imperial Message of 17 November
  1881 (the editors: "the chancellor offered German workers the carrot of social welfare legislation";
  the message pairs "the repression of Social Democratic excesses" with "the positive promotion of
  workers' welfare"); GHDI, Bismarck's Reichstag speech of 15 March 1884 ("dissatisfied workers are just
  what they need"); *Smithsonian*, "Bismarck tried to end socialism's grip" (to "beat the socialists at
  their own game").
- **The Second International**: Marxists Internet Archive, *Under the Socialist Banner*, chapter 1,
  Paris congress of July 1889 ("that the duration of the working day be set at eight hours"; a
  demonstration "on May 1, 1890 ... already ... resolved by the American Federation of Labor at its
  congress of December 1888 in St. Louis, this date is herewith fixed"); chapter 2, the Brussels congress
  of August 1891, which kept May 1 for the demonstration (as summarized by search, with Braunthal,
  *History of the International*, vol. 1, ch. 17).
- **The American Federation of Labor**: Library of Congress business reference guide ("founded on
  December 8, 1886 ... Samuel Gompers becoming its first and longest serving president"); VCU Social
  Welfare History Project ("a loose grouping of smaller craft unions" seeking "higher wages and better
  working conditions").
- **Eugene Debs**, "How I Became a Socialist," *New York Comrade*, April 1902 (Marxists Internet Archive):
  "in the gleam of every bayonet and the flash of every rifle the class struggle was revealed." The 42
  Debs texts dated 1895 on the archive, about 68,700 words, contain neither "reformed" nor "replaced";
  exact-phrase searches for "It cannot be reformed. It must be replaced" found nothing.
- **Haymarket**: PBS *American Experience*; Library of Congress, "Chronicling America: Haymarket Affair";
  Altgeld's pardon message (famous-trials.com): "The state has never discovered who it was that threw
  the bomb." How the police dead other than Officer Degan died is disputed (Avrich; Messer-Kruse).

The Ottoman Empire:
- **Maria Todorova, "Ottoman Empire," *Encyclopedia of Modern Europe*** (Encyclopedia.com): Selim III
  "set up a new infantry, the famous nizam-i cedid (new order), with European-style uniforms, imported
  rifles, and French instructors"; the Janissaries "viewed the nizam-i cedid as such a threat ... that
  Selim was forced to disband it. He was still deposed in 1807 and killed in 1808"; "the Auspicious
  Event: the destruction of the janissary corps by Sultan Mahmud II in 1826 ... A new army along
  European lines was constructed." The same page, "Ottoman Military: Ottoman Army": "(June 1826)."
- **John P. Dunn, "Ottomans Suppress Janissary Revolt,"** EBSCO Research Starters: the opposition to
  Selim was "an alliance of politicians, religious leaders, and soldiers," including "important
  conservative leaders within the ulama." *Encyclopedia of World Biography*, "Selim III": deposed "on May
  29, 1807." The founding year of the New Order (1792 to 1793 by Britannica as returned by search; a
  corps organized from 1790 by Shaw as cited on Wikipedia) was not read in a source opened; the course
  says "in the 1790s."
- **The Edict of Gülhane, 3 November 1839,** Halil Inalcik's translation in J. C. Hurewitz, ed., *The
  Middle East and North Africa in World Politics* (Yale, 1975), as reproduced by the Modern Middle East
  Sourcebook Project (2004): "The guarantees promising to our subjects perfect security for life, honor,
  and property"; "A regular system of assessing taxes"; "An equally regular system for the conscription
  of requisite troops and the duration of their service"; "The Muslim and non-Muslim subjects of our
  lofty Sultanate shall, without exception, enjoy our imperial concessions." Its introduction: proclaimed
  "in Gülhane square on November 3, 1839."
- **Iakovos D. Michailidis, "Modernizing the Empire"**: the 1856 edict's "most remarkable provision ...
  was the legal equation of Muslims and non-Muslims," which "was not well perceived by many Muslims who
  traditionally enjoyed a superior status over the Christians"; Cevdet Paşa (quoted through Braude):
  "Many Muslims began to grumble: 'Today we have lost our sacred national rights ...'"; "the patriarchs
  and other spiritual chiefs were displeased" too. Tanzimat dates: "In 1839, the so-called Tanzimat
  period began, and finished in 1876." World History Commons, "Ottoman Reform Decree, 1856" ("without
  distinction of classes or of religion").
- **Kadir Yıldırım, "Industry in Ottoman Istanbul,"** *A History of Istanbul* (istanbultarihi.ist): "the
  Fez Factory (Feshane) established in 1835 by the Golden Horn with machinery imported from Britain and
  Belgium"; "the Zeytinburnu Iron Works ... constructed in 1843." **Davut Hut, "History of Communication
  in Istanbul,"** same work: "the Istanbul-Edirne line was completed on 19 August 1855."
- **The 1876 constitution and 1878**: the constitution of "11/23 December 1876" (Wikisource, the
  *American Journal of International Law* 1908 translation); *Gale Encyclopedia of World History:
  Governments*, "Ottoman Empire": "In February 1878, after less than fifteen months, Sultan Abdülhamid II
  ... suspended the constitution and dissolved parliament."

Qing China:
- **Benjamin Elman, "Naval Warfare and the Refraction of China's Self-Strengthening Reforms,"** *Modern
  Asian Studies* 38:2 (2004): "the Self-Strengthening reforms initiated after the Taiping Rebellion"; the
  1861 proposal of Prince Gong and Wenxiang for the Zongli yamen; "Jiangnan Arsenal (1865), set up in
  Shanghai by Zeng and Li Hongzhang" ("In the summer of 1865, Li Hongzhang ... and Ding Richang ... rented
  a machine shop in the Hongkew section of Shanghai"); "Fuzhou Shipyard (1866) ... established by Zuo
  Zongtang"; "the China Merchants' Steam Navigation Company in 1872"; "the Chinese losses at sea in
  1894–95." On the Summer Palace: "The Empress Dowager's efforts to garner funds for her ambitious plans
  to expand the Summer Palace did not use up the imperial treasury or leave nothing for the Chinese navy,
  but inadequate funding did set limits on Li Hongzhang's plans"; the "actual problems were: 1) the
  political and regional disorganization of the empire; and 2) naval personnel were insufficiently
  trained."
- **Ronald Gray, "China's Self-Strengthening Movement,"** EBSCO Research Starters: the Zongli Yamen
  established in 1861 under Prince Gong. **Columbia, Asia for Educators**: self-strengthening's "principle
  goal was to maintain the strong essence of Chinese civilization while adding superior technology from
  abroad." **Karl-Heinz Pohl** (Trier): the ti-yong slogan "was coined in this form by Zhang Zhidong
  during the 'Hundred Day's Reform' in 1898, its content was widespread among intellectuals since the
  1860s." The course does not quote the 1898 slogan.
- **The 1867 Tongwen Guan debate**: Theodore Huters, *Bringing the World Home* (Hawai'i, 2005, open
  access), pp. 30 to 34: the Tongwen Guan "founded in 1862 to train translators under the auspices of
  the Zongli yamen"; "the court document submitted on January 28, 1867"; Woren's memorial "presented on
  March 20, 1867," in Huters's translation: "I have heard that establishing a country rests on valuing
  propriety and righteousness, not on schemes and stratagems. Efforts toward the fundamental lie in
  people's hearts and minds, not in skills and crafts. Now, for the sake of trivial crafts, we will honor
  barbarians as our teachers"; Prince Gong (Yixin): "Western learning is something urgently in need of
  study"; "even the bitter opponents Cixi and Yixin were united in favor of the proposal"; "the effort to
  attract a new cadre of highly educated young officials to science studies failed." Linghui Zhu and
  Reine Meylaerts, *JoSTrans* 38 (2022): the memorial of 11 December 1866; Yixin's argument that
  machines, firearms, shipping and warfare all come from astronomy and mathematics; an edict ended the
  debate when "the conservatives had failed to recommend any qualified Chinese instructors." Fang
  Chao-ying, "Wo-jên," in Hummel, *Eminent Chinese of the Ch'ing Period*: Woren "ordered to serve in the
  Tsungli Yamen"; "he pleaded illness and was granted leave. Finally he was relieved of all his posts
  except that of tutor to the Emperor." Zhao Yali, *Beijing Daily*, 23 May 2024, and Lei Yi, *Economic
  Observer*, 2008: 98 registered, 72 sat the examination of 21 June 1867, about 30 or 31 admitted; Woren
  "reportedly" feigned a fall from his horse (Zhao only). Atwill and Atwill, *Sources in Chinese History*
  (ebrary.net excerpt): a second translation of Woren ("the foundation of a nation lies in its
  righteousness and propriety, instead of force and trickery"; "Why must we have foreigners serve as our
  teachers?").
- **The Hundred Days, 1898**: *Encyclopedia of World Biography*, "Kuang-hsü" (Encyclopedia.com): "On June
  12, 1898, Kuang-hsü decreed that institutional reform and national strengthening were to be a new
  national policy"; "On Sept. 21, 1898, she launched a coup d'etat against him"; Cixi's forces
  "imprisoned Kuang-hsü and executed six of the leading reform advisers"; he "thereafter lived in
  humiliating confinement."
