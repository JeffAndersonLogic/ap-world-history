Unit 4 is the first unit where a topic carries more than one learning objective: 4.2 has two,
4.4 three, 4.5 four, each under its own theme. A story has to answer all of them, not the first.

## The unit in one sentence

**Once ships could cross the ocean and come back, the hemispheres were joined, and what
traveled that road (crops, germs, silver, and enslaved people) changed trade, work, and power
at both ends.**

The shorter version for the wall: **The ocean became a road. What traveled it changed
everyone at both ends.**

Why this spine: it is the CED's own unit summary in plain words. KC-4.1 says transoceanic
voyaging joined the hemispheres and "transformed trade and had a significant social impact";
KC-4.2 says labor, production and social structures changed; KC-4.3 says empires grew and were
"shaped by the diverse populations they incorporated." The spine gives 4.1 and 4.2 the road,
4.3 what traveled it without anyone meaning it to, 4.4 and 4.5 the empires and markets built
along it, 4.6 and 4.7 how people pushed back and were sorted, and 4.8 the argument about what
it all changed and what it did not.

**One correction the spine has to carry: the old world did not stop.** Indian Ocean trade kept
flourishing (KC-4.3.II.A.iii), African slavery continued in its older forms (KC-4.2.II.B), and
Asian states set their own terms (KC-4.3.II.A.i). Unit 4 is about Europe reaching new places,
not about Europe replacing everyone else, and the continuity topics (4.4, 4.5, 4.7, 4.8) are
where that is taught.

## How it connects to what comes before and after

- **From Unit 3 (bridge in, for 4.1's BeReady):** 3.4 hands off with "These empires grew over
  land. The next states learn to project influence across oceans." Unit 2's Topic 2.3 is the
  other retrieval: the monsoon schedule, the compass and astrolabe, and Zheng He. That is the
  context Europe's sea tools came out of.
- **The 1453 link stays qualified.** The Unit 3 map and the deep audit both say Constantinople's
  fall is not the direct cause of the Age of Exploration. 4.1 does not use it; 4.2 owns the
  qualified version.
- **Into Unit 5 (hand-off from 4.8):** by 1750 the oceans were joined and states were stronger
  than ever. Unit 5 asks who those states belong to, and what happens when the work of the
  world moves into factories. 4.8 names that turn in one line and does not teach it (its
  current chapter has a "Where Unit 5 Begins" section that does).

## Topic by topic

### 4.1 Technological Innovations

- **Story spine:** Europe borrowed its sea tools from older worlds, built new ships around them,
  and learned the Atlantic's winds, and that turned the ocean into a road that ran both ways.
- **Reasoning move:** Causation (CED p. 75), with 4.A Contextualization as the suggested skill:
  the context is the older Classical, Islamic and Asian knowledge the tools came from.
- **This topic owns:** the diffusion of knowledge and technology from the Classical, Islamic and
  Asian worlds (compass, astrolabe and astronomical tables, lateen sail); new tools, ship designs
  (caravel, carrack, fluyt) and wind and current knowledge (the volta do mar); the claim that
  together they made transoceanic travel and trade possible and changed its patterns.
- **Must-have "so what":** the return voyage. Every tool answers "how do you get home across an
  ocean whose wind does not turn around?"
- **Mention only as a bridge:** Dias, Columbus and da Gama, as proof the tools worked; who paid
  and why (4.2); printing and the Waldseemüller map (not in the CED text).
- **Hands to 4.2:** "Ships could now go and come back. But a voyage cost a fortune. Who paid for
  them, and what did they want?"

### 4.2 Exploration: Causes and Events

- **Story spine:** Rulers paid for voyages because they wanted the trade, and every voyage that
  paid made the next one easier to fund.
- **Reasoning move:** Causation (CED p. 75), across both objectives: the role of states (LO B)
  and the economic causes *and effects* of exploration (LO C). Suggested skill 5.B Making
  Connections, which the CED's own sample activity models (Asian technology from earlier units
  connected to European exploration).
- **This topic owns:** state sponsorship; Portugal's route around Africa and its trading-post
  empire (KC-4.1.III.A); Spain's sponsorship of Columbus and the Atlantic and Pacific voyages
  that followed (KC-4.1.III.B); the English, French and Dutch northern crossings looking for
  another way to Asia (KC-4.1.III.C).
- **Must-have "so what":** each voyage answers "who paid, what did they want, and what did they
  get?" The northern crossings get equal standing; today they are the branch with the least
  coverage.
- **Mention only as a bridge:** the conquests of the Mexica and Inca, smallpox and encomienda
  (4.3 and 4.4); joint-stock companies (4.5). The 1453 story, only in its qualified form.
- **Hands to 4.3:** "Columbus was looking for a route to Asia. What he started was an exchange
  of living things nobody planned."

### 4.3 Columbian Exchange

- **Story spine:** The ships carried more than anyone meant them to, and the plants, animals and
  germs they carried remade populations on both sides of the ocean.
- **Reasoning move:** Causation (CED p. 75): the causes of the exchange and its effects on both
  hemispheres. Suggested skill 3.B Claims and Evidence in Sources.
- **This topic owns:** the exchange itself (KC-4.1.V); disease vectors and diseases and their
  catastrophic effect on Indigenous populations (V.A); American foods as staples in Afro-Eurasia,
  and cash crops on plantations with coerced labor (V.B); Afro-Eurasian crops and animals carried
  to the Americas, and foods carried by enslaved Africans, okra and rice (V.C); the nutritional
  benefit in Afro-Eurasia (V.D).
- **Must-have "so what":** every item answers "what did this do to the people at the other end?"
- **Mention only as a bridge:** the labor systems and slavery themselves (4.4); silver (4.5);
  casta (4.7). The chain "disease emptied the Americas, so Europeans enslaved Africans" is **not**
  taught as a single cause (the deep audit says qualify it; the 4.3 and 4.8 chapters already
  argue against it).
- **Hands to 4.4:** "Disease emptied the land of workers, and new crops needed them. Who would do
  the work, and who would rule?"

### 4.4 Maritime Empires Established

- **Story spine:** Europeans built empires from the sea, but much of the world kept its own
  rules: some things changed fast and some barely moved.
- **Reasoning move:** Continuity and Change (CED p. 75), across three objectives: state building
  (LO E), economic and labor systems (LO F) and systems of slavery (LO G). Suggested skill 2.A
  Sourcing and Situation.
- **This topic owns:** European trading posts in Africa and Asia, and the new maritime empires
  driven by political, religious and economic rivalries (KC-4.3.II.A.i, II.C); Asian states
  limiting European trade (Ming China and Tokugawa Japan are the CED's examples); African states
  growing through the trade, Asante and Kongo (II.A.ii); Indian Ocean networks continuing to
  flourish with Asian merchants (II.A.iii); colonial labor systems, the old (mit'a) and the new
  (chattel slavery, indentured servitude, encomienda, hacienda) (KC-4.2.II.D); slavery in Africa
  continuing in its traditional forms, and the plantation economy's demand for enslaved labor in
  the Americas (KC-4.2.II.B, II.C).
- **Must-have "so what":** every case is sorted as continuity or change, and why.
- **Mention only as a bridge:** joint-stock companies and the VOC (4.5); silver's global flow
  (4.5); casta (4.7).
- **Hands to 4.5:** "The empires exist. How did rulers make them pay, and what did the money do
  to the people inside them?"

### 4.5 Maritime Empires Maintained and Developed

- **Story spine:** Rulers built economic tools to keep their empires paying, and the money,
  goods and people those tools moved reshaped work, families and faith.
- **Reasoning move:** Continuity and Change (CED p. 75), across four objectives: economic
  strategies (LO H), networks of exchange (LO I), society (LO J) and belief systems (LO K).
  Suggested skill 3.A Claims and Evidence in Sources.
- **This topic owns:** mercantilism and joint-stock companies (KC-4.1.IV.C); economic disputes
  turning into rivalries, with the CED's Muslim–European rivalry in the Indian Ocean and the
  Moroccan conflict with Songhai (KC-4.3.III.ii); the Atlantic trading system and the global
  flow of silver to Asia (KC-4.1.IV, IV.D.i); peasant and artisan labor intensifying (KC-4.2.II.A);
  gender and family restructuring, including in Africa (KC-4.2.III.C); cultural mixing in the
  Atlantic world (KC-4.1.IV.D.ii); religions spreading, colliding and blending (KC-4.1.VI).
- **Must-have "so what":** each objective is answered, not just the economic one.
- **Mention only as a bridge:** the mit'a as a labor system (4.4; 4.5 may use Potosí as silver's
  source, not as its main story). Tondibi's battle itself (3.1); 4.5 owns its economic motive.
- **Hands to 4.6:** "States got richer and reached further. Not everyone accepted it."

### 4.6 Internal and External Challenges to State Power

- **Story spine:** The harder states pushed to expand and centralize, the harder people at the
  local level pushed back.
- **Reasoning move:** Causation (CED p. 75): the effects of state power, with 4.B
  Contextualization (explain how a development is situated in a broader context) as the
  suggested skill.
- **This topic owns:** local resistance to expanding, centralizing states (KC-4.3.III.iii), with
  the CED's range of examples across Afro-Eurasia and the Americas (Pueblo Revolts, Fronde,
  Cossack revolts, Maratha conflict with the Mughals, Ana Nzinga, Metacom's War); organized
  resistance by enslaved people in the Americas, Maroon societies and resistance in North
  America (KC-5.3.III.C).
- **Must-have "so what":** at least one Afro-Eurasian case beside an American one, so "state
  power" does not read as "colonial power" only.
- **Mention only as a bridge:** rivalries between European states (KC-4.3.III.ii is 4.5's; the
  Unit 4 contract forbids it as a 4.6 target code).
- **Hands to 4.7:** "Some people resisted the new order. Everyone was sorted by it. Who ended up
  where?"

### 4.7 Changing Social Hierarchies

- **Story spine:** Empires decided who could belong and who could rise: new elites appeared, old
  ones were squeezed, and some groups were welcomed while others were pushed out.
- **Reasoning move:** Continuity and Change (CED p. 75). Suggested skill 3.D Claims and Evidence
  in Sources.
- **This topic owns:** states accommodating or suppressing diversity, the Mughal and Ottoman
  cases and the CED's examples (Jews expelled from Spain and Portugal and accepted in the Ottoman
  Empire; restrictions on Han Chinese under the Qing; the varying status of women in the Ottoman
  Empire) (KC-4.3.I.B); new elites, the Qing transition and the casta system (KC-4.2.III.A);
  existing elites whose power rose and fell against stronger monarchs, Ottoman timars, Russian
  boyars, European nobility (KC-4.2.III.B).
- **Must-have "so what":** every case is continuity or change in who held status, and why.
- **Mention only as a bridge:** Atlantic slavery regimes as a comparison set (4.4).
- **Hands to 4.8:** "Trade, labor and hierarchy all moved. Which change mattered most, and what
  stayed the same?"

### 4.8 Continuity and Change from 1450 to 1750

- **Story spine:** Economic change reached into everyday social life: who worked, who ruled,
  and how families and communities were organized. Argue how much changed, with evidence.
- **Reasoning move:** Continuity and Change (CED p. 75), answering LO N, "Explain how economic
  developments from 1450 to 1750 affected social structures over time." Suggested skill 6.C
  Argumentation.
- **This topic owns:** the argument, using Unit 4 evidence already taught. It introduces no new
  required content. The CED lists seven review Key Concepts for it (KC-4.1, II, II.A, KC-4.2,
  II, KC-4.3, III.ii); its lesson data now carries those (corrected 2026-10-11).
- **Hands to Unit 5:** "By 1750 the oceans were joined and states were stronger than ever. Next:
  who do those states belong to?"

primary sources, BeSurreals, several Skill Builders, and most deep-reading chapters (for 4.4, 4.5
and 4.8 the repair added a closing card and left the old sections). The coherence contract only
checks that terms are present, so leftover off-topic material passes it. **None of the items
below has been changed.** Each is fixed inside its own topic's build, as Unit 3's were.

   source file): 4.1 to 4.7 had no learning objective in their CED metadata; 4.6 had one example
   misworded; 4.8's Key Concept list was nine codes the CED does not give 4.8. Recorded here so
   nobody restores the old list.
