# Foundations Unit Exam

**AP World History: Modern &nbsp;·&nbsp; BeHistorical**
40 multiple-choice questions &nbsp;·&nbsp; 55 minutes suggested

---

## Teacher front matter

### What this exam is

A hybrid, deliberately. The AP exam is entirely stimulus-based multiple choice,
and a Foundations exam built that way would test reading speed on day fifteen of
the course before it tested whether anyone learned the Neolithic. A Foundations
exam built entirely of recall would test nothing the course claims to build. So
this one runs three parts, and each part does a different job.

| Part | Items | Format | What it measures |
|---|---|---|---|
| I | 1 to 18 | Stimulus-based, six sets of three | Working with a source, the national exam's actual format |
| II | 19 to 32 | Traditional stem and four options | Whether the content and its mechanisms are held |
| III | 33 to 40 | Skill identification | Whether a student can recognize the six historical thinking skills done correctly |

Part III is the part that does not exist on the national exam and is the reason
this one is worth giving. Each item lays four sentences beside each other and
asks which is a real causation statement, a real contextualization, a real
comparison. Students who can produce those sentences on an SAQ can almost always
pick them out first, and students who cannot pick them out have never once been
asked to look at the difference directly.

### Content coverage

| Source | Items |
|---|---|
| Foundations 1, Geography and the Neolithic | 4, 5, 6, 19, 20, 21, 22 |
| Foundations 2, Belief Systems | 7, 8, 9, 23, 24, 25, 26, 27, 36 |
| Foundations 3, States and Power | 1, 2, 3, 13, 14, 15, 28, 29, 30, 34 |
| Foundations 4, Trade Networks | 10, 11, 12, 31, 32, 33 |
| Foundations 5, The World at c. 1200 | 16, 17, 18, 35, 37, 40 |
| Foundations 0, the six thinking skills | 38, 39, and all of Part III by design |

Every item traces to a First & 10 reading or to an eBook chapter, and the answer
key names the section. Nothing on this exam tests a fact the course did not
teach.

### How we know: what the stimuli are

Two kinds, and the distinction matters enough to state on the page rather than
bury.

**Genuine primary sources, excerpted and adapted:** Herodotus on the Persian
couriers (Set 1), al-Bakri on the capital of Ghana (Set 3), and Thucydides'
version of Pericles' funeral oration (Set 5). These are real texts, named and
dated on the page the way the national exam names and dates its own. The wording
is adapted for length and reading level, which is standard practice and is
labeled as such.

**Course-constructed data displays:** the bioarchaeological summary (Set 2), the
monsoon calendar and Belitung cargo description (Set 4), the Attica population
estimates (Set 5), and the six-region table (Set 6). These are built from the
Foundations chapters and are labeled as summaries rather than dressed up as
documents somebody wrote. The AP exam uses constructed charts and tables too.

Nothing on this exam is an invented quotation attributed to a real person, and
nothing invents a statistic. Where the underlying scholarship is disputed, the
stimulus says so on the page, because the disputed figures in this course
(Cahokia's population, the mortality of the Plague of Justinian, the size of the
Athenian citizen body) are exactly the ones the chapters hedge, and an exam that
un-hedged them would teach against the reading.

### Distractors

Almost every wrong answer on this exam is a specific misconception the chapters
name in a **Common mistake to avoid** note. That is the design: a student who
picks the Han inventing the civil service examination, or Constantine making
Christianity official in 313, or the Sahara being impassable before the camel,
has told you which note did not land. The answer key names the misconception
behind each distractor, so item analysis is a reteach list rather than a score.

### Scoring note

No penalty for guessing, four options throughout, matching the national exam.
The key runs A 10, B 9, C 10, D 11, with no run longer than one, so there is no
position to pattern-match.

## Questions 1 to 3 refer to the passage below.

> There is nothing mortal that travels faster than these Persian couriers. Men
> and horses are stationed a day's journey apart, a man and a horse for each day
> of the whole journey. Neither snow, nor rain, nor heat, nor darkness of night
> prevents them from completing their appointed stage with the utmost speed. The
> first rider hands his dispatch to the second, the second to the third, and so
> it passes from hand to hand along the whole line.

**Herodotus, *Histories*, Book 8. Greek historian, writing c. 430 BCE. Excerpt, adapted.**

---

**1.** The system Herodotus describes solved which governing problem for the
Achaemenid Empire?

- **(A)** It removed the need for satraps by allowing the king to govern each province directly from the capital.
- **(B)** It standardized language and religion across three continents, reducing the diversity the empire had to manage.
- **(C)** It allowed dispatches to move at the speed of the relay rather than the endurance of one rider, so the center could learn of provincial events and answer them quickly.
- **(D)** It enabled the mass deportation of rebellious populations, the method Persia inherited from the Assyrians.

**2.** A historian evaluating this passage as evidence would most reasonably
note that

- **(A)** because the passage describes a physical system rather than a person, it carries no point of view.
- **(B)** Herodotus held office at the Persian court, which gave him access to royal records.
- **(C)** the passage is unusable, because Herodotus wrote decades after the road was built and never saw it.
- **(D)** Herodotus wrote for a Greek audience about the empire that had invaded Greece, so his account may overstate Persian speed even though archaeologists have traced the road itself.

**3.** Compared with the way Han China solved the same problem of ruling at
distance, the Persian approach shown here depended on

- **(A)** moving information quickly to a center that otherwise left local law, religion and ruling families in place.
- **(B)** a centrally appointed, salaried and rotated officialdom watched by a separate censorate.
- **(C)** extending legal membership in the ruling people to conquered populations.
- **(D)** selecting administrators by written examination on a moral canon.

**4.** The pattern in the table is best explained by which of the following?

- **(A)** Farming populations were smaller, so each individual received a smaller share of the harvest.
- **(B)** A diet narrowed to starchy cereals, denser settlement and long repetitive labor together worsened individual health even as food produced per hectare rose.
- **(C)** Foragers deliberately limited their numbers in order to protect their health.
- **(D)** The shift to agriculture happened during a period of global cooling that reduced the food supply.

**5.** Farming populations grew rapidly despite the pattern shown. The best
explanation is that

- **(A)** farming reduced mortality more than it reduced fertility.
- **(B)** the skeletal evidence is unreliable, so the health decline probably did not happen.
- **(C)** foragers were absorbed into farming populations faster than farmers reproduced.
- **(D)** farming raised both fertility and mortality, and the rise in births, driven by shorter birth intervals for settled women and by cereal weaning foods, outpaced the rise in deaths.

**6.** A student uses this table to argue that agriculture was not an improvement.

- **(A)** the table reports averages, and averages never support a historical claim.
- **(B)** bones cannot record disease, so the indicators listed do not measure health.

**Source A. The monsoon calendar of the Indian Ocean**

|---|---|---|---|

**Source B. The Belitung wreck**

An Arabian-style ship, built with a hull sewn together using rope rather than
nailed, went down off Indonesia in roughly the 830s CE. Its hold carried tens of
thousands of Chinese ceramics packed for sale, together with gold and silver
objects. The wreck was excavated commercially, which recovered the objects and
destroyed the archaeological context, and archaeologists argued bitterly about
it for that reason.

**Description compiled for this course from the Foundations 4 chapter.**

# PART III. Historical Thinking Skills

**Directions.** Each question below places four sentences side by side. All four
may be true. Only one does the thinking move the question names. Choose the one
that performs the skill, not the one that sounds most impressive.

---

**33.** Which of the following is a true **causation** statement about Indian
Ocean trade, rather than a description or a correlation?

- **(A)** The Indian Ocean network was extremely important to premodern trade.
- **(B)** Many of the wealthiest port cities of the premodern world were located on the Indian Ocean.
- **(C)** Because the monsoon reversed in June and again in November, a round-trip voyage took most of a year, so merchants had to overwinter in foreign ports, which produced permanent diaspora communities in every major harbor.
- **(D)** Indian Ocean trade expanded during the same centuries in which Islam spread across the region.

**34.** Which statement explains a **mechanism** rather than naming a feature?

- **(A)** Persia governed its territory through provinces called satrapies.
- **(B)** By leaving local religion, law and ruling families in place, Persia made revolt more costly to a subject people than compliance was, while three separately reporting officials in each province kept any satrap from becoming a rival king.
- **(C)** The Persian Empire stretched from the Indus River to the Aegean Sea.
- **(D)** Persia was an unusually large and unusually diverse empire.

**35.** Which of the following is **contextualization** rather than an
introduction?

- **(A)** Song China was a dynasty in East Asia during a period shaped by Confucian tradition.
- **(B)** The Song dynasty ruled China from 960 to 1279 CE.
- **(C)** A great many important developments took place in Song China.
- **(D)** Driven south of the Huai River in 1127, the Song court found itself governing the most productive rice region in the world, and, having lost its overland trade routes with the north, taxed maritime commerce at ports such as Quanzhou to fund the state.

**36.** Which of the following is a complete **continuity and change** statement
about Buddhism's arrival in China?

- **(A)** Translators rendered Buddhist concepts through existing Daoist vocabulary and Chinese Buddhism grew a literature on filial piety, while the Four Noble Truths and the monastic community remained the center of the tradition.
- **(B)** Buddhism changed a great deal once it reached China.
- **(C)** Chinese Buddhism was entirely different from Indian Buddhism.
- **(D)** Buddhism reached China along the Silk Roads during the first centuries CE.

**37.** Which of the following is a **comparison** rather than a parallel list?

- **(A)** Kilwa was a Swahili port that exported gold and ivory. Cahokia was a Mississippian center that traded shell and copper.
- **(B)** Kilwa and Cahokia were both significant cities before the year 1200.
- **(C)** Both Kilwa and Cahokia grew wealthy at the center of long-distance exchange, but the networks they anchored differed in reach: Kilwa's joined the Indian Ocean system while Cahokia's remained internal to its own hemisphere.
- **(D)** Kilwa was more advanced than Cahokia, because Kilwa traded with Asia and Cahokia did not.

**38.** Which of the following is a defensible **argument** rather than a summary?

- **(A)** Trade networks carried goods, ideas, crops and diseases across Afro-Eurasia before c. 1200.
- **(B)** The non-goods carried along the premodern trade networks had larger long-run consequences than the cargo did, precisely because the cargo was profitable enough to keep the whole system running.
- **(C)** This essay will discuss the three major trade networks of the premodern world.
- **(D)** Trade networks were very important in world history.

**39.** Which of the following is a proper **sourcing** analysis of Ashoka's rock
and pillar edicts?

- **(A)** Because the edicts date from Ashoka's own reign, they are the most reliable possible evidence for what he privately believed.
- **(B)** They are worthless as evidence, because every royal inscription is propaganda.
- **(C)** They should be discounted because they were carved in stone rather than written on paper.
- **(D)** They are royal inscriptions the king commissioned about himself, so they are strong evidence for what a ruler wanted to be seen doing and for the reach of his administration, and weaker evidence for what he actually felt.

**40.** Which statement about the course's c. 1200 starting point is most
defensible?

- **(A)** The boundary is an argument rather than an event: it is drawn through a cluster of developments, and it makes Afro-Eurasian interconnection the given condition, so European dominance becomes something a student has to explain rather than assume.
- **(B)** In 1200 the world became interconnected for the first time.
- **(C)** The year 1200 marks the specific event that began the modern era.
- **(D)** The c. 1200 boundary fits every world region equally well.

## Part I

**1. (C)** &nbsp; *Causation.* Foundations 3, section 01, "How it held."
The relay is the mechanism: a rested rider at each station means the dispatch
moves at the speed of the system rather than the endurance of one man or animal.
**(A)** is the centralization confusion, and it inverts Persia, which delegated
to satraps rather than governing directly. **(B)** is the tolerance misconception
running backwards; Persia deliberately did not standardize language or religion.
**(D)** is the Assyrian contrast the chapter draws, and it is the method Persia
chose against.

**2. (D)** &nbsp; *Sourcing.* Foundations 3, section 01, **How we know**.
The chapter's own note: treat the seven-day claim as a Greek estimate of Persian
speed rather than a measured fact, while accepting the road, which archaeologists
have traced. **(A)** is the belief that a description of a thing has no point of
view. **(B)** is factually wrong; Herodotus was Greek and held no Persian office.
**(C)** is the overcorrection worth naming out loud in review, since students who
learn to distrust sources often overshoot into discarding them.

**3. (A)** &nbsp; *Comparison.* Foundations 3, closing, "Administering distance."
**(B)** is Han China, the other half of the pairing. **(C)** is Rome. **(D)** is
the Sui and Tang examination system, which doubles as the misconception tested
directly in item 23. A student who picks D here and D on 23 has one error, not
two.

**4. (B)** &nbsp; *Causation.* Foundations 1, section 07, "What the skeletons say."
**(A)** and **(C)** invent mechanisms the evidence does not support. **(D)**
reverses the climate story: the Holocene was warmer and steadier, which is what
made cultivation a reasonable bet.

**5. (D)** &nbsp; *Causation.* Foundations 1, section 07, "Why populations grew
anyway," the demographic scissors. **(A)** is the answer students reach for
first and it is backwards. **(B)** is the overcorrection again. **(C)** is not
supported.

**6. (C)** &nbsp; *Argumentation.* Foundations 1, closing, question 3.
The chapter's whole framing: improvement for whom, measured by what, over what
timescale. **(A)**, **(B)** and **(D)** are all methodological objections that
sound sophisticated and are simply wrong, which is deliberate; students who
have learned that "you can question the evidence" often reach for the nearest
skeptical-sounding sentence instead of the strongest objection.

**7. (A)** &nbsp; *Continuity and change.* Foundations 2, section 08, "Islam in
the Sahel." Syncretism visible as town planning. **(B)** and **(C)** are the two
directions students collapse this into, total conversion or total conquest.
**(D)** misses that this is the pattern rather than the exception.

**8. (D)** &nbsp; *Causation.* Foundations 2, section 08, and Foundations 4,
section 03. The state needed people who could read, write and handle contracts
with North African merchants. **(A)**, **(B)** and **(C)** are invented
obligations; none is in the record.

**9. (B)** &nbsp; *Sourcing.* The attribution line on the stimulus states it, and
the chapter's **How we know** note makes the same point. **(A)** is the belief
that physical description needs no scrutiny. **(C)** is factually wrong.
**(D)** is a plausible-sounding overcorrection worth discussing: a hostile or
distant source is still evidence, and part of what it evidences is what the
writer expected instead.

**10. (C)** &nbsp; *Causation.* Foundations 4, section 02, "The geography, which
is really a calendar." The wind built the diaspora communities, which is the
mechanism rather than the feature. **(B)** is physically impossible and is there
as a floor. **(A)** and **(D)** are unsupported.

**11. (A)** &nbsp; *Sourcing and evidence.* Foundations 4, section 02, **How we
know: a wreck is a sealed sample**. **(B)** is contradicted by the stimulus
itself, which says this wreck was excavated commercially, so students who read
the source carefully can eliminate it. **(D)** is the overreach from one sample
to a whole system.

**12. (D)** &nbsp; *Comparison.* Foundations 4, section 01 **useThis** comparison
and section 02. Bulk against high-value, which is the single contrast that
explains most of the difference between the two networks. **(C)** is
contradicted by the stimulus, which describes a ship built in the western Indian
Ocean tradition.

**13. (B)** &nbsp; *Argumentation.* Foundations 3, section 03, "Who was on top."
Both halves have to be held: the democracy was real, and the citizen body was a
minority. **(A)** and **(C)** are the cynical collapse, **(D)** is factually
wrong and is answered by item 15.

**14. (C)** &nbsp; *Sourcing.* Foundations 3, section 03, **How we know**.
Thucydides states that he reconstructed speeches. **(A)** is the transcript
assumption. **(D)** is invented.

**15. (D)** &nbsp; *Comparison.* Foundations 3, closing, "Who may belong," which
the chapter calls the single sharpest contrast available in the unit.

**16. (A)** &nbsp; *Continuity and change, periodization.* Foundations 5,
sections 05 and 07, "The failure mode: assuming change means improvement."
**(C)** is projecting the ending onto the beginning. **(D)** is the isolation
equals backwardness error, tested again from the other side in item 18.

**17. (B)** &nbsp; *Contextualization.* Foundations 5, section 02, "What holds it
up." **(D)** is a chronology trap worth flagging in review: the Mongol sack of
Baghdad is more than fifty years after this snapshot, and a student who picks it
is reading the shadow of an event backwards onto a moment where it has not
happened.

**18. (C)** &nbsp; *Comparison.* Foundations 5, closing, question 2, which the
chapter calls the comparison most easily got wrong in the course. **(D)** is the
error stated plainly. Write "isolated from Afro-Eurasia," never "isolated."

**20. (A)** &nbsp; Foundations 1, section 05, the four things a river supplies.

## Part III

These eight are the items worth reviewing as a block, because a student who
misses several of them has a skill problem rather than a content problem, and
reteaching the content will not fix it.

**33. (C)** &nbsp; *Causation.* **(D)** is the item to spend time on: it is a
correlation dressed as a cause, and it is the single most common way a causal
claim goes wrong. Two things happening in the same centuries is not one causing
the other. **(A)** and **(B)** are description.

**34. (B)** &nbsp; *Causation, mechanism against feature.* **(A)** names the tool
without saying how it worked, which is exactly what the Foundations 3 success
criteria warn against: "not just it helped the ruler but how and why."

**35. (D)** &nbsp; *Contextualization.* Foundations 5, section 07, which uses
almost this pair as its worked example. **(A)** is an introduction posing as
context, naming a background and connecting nothing. **(B)** is a fact.

**36. (A)** &nbsp; *Continuity and change.* **(B)** and **(C)** give change with
no continuity, and **(D)** gives a fact with neither. A complete CCOT statement
has to hold something still.

**37. (C)** &nbsp; *Comparison.* **(A)** is the parallel list, accurate and not a
comparison, which is the distinction Foundations 5 section 07 names. **(D)** is
a comparison that smuggles in the isolation equals backwardness error, so it is
wrong twice over and is worth pointing at directly.

**38. (B)** &nbsp; *Argumentation.* **(A)** is true and undisputable, which is
what disqualifies it. **(C)** announces a topic. If nobody could reasonably
disagree with it, it is not a thesis.

**39. (D)** &nbsp; *Sourcing.* Foundations 2, section 04, **How we know**.
Both clauses belong in the same sentence: strong evidence for what a ruler wanted
to be seen doing, weaker for what he felt. **(B)** is the discard-it
overcorrection, which shows up in this exam three times on purpose.

**40. (A)** &nbsp; *Periodization.* Foundations 5, section 08. **(B)** is the
sentence the chapter quotes as the classic failure, and it is false in both
directions: Afro-Eurasia had been connected for over a thousand years, and the
Americas were not connected in 1200 or for three centuries after.

## Item analysis: what a miss tells you

| If the class missed | Reteach |
|---|---|
| 5, 6 | The demographic scissors. Populations and individuals are different subjects. |
| 20, 34 | Feature against mechanism. This is the highest-value reteach on the exam. |
| 21, 32 | Made possible against caused. Multiple causation. |
| 23, 29 | The examination system's actual chronology, Sui and Tang, expanded Song. |
| 26, 27 | Dates and pace: legalized against official, conquest against conversion. |
| 2, 9, 11, 39 | Sourcing without overcorrecting into discarding the source. |
| 16, 18, 40 | Periodization, and isolation against backwardness. |
| 33, 37 | Correlation posing as cause; parallel list posing as comparison. |

**The overcorrection pattern.** Items 2(C), 6(A) and (B), 9(D), and 39(B) all
offer a skeptical-sounding option that throws the source away. If students are
choosing those, they have learned that sources have bias and have not yet learned
what to do about it. That is a good problem and a specific one, and the fix is
the sentence pattern in the Foundations 2 note on Ashoka: name what the source is
strong evidence for and what it is weak evidence for, in the same sentence.
