First & 10 Reading

Guns Broke the Walls

For centuries a thick wall let a lord say no to a king. Then cannons arrived, and only a few states could afford them. That is how a handful of empires got huge, and why they soon ran into each other.

CausationContextualizationArgumentation

The Story to Hold Onto

A cannon breaks the wall → Only a big treasury can pay for it → Big states grow → Empires meet → Rivalries. Every example in this reading is one link in that chain.

Question for Today

How did guns help a few empires get so big between 1450 and 1750, and what happened when those empires ran into each other?

1

The Idea

A Wall Let a Lord Say No to a King

In Unit 2 you followed the trade networks that connected Afro-Eurasia, and you saw that gunpowder traveled on them. This unit asks a new question: what did states do once they had it?

To see why the answer mattered, start with the problem guns solved. Picture a duke in a stone castle who will not obey the king. The king could send an army, but the walls are thick and tall. He could starve the duke out, which takes months while the king's own army has to be fed. Or he could storm the wall and lose thousands of soldiers. The cost was so high that kings often left the duke alone. The same was true of walled cities and mountain forts. A strong wall let a local lord say no to a faraway ruler.

The most famous walls in the region protected Constantinople. The Theodosian Walls, built in the 400s, had stopped attackers for about a thousand years. Any attacker laying a siege to that city had to either starve it or climb those walls, and for a thousand years nobody had managed either.

AP Thinking, Contextualization

Start with what was already true. Before you explain why guns changed things, say what they replaced: a world where a wall could beat an army.

2

The Cannon

The Cannon Changed the Math

Gunpowder began in China and spread west along the networks of exchange. By the 1400s, rulers could cast huge guns out of bronze and iron. A wall is built tall and thin because that stops ladders and battering rams. A heavy iron ball does not climb the wall. It hits it sideways and breaks it. Sieges that used to take a year began to take weeks.

In 1453 the young Ottoman sultan Mehmed II set out to take Constantinople. A Hungarian engineer named Urban helped build giant guns called bombards. The siege began on 6 April, and the Ottoman guns pounded the walls day after day. On 29 May, about seven weeks later, the city fell. Walls that had held for a thousand years were broken by a new weapon, and people everywhere understood that the old rules had changed.

You can still see what one of those guns looked like. The Dardanelles Gun, cast in 1464, survives in a museum in England. It is huge, and you can tell from its size how much metal and skill it took to make.

AP Thinking, Causation

Name the cause, the mechanism, and the effect. The cause is the cannon. The mechanism is that a wall built to stop things that climb cannot stop things that smash. The effect is that sieges took weeks instead of a year.

3

The Catch

Only a Big State Could Pay for a Cannon

Here is the catch. A duke cannot build a cannon in his backyard. A cannon needs huge amounts of metal, workshops to cast it, a steady supply of gunpowder, trained crews to fire it, and teams of people and animals to drag it to the wall. All of that costs a fortune. Only a state that could tax many people and fill a treasury could pay for it.

So the new weapon did more than help rulers win battles. It helped big, rich rulers beat small, local ones. The same guns pushed outward against neighboring states and inward against rebel nobles and cities that used to get away with saying no. A wall could no longer protect you from your own ruler.

This is how four land empires got huge. The Ottoman Empire grew across Southern Europe, the Middle East, and North Africa. At Chaldiran in 1514, Ottoman firearms and artillery broke a charge by the cavalry of the Safavid Empire in Iran, and the Safavids then built musketeer and artillery forces of their own. In 1526 the founder of the Mughal Empire, Babur, beat a much larger army at Panipat in India with cannons and soldiers carrying matchlock guns. The Qing Dynasty, ruled by the Manchus, used firearms to take China in 1644 and then pushed across the steppe, the grasslands where nomad horsemen had once been nearly unbeatable.

AP Thinking, Causation

The treasury is part of the mechanism. If you only write "they had guns," you leave out why only some states grew. A strong answer says the guns were expensive, so the states that could pay for them got bigger.

4

When Big States Meet

Big States Grew Until They Bumped Into Each Other

Empires that keep growing eventually reach someone else's border. When they did, disputes over power and religion could turn neighbors into a rivalry. The College Board names two examples.

The first is the Safavids and the Mughals. Kandahar was a fortress on the border between their empires, and each wanted it. That is the political side of the dispute. The religious side was real too: the Safavids made Shia Islam the religion of their state, while the Mughal emperors were Sunni Muslims. Historians argue about how much each mattered. A strong answer names both and explains how they fed each other.

The second is Morocco and the Songhai Empire in West Africa. In 1591 Morocco sent a few thousand soldiers across the Sahara with firearms. At Tondibi they destroyed a Songhai army that was many times larger, and the Songhai empire broke apart. It is one of the clearest tests of the whole story: same century, same region, one side with guns and one without.

There is also a twist. Morocco won the battle and could not really govern what it won, and Kandahar kept changing hands even though both sides had cannons. Guns can win a place. They do not run it. Mehmed took Constantinople in seven weeks. Now he had to run it, and that is the next topic.

AP Thinking, Causation

Do not stop at "two states fought." Say what the dispute was about, political, religious, or both, and how it turned neighbors into rivals. Then say what weapons let each side do about it.

BeReady: 10-Second Takeaway

A strong wall once let a lord say no to a king. Cannons broke the walls, as at Constantinople in 1453, and only big states with big treasuries could afford them. So the Ottoman, Safavid, Mughal, and Qing empires got bigger, and when growing empires met, political and religious disputes turned them into rivals, as at Kandahar and Tondibi. Guns could win a place, but they could not run it.
