First & 10 Reading

Big States Got Bigger

Between c. 1450 and c. 1750, a few empires grew bigger than almost any states before them. Gunpowder is part of the reason, but not in the way you might think. A cannon was a weapon, and it was also a bill that only a rich state could pay.

CausationGovernanceArgumentation

Before You Read

Cannons break walls → Only rich states can pay for them → Conquest brings land and taxes → Taxes buy more guns. That loop is the whole topic. Watch for it in every empire.

Reading Target

How and why did a few land-based empires grow so big between c. 1450 and c. 1750, and what happened when growing empires ran into each other?

1

The Old Rule

For a Thousand Years, a Wall Beat an Army

Last unit, you followed gunpowder as cargo. It was a Chinese invention that traveled west along the trade routes, one more thing carried in a merchant's pack. This unit starts with what happened once rulers learned to build it big.

For most of history, the best defense against an army was a wall. Attackers could try ladders, tunnels, or starving a city out, but a strong city behind thick stone walls could often hold out for months. A long attack on a walled place is called a siege.

The most famous walls in the world belonged to Constantinople, the capital of the Byzantine Empire. Its great land walls were built in the 400s. Over the next thousand years, army after army attacked them, and none broke through by land. In 1422 the Ottoman Turks tried, with small cannons, and they failed too.

AP Thinking, Causation

Find what changed. The same attacker hit the same walls in 1422 and in 1453. It failed the first time and won the second. When you explain a cause, look for the one thing that was different.

2

The Turn

1453: The Walls Fall, and the Bill Comes Due

In 1453 the Ottoman sultan Mehmed II came back with something new: giant cannons. The biggest were called bombards, bronze guns so heavy that teams of oxen had to drag them to the city. They fired huge stone balls that cracked walls no earlier weapon could break. After about seven weeks, the walls were broken and the city was his. It became the Ottoman capital, Istanbul.

Here is the part that matters most. A cannon like that is not just a weapon. It is a bill. A ruler needed tons of metal, a foundry to cast the gun, experts who knew how to make and fire it, gunpowder by the ton, animals and roads to move it, and soldiers paid to protect it. According to a Byzantine writer of the time, the engineer who built Mehmed's biggest guns had first offered his services to the Byzantine emperor. The emperor could not afford him.

So gunpowder did not help every state equally. It rewarded whoever already had the most money. A big state with a large treasury, the money a government collects and spends, could buy guns, use them to take more land, tax that land, and buy more guns. Big states got bigger.

AP Thinking, Causation

Name the mechanism, not just the weapon. "Gunpowder made empires big" is too simple. The mechanism is a loop: guns take land, the land pays taxes, and the taxes buy more guns. Guns were necessary, but money is what made them work.

3

Four Empires

One Loop, Four Empires

That loop helps explain how four great land empires grew between c. 1450 and c. 1750. Each one has a moment when gunpowder did the work.

The Ottoman Empire used its guns to take Constantinople, then spread across Southeastern Europe, the Middle East, and North Africa. In South Asia, a ruler named Babur used field guns and handguns to win the Battle of Panipat in 1526, which opened northern India to the Mughal Empire. In Iran, the Safavid Empire lost a major battle to Ottoman guns at Chaldiran in 1514, and its later ruler Shah Abbas I built his own units of musket soldiers and cannons.

In East Asia, the Manchus, a people from the northeast of China, conquered China in 1644 and founded the Qing Dynasty. Their armies used cannons, and by the 1750s the Qing had pushed their rule into Mongolia, Tibet, and Central Asia. Russia grew across Siberia in the same centuries.

AP Thinking, Comparison

Same loop, different places. Four empires in very different lands all grew the same way: a rich state bought guns, and the guns won land. Gunpowder was not the only reason. These empires also needed horses, allies, and good government. But it is the reason they share.

4

The Twist

Growing Empires Ran Into Each Other

Empires that keep growing eventually run into each other, or into a neighbor with something they want. A long-running competition between two states is a rivalry. Political disputes, over land and power, and religious disputes, over beliefs and who has the right to rule, both turned neighbors into rivals.

The Safavids and the Mughals fought for more than fifty years over Kandahar, a fortress city on the main road between Iran and India. Both empires were Muslim, and historians see this fight as political: whoever held Kandahar controlled the road. The city changed hands four times. In the 1650s the Mughals brought their cannons three times and still could not take it back. Guns did not always win.

In West Africa, the Songhai Empire had grown rich on the gold and salt of the Sahara, the same trade you studied in Topic 2.4. The sultan of Morocco claimed to be the rightful ruler of all Muslims and demanded the money from Songhai's salt mines. Songhai refused. In 1590 Morocco sent an army of a few thousand soldiers with guns across the Sahara. In 1591, at Tondibi, near the Songhai capital of Gao, they destroyed a Songhai army many times larger. Songhai never recovered as an empire.

AP Thinking, Causation

A rivalry needs a reason. Do not stop at who fought whom. Name the dispute: at Kandahar it was political, control of a fortress and a road; at Tondibi it was political and religious, money and the claim to rule all Muslims. Then say what the guns did, and where they were not enough.

BeReady: 10-Second Takeaway

Cannons could break the old walls, and only big, rich states could afford cannons, so big states got bigger. That loop, guns take land and land pays for guns, helped the Ottoman, Safavid, Mughal, and Qing empires grow between c. 1450 and c. 1750. As they grew, they ran into rivals. At Kandahar, the Safavids and Mughals fought over a fortress and a road, and Mughal guns could not take it back. At Tondibi in 1591, Morocco's guns destroyed the Songhai army.
