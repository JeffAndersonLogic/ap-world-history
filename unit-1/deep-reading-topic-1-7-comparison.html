Topic 1.7  ·  Deep Reading  ·  AP World History: Modern

The Same Four Questions

Six regions, none of them in contact with all the others, every one of them facing the same problems: how to take resources, how to make taking them feel legitimate, and how to hold together people who had no particular reason to obey. This chapter puts the answers side by side, and then shows you exactly what a comparison sentence has to contain before it earns anything.

01

What Comparison Actually Is

The skill  ·  Category, evidence, reasoning

Comparison is not putting two things next to each other. It is choosing a category, showing where two cases sit inside it, and explaining what produced the gap between them. Skip the category and you have a list; skip the explanation and you have a description.

The category comes first

"Compare Song China and Mali" is not answerable as it stands, because everything is different about them and so is everything about any two societies. The question becomes answerable the moment you fix a category: compare how each funded its government, or how each recruited officials, or how each used religion to justify rule. A category is a single question you ask of both cases, and the whole comparison lives inside it.

Four categories cover almost everything Unit 1 asks. Extraction: what does the state take, and from whom? Legitimacy: why should anyone obey it? Administration: how does it govern people its rulers will never see? Social order: who is on top, who is excluded, and can anyone move? Sections 02, 03 and 04 walk the six regions through the first, second and fourth of those, with administration folded into the first because the two are so tightly linked.

The two halves, and the part that earns the point

A full comparison has three components, and most student answers have two. The claim: within the chosen category, here is a similarity or a difference. The evidence: a specific, named, dated fact from each case, not a generality. The reasoning: the explanation of why the similarity or difference existed. That third component is what the success criteria mean by explaining rather than only naming, and it is the one most often missing.

Here is the same comparison written twice. Weak: "Song China and Europe both had systems of government, but Song China was more centralized than Europe." That has no specific evidence and no explanation, and every word of it could be written by someone who had studied neither. Strong: "Both faced armed men who could seize local power, but the Song answered with salaried officials selected by anonymous written examination and rotated between posts, while European kings answered by granting land to warriors in exchange for service. The difference exists because the Song inherited a functioning tax system that could pay officials in cash, while post-Carolingian rulers had land and almost no revenue, so land was the only currency available to them."

Notice what the strong version does. One category, government's relationship to armed subordinates. Specific evidence from each side. And a causal explanation that names a prior condition, the presence or absence of a cash tax system, which is why the two societies took different paths. That structure is reusable for any pair in this unit, and the rest of this chapter exists to give you the material to fill it with.

Common mistake to avoid

"Because of geography" is not an explanation, it is the place where an explanation should go. Geography never causes anything directly; it changes what is cheap, what is possible and what is worth doing, and the sentence has to say which. Not "Mali was rich because of geography," but "Mali's towns sat where the river routes met the desert crossings, so gold moving north and salt moving south both passed through territory it policed, and it could tax traffic it neither produced nor transported." Same idea, one more step, and the step is the whole answer.

Use this in your answer
Strongest tool, with its mechanism
The three-part comparison. Claim within a named category, specific evidence from each case, and a causal explanation of the gap. If your sentence contains a similarity or difference and stops, add the word "because" and finish it; that addition is usually the difference between a described comparison and a scored one.
Best evidence of exclusion or limit
The most common structural failure is comparing a mechanism in one region against a generality in the other, for example Song examinations against "European kings had power." Both sides need evidence of the same grain.
Comparison hook
This section is the tool itself, so use it on the pairs in sections 02 to 04 rather than looking for a pairing here.

Terms to use precisely

Category of comparison
The single question asked of both cases, such as how each state raised revenue, without which a comparison is only a list.
Claim
The statement that two cases are similar or different within the chosen category.
Specific evidence
A named, dated, concrete fact from each case; "the exam sealed candidates’ names from 992" rather than "China had exams."
Reasoning
The explanation of why the similarity or difference existed, which is the component the success criteria single out and students most often omit.
Mechanism
How a tool of rule actually worked, step by step, which is what makes evidence explanatory rather than decorative.
02

Extraction: Six Answers to One Question

c. 1200 to c. 1450  ·  What each state took, and how

Every state must take resources from people who would rather keep them. What it takes, and how, is determined less by culture than by what can actually be measured, moved and defended in that particular place.

The six answers side by side

Song China taxed land, taxed commerce, and ran monopolies on salt, tea and wine, assessed and collected by salaried officials working from written registers. It could do this because it had an examined bureaucracy, a literate population, paper records and, from 1024, paper money. It is the most administratively capable state in the unit and its revenue mix looks startlingly modern.

Dar al-Islam, having no single state, extracted through whichever sultanate held a given territory: land revenue assigned to officers as iqta rather than paid as salary, customs on the enormous transit trade, and the jizya on non-Muslim subjects. The Delhi Sultanate and the Mamluks both paid soldiers with revenue assignments because neither had a cash-tax apparatus deep enough to pay armies directly.

South and Southeast Asia split by geography, which is why the topic pairs them. Land states, Vijayanagara and the Khmer, taxed the harvest and therefore had to build irrigation and command labor. Sea states, Srivijaya, Majapahit and Melaka, taxed cargo passing a chokepoint, which required a harbor, an anti-piracy patrol and a legal system foreign merchants trusted, and required almost no farmland at all.

The Americas gives the clearest contrast in the unit. The Mexica took goods: a defeated city kept its ruler and delivered cloth, cacao, feathers and maize on a schedule. The Inca took labor: the mit'a obligation levied work on communities, provisioned from state storehouses, because an empire with no coinage, no markets to speak of and no wheeled transport across 2,500 miles of mountains could not haul bulk tribute to a capital.

Africa taxed movement. Mali and the Hausa cities taxed the trans-Saharan traffic in gold and salt, Kilwa and the Swahili ports taxed what crossed the ocean, Great Zimbabwe sold plateau gold into that same network while its internal wealth was measured in cattle, and Ethiopia granted the produce of land, as gult, to nobles and monasteries in exchange for service.

Europe had the weakest extraction of all six, and everything else about it follows. With no professional revenue service, kings lived off their own estates, customs duties and whatever an assembly of nobles, clergy and townsmen would grant, which is why Parliament and the Estates General exist.

The pattern worth naming

Read the six together and a rule appears: a state extracts what it can measure and move. Where an administration could count households and keep records, it taxed production directly, as in Song China. Where distances were vast and there was no currency, it took labor, as the Inca did. Where goods flowed through a bottleneck, it taxed the flow, as Mali, Melaka and Kilwa did. And where the state had neither records nor a bottleneck nor spare cash, it paid its servants in the revenue of land itself, which is what Europe, the Delhi Sultanate and Ethiopia all independently arrived at.

That last convergence is the most useful single observation in this chapter, because it is a similarity nobody expects. European feudal grants, the Islamic iqta and the Ethiopian gult are three unconnected societies solving the same problem the same way: pay your soldiers and officials with the income of land because you cannot pay them in coin. They differ in the details that matter, since the iqta was designed to be transferable and the European fief became hereditary, but the underlying logic is identical, and a student who names all three in one sentence has demonstrated exactly the kind of cross-regional thinking this unit is for.

How we know: revenue systems leave the best records

We know more about taxation than about almost anything else in this period, and the reason is mundane. A state that collects has to write down who owes what, and those documents get kept, copied and archived because someone will need them next year. Song tax and monopoly registers, the tribute lists in the Codex Mendoza, Inca khipu recording storehouse contents, and English manorial accounts all survive for the same reason. It also means our picture is tilted toward what states counted, which is worth remembering when a region looks poorly documented: sometimes that means less was happening, and sometimes it only means fewer people were writing it down.

Use this in your answer
Strongest tool, with its mechanism
The measure-and-move rule. The mechanism is that extraction methods are limited by what a state can count and transport, so a bureaucracy with written records taxes production, a state without currency across long distances takes labor, a state on a chokepoint taxes transit, and a state with none of those pays its servants with the revenue of land because it has nothing else to give them.
Best evidence of exclusion or limit
Every method carries its own failure. Tribute buys speed and no loyalty; labor levies require constant provisioning; transit tolls vanish if a rival port undercuts you; land grants become hereditary and hollow out the ruler who made them.
Comparison hook
The strongest pairing in this section is the Mexica against the Inca, because the two are close enough in scale and date that the difference cannot be explained by development, only by terrain and technology.

Terms to use precisely

Extraction
What a state takes from its subjects and how; the first question to ask of any state and the one that shapes the rest of its institutions.
Revenue assignment
Paying an official or soldier with the income of a district instead of a salary; the iqta, the European fief and the Ethiopian gult are three independent versions.
Tribute
Goods owed on a schedule by a subordinate community that continues to govern itself, the Mexica system and the cheapest form of empire.
Labor tax
An obligation of work rather than goods, the Inca mit'a, suited to a state without currency, markets or bulk transport.
Transit toll
Taxation of goods passing through a controlled point, which requires security and reputation rather than administration over a population.
03

Legitimacy: Why Anyone Obeyed

c. 1200 to c. 1450  ·  Six justifications for power

No state in this unit had enough soldiers to compel a majority of its subjects. Every one of them therefore needed most people to obey without being forced, and the story each told about why it should be obeyed is the most revealing thing about it.

What each state claimed

Song China claimed the Mandate of Heaven, and layered Neo-Confucianism on top of it. The Neo-Confucian move is the sophisticated one: it placed social hierarchy inside the structure of the universe, so that a son's obedience to his father and a subject's to the emperor were the same act, and every household rehearsed the political order daily without an official present.

Islamic states claimed legitimacy through the caliph's investiture and through visible defense and patronage of the faith. After 945 the caliph had no power at all, which is exactly why the arrangement is so revealing: a Turkic commander with an army needed something an army cannot supply, and a caliph with nothing else could supply precisely that.

South and Southeast Asian states made the ruler the pivot of the sacred order. Vijayanagara's kings endowed temples that became landholders, lenders and employers in the king's name. Khmer rulers built the greatest temple complex on earth around a royal cult. Theravada kings in Ayutthaya and Sukhothai ruled as the realm's greatest makers of merit, which is a claim that can be publicly demonstrated by building a monastery and cannot easily be argued with.

The Mexica claimed that the sun required human hearts and that they were the people charged with supplying them, which made military success and religious duty the same thing and made the public sacrifice of captives, watched by delegations from subject cities, into an argument and a threat at once. The Inca claimed the Sapa Inca was the son of the sun, and paired that with reciprocity: the state fed and supplied the workers it summoned, so the tax arrived looking like an obligation between relatives.

African states split instructively. Mali's rulers and the Hausa elite drew legitimacy from Islam and from the connections it opened, of which Mansa Musa's hajj in 1324 is the loudest example. Ethiopia's Solomonic dynasty claimed descent from Solomon and the Queen of Sheba, which required recognition from nobody outside Ethiopia. Great Zimbabwe took the trade and kept its own religious tradition.

Europe is the outlier and should be used as one. Its rulers claimed to rule by God's will, and the institution that spoke for God was a separate corporation with its own law, courts, land and revenue, which could excommunicate a king and release his subjects from their oaths. Alone among these regions, Europe's source of legitimacy could turn on the ruler and survive the fight.

The two families of answer

Sort them and there are essentially two designs. In the first, the legitimating institution is inside the state: Confucian scholars were the Song administration, Theravada monks depended on royal donation, the Inca sun cult was staffed by the state, and the Ethiopian church was written into the dynasty's own founding story. In the second, it is outside: the caliph invested sultans he did not control, and the pope crowned and excommunicated kings he did not obey.

The consequences are opposite and predictable. An internal legitimator is reliable and cannot restrain the ruler, so those states could be strong and could also become tyrannical without institutional check. An external legitimator is a constraint and a rival, which is why European monarchs spent centuries fighting the papacy and why sultans found it convenient to keep a powerless caliph in a palace in Cairo. If you need one sentence about why European political development diverged, this is a better candidate than anything about culture: the institution that legitimized power in Europe was never absorbed by it.

Use this in your answer
Strongest tool, with its mechanism
Internal versus external legitimation. The mechanism is that when the institution conferring legitimacy is staffed and funded by the state, it reliably supports the ruler and can never restrain him, while an institution with independent land, law and revenue can withhold legitimacy, which turns it into a permanent constraint and a permanent rival.
Best evidence of exclusion or limit
Legitimacy stories are claims, not descriptions. The Mandate of Heaven explained a dynasty's fall only after it had fallen, and Persian-style tolerance or Inca reciprocity were policies that worked until the moment a revolt showed how much of the obedience had been calculation.
Comparison hook
The sharpest available pairing is caliph against pope: two external legitimators facing the same pressure, one captured and kept as an ornament, the other outlasting the rulers who fought it.

Terms to use precisely

Legitimacy
The reason subjects accept a state’s authority without being forced, which every state in this unit needed because none could compel a majority.
Mandate of Heaven
The Chinese claim that Heaven grants rule to the just and withdraws it from the unjust, which explains a dynasty’s fall only in retrospect.
Investiture
A superior authority formally conferring office, as the caliph did on a sultan and the pope on an emperor, which makes the conferrer indispensable.
Divine descent
The claim that a ruling line descends from a sacred figure, used by the Ethiopian Solomonic dynasty to make its independence a matter of ancestry.
Merit-making
The Theravada practice by which a king’s temple building publicly demonstrated the moral standing that justified his rule.
04

Social Order: Who Was on Top, and Could Anyone Move

c. 1200 to c. 1450  ·  Hierarchy, exclusion, mobility

Every society here was steeply unequal. What differs is the principle that fixed a person's place, and whether anything at all could change it, and that difference is where the most sophisticated comparisons in this unit live.

What fixed a person's position

In Song China the qualification for power was formally education, which is genuinely unusual: a merchant's son could sit the examination. What fixed position in practice was the twenty unpaid years of study the examination required, which only landholding families could fund. So the principle was achievement and the outcome was inherited advantage, and being able to say both at once is the mark of a strong answer.

In Dar al-Islam the fundamental legal division was religious: Muslim and dhimmi, with the jizya as the boundary marker. Within the Muslim community, a mamluk purchased as a child could rise to rule Egypt, and the son of an enslaved concubine was born free and legitimate, so the hierarchy was steep but startlingly permeable in specific directions.

In South Asia position was fixed by birth into a jati, enforced not by the state but by the community through marriage, occupation and purity rules, with untouchability as the sharpest exclusion in the unit. Bhakti devotion challenged it directly and did not dislodge it. This is the least mobile order here, and notably it survived three centuries of rule by a state that had no stake in it.

In the Americas, Mexica society ranked nobles above commoners with real if limited mobility through military capture and the merchant guilds, and its enslaved status was not hereditary. Inca society ranked the Inca core above incorporated local elites above everyone else, and moved entire communities under mitma while removing chosen women from theirs for state service.

In Africa, position generally ran through lineage, age and clientage rather than through a legal caste order, and cattle in the southern plateau societies were the visible measure of a following. Islam added a second axis in the Sahel and on the coast, where the Muslim urban and commercial elite was distinct from the unconverted countryside.

In Europe the three orders of those who pray, fight and work presented hierarchy as divinely appointed function. Serfdom bound peasants to land, and Jewish communities were excluded from guilds and landholding, required to wear distinguishing dress after 1215, expelled from England in 1290 and France in 1306, and massacred during the plague years.

The one thing that actually moved a hierarchy

Across all six regions, the mechanism that produced real change in a person's position was not a philosophy, a revolt or a decree. It was scarcity. Western European serfdom dissolved because plague mortality made labor scarce and land abundant, so a peasant could walk to a manor that would take him and legislation that tried to freeze wages failed. The same shock in eastern Europe hardened serfdom instead, because peasants could not move and lords held more leverage over weaker monarchies.

That contrast is the most valuable single item in this chapter for a causation question, because it disposes of the idea that a cause has an effect. The plague did not have an effect on serfdom; it had opposite effects in two regions, and which one you got depended on whether there was anywhere for a peasant to go. When you are asked to explain a difference, the answer is very often not a difference in the shock but a difference in the structure the shock landed on.

Common mistake to avoid

Do not build a comparison out of ranking societies by how well they treated women or minorities, and do not soften a hierarchy because the alternative sounds harsh. Both moves lose points, and for the same reason: they replace an analysis with a verdict. The productive version names the specific rule and its specific consequence. Not "Islamic society treated women better than European society," but "Islamic law let a woman own property in her own name, keep it in marriage and inherit a fixed share set at half a man's, while a wife's property in much of Europe passed to her husband's control at marriage." Two rules, both stated, and the comparison is now something a reader can check.

Use this in your answer
Strongest tool, with its mechanism
Structure decides what a shock produces. The mechanism is that identical pressure, mass mortality, produces opposite results depending on whether the underlying structure gives the pressed party an exit: western European peasants could move and their obligations dissolved, eastern European peasants could not and their obligations tightened.
Best evidence of exclusion or limit
Mobility in every one of these societies was exceptional rather than normal. A mamluk could become sultan and almost no one else could become anything, and naming the exception without naming its rarity overstates the case.
Comparison hook
The strongest pairing here is Song China's formal openness against South Asian jati: one society made education the qualification for power and still concentrated it in one class, the other made birth the qualification and enforced it without the state's help at all.

Terms to use precisely

Social mobility
The possibility of changing one’s inherited position, which existed in narrow specific channels in most of these societies and almost nowhere as a general rule.
Ascribed status
A position fixed at birth, as with jati in South Asia or the three estates in Europe, as against status earned by achievement.
Exclusion
A rule barring a defined group from something, such as women from the Song examination or Jews from European guilds; the specific rule is what makes it evidence.
Scarcity
The condition under which the value of labor rises, which is the one force in this unit that reliably changed the position of ordinary people.
Structure
The existing arrangement a shock lands on, which explains why the same plague dissolved serfdom in the west and hardened it in the east.
05

Writing It Without Collapsing

The paragraph  ·  Four failures and the fix for each

A comparison collapses in four predictable ways, and every one of them is visible in the first sentence. Learn to spot them in your own draft and the rest of the work is just having read the chapters.

The four failures

The list. "Song China had a civil service exam. Mali had trans-Saharan trade." Both true, no category, so there is nothing being compared. The fix is to name the question you are asking of both: how did each state get the people or the money it needed? Now the two facts are answers to the same question and can be set against each other.

The ranking. "Song China was more advanced than Europe." This substitutes a judgment for an analysis, and it is also usually wrong in a way a reader will notice, since the comparison depends entirely on which category you pick. The fix is to convert the ranking into a difference with a cause: more centralized, and here is the prior condition that produced the centralization.

The mismatched grain. "The Song exam sealed candidates' names from 992 and recopied papers from 1015, while in Europe the nobles were powerful." One side has a mechanism, the other has a mood. The fix is to raise the weaker side: European kings granted land to warriors in exchange for military service, so their servants held hereditary estates rather than transferable posts. Now both sides are the same size.

The stopped explanation. "The difference exists because of geography," or "because of their different cultures," or "because of their religions." Each of those is a category, not a cause. The fix is one more step: geography changed what was cheap or possible, so name what. Culture is a set of practices, so name the practice. Religion did something specific, so name what it did.

The paragraph, assembled

Build it in four sentences. First, name the category and state the claim. Second, give the evidence from case one, specific, named, dated. Third, give the evidence from case two, at the same level of detail. Fourth, explain the difference by naming a prior condition that differed, and say how that condition produced the outcome.

Worked example, and notice that all of it comes from earlier chapters. Category and claim: both the Mexica and the Inca ran large empires assembled by conquest in the fifteenth century, but they extracted completely different things from their subjects. Evidence one: the Triple Alliance left a defeated city its own ruler, gods and government and fixed a tribute quota in cloth, cacao and maize, collected on a schedule. Evidence two: the Inca levied the mit'a labor obligation on communities, fed the workers from state storehouses, resettled populations under mitma and spread Quechua as an administrative language. Reasoning: the difference follows from what each empire could physically move, since central Mexico is compact and easy to march across so bulk goods could be carried to a capital, while the Inca faced 2,500 miles of vertical terrain with no wheeled transport and no coinage, which makes hauling tribute absurd and marching workers to the job sensible.

Four sentences, one category, two mechanisms, one causal explanation. It is not a matter of writing talent. It is a matter of knowing what each state actually did, which is what the six chapters before this one are for.

How we know: hold your evidence to the same standard on both sides

One habit worth carrying beyond this course. When you compare two societies, ask what kind of source each claim rests on, because it is easy to compare a well-documented case against a poorly documented one and mistake the gap in the records for a gap in the history. Europe in this period has merchant archives with a hundred and fifty thousand letters in a single collection; the Wangara traders who ran the trans-Saharan commerce left nothing comparable. That does not mean their business was simpler. It means the paper survived in Tuscany and did not survive in the Sahel, and an honest comparison says so rather than quietly treating silence as absence.

Use this in your answer
Strongest tool, with its mechanism
The four-sentence comparison paragraph. Category and claim, evidence from case one, evidence from case two at the same grain, then a causal explanation naming a prior condition that differed. Check your draft against the four failures before you check anything else, because all four are visible in the opening sentence.
Best evidence of exclusion or limit
The paragraph is only as good as the mechanisms in it. If you cannot state how a tool of rule worked, no amount of structure will produce a strong comparison, which is why the fix for a weak draft is usually rereading a chapter rather than rewriting a sentence.
Comparison hook
Use the closing cards below as models. Each is one category, two mechanisms and one causal explanation, in that order.

Terms to use precisely

Category error
Comparing two facts that answer different questions, which produces a list rather than a comparison.
Grain
The level of detail in evidence; a mechanism on one side and a generality on the other is the most common structural weakness in student comparisons.
Causal explanation
A statement of the prior condition that produced a difference, and of how it produced it; the component the success criteria explicitly require.
Survivorship bias
Mistaking what happened to be preserved for what happened, which distorts comparisons between well-documented and poorly documented regions.
Periodization
Attention to whether two things being compared actually overlap in time, since a difference between 1250 and 1450 may be change over time rather than regional variation.

Four Finished Comparisons

These are models, not summaries. Each one is a single category, two mechanisms at matching grain, and an explanation of the gap, in that order. Read one, then cover it and rebuild it from memory with a different pair of regions; that exercise is worth more than rereading the whole chapter.

Extraction

The Mexica took goods and the Inca took work, and the terrain decided which

Both empires were assembled by conquest in the fifteenth century and both ruled millions. The Triple Alliance fixed tribute quotas on defeated cities, appointed collectors and left the local ruler in place, so its income arrived as cloth, cacao and maize delivered on schedule by societies that continued to govern themselves. The Inca levied the mit'a obligation of rotating labor on communities, provisioned the workers from state storehouses, resettled whole populations under mitma and spread Quechua as an administrative language. The difference exists because of what each empire could move and measure: central Mexico is compact and marchable, so goods could reach a capital, while the Andes run 2,500 miles of vertical terrain with no wheeled transport and no coinage, where hauling bulk tribute is absurd and summoning workers to the work is not.

Legitimacy

Song scholars were inside the state; the Latin Church was outside it

Both supplied the framework that made an unequal social order feel like the order of the universe. Neo-Confucianism placed hierarchy inside cosmic principle and made filial obedience in every household a rehearsal of obedience to the emperor, and the men who taught it were the same men who staffed the administration. The Latin Church collected a universal tithe, ran its own courts under canon law, held a large share of Europe's land, monopolized literacy, and could place a kingdom under interdict and release subjects from their oaths. The difference exists because the Church built an independent hierarchy during centuries when no western European state was strong enough to absorb it, while Chinese religious institutions never developed an authority the throne did not already contain. That is why a pope could bring a king to terms and no Confucian academy could.

Networks and belief

Trade carried religion only where the receiving state had a use for it

Kilwa on the Swahili coast handled the gold that came from the Zimbabwe plateau, and Kilwa converted to Islam while Great Zimbabwe did not, though both grew wealthy on the same metal moving the same route. In the Sahel and in Melaka the pattern matches Kilwa: rulers converted and acquired literate scribes, a commercial law merchants recognized from Morocco to India, a shared calendar and a diplomatic language. The difference exists because conversion was worth most to a state that needed an administrative apparatus it did not have, and worth least to one whose authority already rested on cattle, kinship and a religious tradition of its own. Trade transmits religion where the receiving state finds it useful and stops where it does not, which is why "Islam spread along trade routes" is a claim about administration as much as about belief.

Causation

Identical shocks produce opposite results when structures differ

The Black Death killed a third to a half of Europe between 1347 and 1351, leaving the same farmland with far fewer workers. In England, France and the Low Countries wages rose, rents collapsed, the Statute of Labourers of 1351 failed to freeze either, and serfdom dissolved within a century. In Poland, Hungary and later Russia the same shortage produced tightened restrictions and a serfdom harsher than the west had ever known. The difference exists because western peasants had somewhere to go, dense towns whose charters freed a serf resident for a year and a day, and neighboring manors competing for labor, while eastern peasants faced landlords with more political leverage over weaker monarchies and no urban network to escape into. Whenever you are asked to explain why one cause produced different effects, look at the structure it landed on before you look for a second cause.
