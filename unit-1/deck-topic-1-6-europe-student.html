AP World History · Unit 1 · Topic 1.6

Developments in Europe

Political, Social, and Religious Structures · c. 1200 to c. 1450

BeHistorical

The Question

Why did Europe never rebuild a Rome, and what filled the space instead?

  • No cash, so lords paid in land
  • No single crown, so the Church filled the gap
  • No king could tax without asking first

Where in the World

A fragmented map, a shared faith

Politically

Kingdoms, nobles, and Church lands, divided

Religiously

One Roman Catholic Church, crossing every border

Economically

Towns and trade growing, especially in Italy and Flanders

Key Takeaway

Fragmented politics. One faith, holding the pieces together anyway.

The Mechanism Behind Everything Today

A state with no cash

  • Almost no tax system and almost no coined money in circulation
  • A ruler who cannot pay in coin pays in land
  • Land for loyalty and service is feudalism
  • The economic system running underneath it is manorialism

Key Takeaway

If you cannot pay a soldier in coin, you pay him in land, and a soldier with land is no longer entirely yours.

Part One

Fragmentation

Land granted for loyalty, and a farm that fed the whole system.

The Political System

One hierarchy, four ranks

The King
Great Lords & Nobles
Granted land for sworn loyalty and military service
Knights
Land re-granted beneath them, on the same terms
Serfs
Work the manor, owing dues and labor for protection

Key Takeaway

A serf is bound to the land. A serf is not a slave, and that difference matters later.

The Economic System

Manorialism: the quiet engine

  • The heavy plow turned the wet clay soils of the north
  • The horse collar made a faster animal usable for plowing
  • The three-field system left only a third of the land idle

Key Takeaway

The manor was not stagnant. It roughly doubled a continent's population before it broke anything.

Part Two

The Church

The institution that did the jobs a state was not there to do.

Case One · An Institution, Not a Building

The one institution every kingdom answered to

The tithe

A tenth of production, the only universal tax

Canon law

Its own courts, its own code

Largest landholder

Monasteries controlled vast estates

Trained the clerks

Latin literacy, for a long time, was the Church's

Key Takeaway

A comparison of European power that skips the Church is measuring the wrong institution.

Part Four

Towns and Trade

A place where a lord's authority stopped, and money outranked land.

Case Three · What a Town Bought

Moving money without moving coin

Part Five

The Fourteenth Century

Famine, plague, and revolt, arriving inside forty years of each other.

The Shock

A cost of connection

1315–17

The Great Famine, ruined harvests

1347

Plague arrives in Sicily, by Genoese ship

⅓ to ½

Of Europe's population, dead within four years

The same network

That carried silk and mathematics westward

Key Takeaway

The plague traveled the same routes as silk and paper. Integration transmits everything, not only what traders intended.

The Mechanism

Before and after the plague, on one manor

Before, c. 1340

Labor is cheap

Land is scarce. Workers are abundant. A lord sets the terms.

After, c. 1360

Labor is dear

Land is abundant. Workers are scarce. A peasant can walk away and be hired.

Key Takeaway

Serfdom faded in the west, and hardened in the east, from the exact same shock.
