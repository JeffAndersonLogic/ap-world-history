Developments in Europe

Political, Social, and Religious Structures · c. 1200 to c. 1450

BeHistorical

The Question

When Rome was gone, who was in charge?

  • Who protects us? Feudalism
  • Who feeds us? Manorialism
  • What do we share? The Church

Where in the World

One empire, two halves, two fates

West, in tan

Collapses, 400s

East, in blue

Survives 1,000 years, from Constantinople

Kept in the east

Roman law · Greek learning · wealth

Key Takeaway

Rome collapsed in the west, survived in the east. Medieval Europe is the western half.

The Problem

What stopped working

Rome provided

Before

An army

Taxes

Courts, written law

Coins

Safe roads

Nobody provided

After

No one comes

No way to pay soldiers

Local courts only

Pay in goods and labor

Trade dies

Key Takeaway

One problem left: nobody could keep you safe.

The Key Word

Centralized power, and decentralized power

Centralized · Song China

ONE government ONE law ONE tax system

One order travels 1,000 miles.

Decentralized · Medieval Europe

Hundreds of lords. Each with his own land, court, soldiers.

Key Takeaway

Decentralization: power in many small local pieces. Not weak. Not gathered.

The Answer

Three problems, three systems

Each needed its own answer.

Feudalism · political Manorialism · economic The Church · social

Part One · Political

Feudalism: land traded for loyalty

No taxes, so no coin. Pay in land instead.

Fief
The land, granted for service
Lord
Grants the fief
Vassal
Receives it. Owes military service
Homage
The public oath. No court above them, so the ceremony was the contract

Key Takeaway

A private deal between two people. Not a law.

Part Two · Economic

Manorialism: the farm underneath the politics

Feudalism: who commands. Manorialism: who eats.

Manor
Fields, village, church, mill, woodland
Demesne
The lord’s own fields. Unpaid labor
Serf
Bound to the manor. Not free to leave
Obligations
Labor, harvest, fees. For land and protection

Key Takeaway

Unfree, but not a slave. Not property. Not sellable.

Close to self-sufficient. What you get when roads are unsafe.

Part Two · Economic

Three technologies

Technology one

The heavy plow

Coulter cuts. Moldboard turns.

Northern clay can be farmed.

Technology two

The horse collar

Sits on the shoulders, not the windpipe.

Pulls hard. Faster than an ox.

Technology three

The three-field system

Autumn grain · spring legumes · one resting.

Land in use: half → two thirds.

Key Takeaway

More food → more people → a surplus. Surplus makes towns.

Part Three

The Church

One institution, crossing hundreds of borders.

Part Three · Social

What made it unifying

One language

The same Latin mass everywhere

The parish

The only institution in the village. Kept the records

Monasteries

Schools, hospitals, books, nearly all literacy

Law & the tithe

Its own courts. A tenth of everything

Key Takeaway

One institution doing a government’s jobs. On a continent with no government.

Part Three · Social

When a king said no

King John refused the pope’s choice of archbishop. The pope did not send an army. He did this instead.

1208

Interdict

The pope closes every church in England.

No weddings. No funerals. No services. For six years.

1209

Excommunication

John is thrown out of the Church.

His nobles are told their oaths to him no longer count.

Key Takeaway

The pope punished an entire country to pressure one man, and it worked.

Part Three · Social

The king gives in

1213

Surrender

John hands England to the pope and gets it back as a fief.

The king of England is now a vassal.

1215

Magna Carta

Nobles force John to sign away some of his power.

The pope cancels it ten weeks later.

Key Takeaway

The pope sent no soldiers. He did not need to. Feudalism ran on oaths, and the Church could cancel them.
