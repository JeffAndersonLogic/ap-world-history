Rice, Water, and the Strait

Two ways to build a state in this region, and the geography chooses for you. Control land and you tax rice, which means water, labor and a temple to justify taking both. Control a strait and you tax everyone else's cargo, which means a harbor, a navy and a reputation merchants trust. This chapter works through both, so the comparison the topic asks for has real machinery behind it.

01

South Asia After the Sultanate Arrived

1206 to 1450  ·  Delhi, the Deccan, and Vijayanagara

India in this period is not one state and never was. It is a Muslim-ruled north, a contested Deccan in the middle, and a Hindu south that consolidated in direct response to being raided, and the interaction between the three is the topic.

The northern state

The Delhi Sultanate, established in 1206 and treated at length in the Topic 1.2 chapter, ruled northern India through a Turkic and Persian Muslim military elite governing an overwhelmingly Hindu population. Its administrative instrument was the iqta, a revocable assignment of a district's revenue to an officer who maintained troops from it, and its fiscal instrument was the jizya, the tax on non-Muslims. It repeatedly threw back Mongol invasions, most successfully around 1300, which is why northern India became a refuge for scholars and artisans fleeing Mongol conquests further west.

Its reach southward was raiding before it was rule. In 1310 and 1311 the general Malik Kafur drove campaigns deep into the far south, and the plunder from those campaigns, temple treasuries above all, financed the sultanate for years. In 1327 Sultan Muhammad bin Tughluq attempted to move his capital from Delhi to Daulatabad in the Deccan, roughly 700 miles away, in order to govern the south properly. He compelled much of Delhi's population to make the journey, the experiment failed, and he moved back. It is the single best example in the unit of an ambition exceeding the administrative capacity available to carry it out.

Vijayanagara: a state built by the invasion it opposed

Vijayanagara was founded in 1336 in the Deccan by two brothers, Harihara and Bukka, who had served the sultanate before establishing an independent Hindu kingdom in the south. Its rival, the Bahmani Sultanate, broke away from Delhi in 1347, and the two fought over the fertile land between the Krishna and Tungabhadra rivers for two centuries.

The state's foundations were water and horses. Its capital, on the Tungabhadra, sat in a dry granite landscape and was made viable by an enormous system of dams, canals and reservoirs that watered the surrounding fields; the kings taxed the irrigated crop and endowed temples out of the proceeds. Its weakness was cavalry, because India does not breed good warhorses in quantity, so Vijayanagara imported them by sea from Arabia and Persia at ruinous prices, which tied a land empire's military strength to its access to the coast.

Temple patronage was politics. A king who rebuilt or endowed a great temple bound the priesthood, the local landholders and the pilgrims who came to it into a relationship with his throne, and the temple in turn became a landholder, an employer, a bank and a school. When a checkpoint asks how a state used religion to build power, Vijayanagara's temple endowments are the cleanest South Asian example available.

How we know: outsiders described the capital, and they were impressed

Two foreign visitors left descriptions of Vijayanagara in this period: Nicolo de' Conti, a Venetian merchant, in the 1420s, and Abd al-Razzaq, an envoy from the Timurid court in Persia, in 1443. Abd al-Razzaq describes a city of seven concentric walls, bazaars of jewelers selling openly, and a court of extraordinary wealth. Both men were strangers with reasons to exaggerate a marvel, and both are describing something real: the ruins at the site cover roughly sixteen square miles, and the irrigation works they mention are still visible. Use the archaeology as your evidence and the travelers as color.

Strongest tool, with its mechanism
Temple endowment as statecraft, in Vijayanagara. The mechanism is that a king who funds a temple acquires the priesthood, the pilgrims and the landholders around it as clients, while the temple itself becomes a landowner, employer and lender that redistributes the state's wealth locally in the king's name.
Best evidence of exclusion or limit
Muhammad bin Tughluq's forced move to Daulatabad in 1327 and back is the best available evidence that these states could conquer distances they could not administer.
Comparison hook
Against Song China on ruling at distance: the Song governed a subcontinent-sized realm through examined salaried officials on paper records, while the Delhi Sultanate assigned revenue districts to soldiers and hoped they stayed loyal. Salaried administrators versus armed revenue-holders is the same choice European monarchs faced, and India got the same answer Europe did.

Rajput
The warrior clans of northwestern India whose fortified kingdoms resisted, allied with, and were eventually absorbed by the northern sultanates.
02

Belief and the Social Order in South Asia

c. 1100 to 1450  ·  Bhakti, Sufi, and the caste order

The caste system is the deepest continuity in South Asian history, and this period produced the most serious devotional challenge to it, from two religions at once, with the challengers ending up remarkably similar to each other.

What caste actually is

Two words, and they are not synonyms. Varna is the ancient fourfold scheme of Brahmins, warriors and rulers, merchants and farmers, and laborers, with some communities placed outside the scheme altogether and subjected to the exclusions later called untouchability. Jati is the thing people actually lived in: thousands of local birth-groups, each tied to an occupation, each marrying within itself, each with a place in the local hierarchy that could differ from village to village. Varna is the theory; jati is the practice, and confusing them is why so many essays about caste go vague.

What makes it a durable system of power rather than merely a ranking is that it is enforced without a state. Purity rules govern who may eat with, marry, or touch whom; occupation is inherited; and the sanction for breaking the rules is not a court but expulsion by your own community, which in a village economy is ruin. A Muslim sultanate could sit on top of this for three hundred years without altering it, because its everyday enforcement ran through families, marriage networks and village institutions rather than through anything the state had to do.

Bhakti: devotion that needs no priest

The Bhakti movement, which spread north from southern India across these centuries, taught that what God wants is love and personal devotion, and that ritual, Sanskrit learning and priestly mediation are not required to offer it. The consequences follow immediately. Bhakti poets composed in vernacular languages that ordinary people spoke rather than in the Sanskrit of an elite literary tradition most people could not read: Marathi, Kannada, Tamil, Hindi, Kashmiri. Devotion was open to people of any jati, and to women, and the movement produced women poets whose verse is still sung.

Basava, in twelfth-century Karnataka, rejected caste distinction and temple ritual outright and founded a community that would not observe them. Ramanuja, in the same century, gave devotional worship a philosophical foundation. In fourteenth-century Kashmir, the poet Lal Ded composed verses in Kashmiri that Hindus and Muslims both preserved and recited. It is a genuine social challenge and it should be treated as one, with a caution attached: caste survived the challenge everywhere, and most Bhakti communities eventually settled into being jatis of their own.

Sufism, and how close the two got

Sufi orders arrived with and after the sultanate, and the Chishti order became the most influential in India. Muin al-Din Chishti settled at Ajmer, where he died in 1236, and his tomb became a pilgrimage site visited by Hindus as well as Muslims, which it remains. Nizamuddin Auliya at Delhi, who died in 1325, kept a distance from the sultan's court that was part of his authority, and the poet Amir Khusrau in his circle wrote in Persian and in the local vernacular and helped shape the devotional music of the subcontinent.

Set Bhakti and Sufism side by side and the resemblance is the historical point. Both prized personal ecstatic devotion over legal formality, both used music and poetry, both formed around charismatic teachers rather than around a formal clergy, although Sufi orders grew substantial institutions of their own, with lodges, teaching lineages and shrines, both used the vernacular, and both drew followers from across the social order. The shared shrines, shared saints and shared poetry that resulted are the best evidence of syncretism in the unit, and the fact that the same religious form emerged inside two different religions at the same time is a much more interesting claim than "the religions influenced each other."

Common mistake to avoid

Buddhism was not a major religion in India in this period, and writing about "Hindus and Buddhists in the Delhi Sultanate" is an error. Buddhism had been declining in India for centuries as devotional Hinduism absorbed its appeal and its monasteries lost patronage, and the great monastic university at Nalanda was destroyed around 1193. By 1200 Buddhism had lost most of its institutional base in the land of its birth, its great monasteries destroyed or unfunded, although Buddhist communities and pilgrimage sites persisted. It was simultaneously flourishing in Sri Lanka, becoming the state religion across mainland Southeast Asia, and dominant in Tibet, China, Korea and Japan. That is a change-over-time answer ready to use: the religion did not decline, it relocated.

Caste as self-enforcing order. The ancient fourfold ranking of Brahmins, warriors, merchants and laborers, with an excluded fifth group; the theory of the caste order.

Syncretism
The blending of religious traditions, visible in South Asia in shared shrines, shared saints and the closeness of Bhakti and Sufi devotion.

The Sea States: Taxing Everybody Else’s Cargo

c. 700 to 1450  ·  Srivijaya, Majapahit, Melaka

A maritime state does not need much territory. It needs a strait nobody can avoid, a harbor merchants prefer, and enough force to make skipping it expensive, and everything else about how it governs follows from that.

The monsoon, which is the engine

The monsoon winds reverse twice a year with great reliability. From roughly April to September they blow from the southwest, carrying ships from East Africa and Arabia toward India and onward east. From roughly November to March they blow from the northeast, carrying them home. A captain who understood the pattern could plan a round trip years in advance, which made Indian Ocean voyaging predictable in a way few long-distance routes were: a merchant could commit capital to a voyage already knowing the season the wind would bring it back.

Now notice the consequence that produces states. If you arrive on one monsoon and cannot leave until the other, you are in a foreign port for months. Merchants therefore did not simply visit; they lodged, warehoused goods, hired agents, learned the language, married locally and left resident relatives behind to handle business between voyages. That is how permanent Arab, Persian, Gujarati, Tamil and Chinese communities appeared in ports all around the ocean, and it is the mechanism behind the spread of Islam to Southeast Asia. A ruler who wanted that trade had to make foreigners welcome, secure and legally protected, which is a completely different governing problem from taxing peasants.

Srivijaya and the chokepoint

Srivijaya, centered on Palembang in Sumatra from the seventh century, dominated the Strait of Melaka, the narrow passage between Sumatra and the Malay peninsula through which most traffic between the Indian Ocean and the South China Sea had to pass, holding it through a network of allied and subordinate ports rather than as territory. It taxed that traffic, suppressed the piracy that would have driven it away, and used the revenue to buy the loyalty of coastal chiefs who might otherwise have raided the ships themselves. It was also a major center of Buddhist learning, wealthy enough that Chinese monks stopped there to study Sanskrit on the way to India.

Its weaknesses are instructive. A state whose income is a toll has no depth: a rival port that undercuts you, or a raid on your capital, takes the revenue directly. Chola naval raids from southern India in 1025 badly damaged Srivijayan dominance, and it declined thereafter into one Sumatran power among several.

Majapahit and Melaka

Majapahit, founded on Java in 1293, is the most influential Javanese maritime power of the fourteenth century, and it had something Srivijaya lacked: Java's rice. A Javanese state could feed itself and field an army from its own fields, then use naval power to dominate the spice-producing islands to the east and the ports along the routes. Under Hayam Wuruk and his formidable chief minister Gajah Mada in the fourteenth century, Majapahit claimed authority over most of the archipelago. It was Hindu-Buddhist in its court religion, at a moment when the ports around it were converting to Islam.

How we know: a court poem is a claim, not a map

Almost everything textbooks say about Majapahit's extent comes from the Nagarakretagama, a Javanese court poem of 1365 praising the king, which lists ninety-eight tributary territories. It is a panegyric written to flatter a living ruler by a poet in his service, and "tributary" in it may mean anything from genuine subordination to a single exchange of gifts. Historians accept that Majapahit was the dominant power of the archipelago and treat the list as an assertion of prestige rather than as an inventory. When you cite Majapahit's extent, say what the source is; that one sentence is worth more than the confident number.

Melaka, founded around 1400 by a prince from Palembang, is the model case and belongs in every answer about this region. It had a superb deep harbor at the narrowest part of the strait, and its rulers built the rest deliberately: predictable low duties, warehouses, a harbor master for each major merchant community who settled disputes in their own language, suppression of piracy, and conversion to Islam, which plugged the port into the Muslim commercial network that ran the western half of the ocean. It also secured Ming Chinese recognition, and the treasure fleets of Zheng He called there. Within decades it was the greatest entrepot in Southeast Asia, and it had no agricultural hinterland worth mentioning. A port is a policy.

Strongest tool, with its mechanism
Chokepoint taxation. The mechanism is that a strait no ship can avoid converts geography into revenue, but only if the state also suppresses piracy, keeps duties predictable and makes resident foreign merchants secure, because a toll they can dodge by using a rival port is not a toll.
Best evidence of exclusion or limit
A state living on tolls has no depth. One naval raid, as the Cholas made on Srivijaya in 1025, or one better-run rival port, removes the entire income at once.
Comparison hook
Against the Swahili coast in Topic 1.5: Kilwa and Melaka are the same institution on opposite sides of the ocean, cosmopolitan Muslim port cities living on transit trade with small hinterlands. Against Vijayanagara: land states tax the harvest and need water; sea states tax the cargo and need a harbor.

Terms to use precisely

Monsoon
The seasonally reversing winds of the Indian Ocean, which made long-distance voyages predictable and forced merchants to reside for months in foreign ports.
Srivijaya
The Buddhist maritime state on Sumatra that taxed traffic through the Strait of Melaka from the seventh century until Chola raids broke its dominance after 1025.
Majapahit
The Hindu-Buddhist Javanese empire founded in 1293, which combined Java’s rice surplus with naval power to dominate the archipelago.
Melaka
The port founded around 1400 whose deliberate policies, low duties, harbor masters, anti-piracy patrols and conversion to Islam, made it the region’s greatest entrepot.
Entrepot
A port where goods are landed, stored and re-exported rather than consumed, living on transit rather than on production.

The Land States: Angkor and Its Water

c. 900 to 1450  ·  Angkor, Pagan, Sukhothai, Ayutthaya

Mainland Southeast Asia produced states built on rice, and the largest of them built the most ambitious water management system in the pre-modern world. Then the rain stopped being reliable.

What the Khmer built

The Khmer Empire, centered on Angkor in what is now Cambodia, dominated mainland Southeast Asia from the ninth century to the fifteenth. Its wealth came from rice, and its rice came from an engineered landscape: enormous rectangular reservoirs called barays, the largest of them about five miles long, fed by canals and rivers, holding the wet-season flood and releasing it through the dry months. Whether the barays primarily irrigated fields or primarily served ritual and urban water supply is a live argument among specialists, and both functions mattered.

Angkor Wat, built under Suryavarman II in the twelfth century, is the largest religious monument on earth, dedicated to Vishnu and aligned to the solstice. Jayavarman VII, who ruled from about 1181, converted the state's patronage to Mahayana Buddhism, built the Bayon with its great carved faces, and constructed rest houses and hospitals along the roads. That switch is worth noticing: the same state apparatus was used to promote first a Hindu and then a Buddhist royal cult, which tells you the religion was serving the kingship rather than the reverse.

How we know: a Chinese envoy and a laser

Two sources carry most of what we know, and they are three centuries and a technology apart. Zhou Daguan, a Chinese envoy, lived at Angkor in 1296 and 1297 and wrote the only surviving eyewitness account: the palace, the markets, the festivals, and the observation that Khmer women conducted much of the trade. Then from 2012 onward, airborne laser scanning stripped away the forest canopy digitally and revealed that the temples sat inside a low-density urban sprawl of roads, ponds, canals and house mounds covering hundreds of square miles. The temples were always visible; the city around them was not, and no historian knew its scale until a laser flew over it.

Theravada Buddhism and the states it legitimized

Across mainland Southeast Asia in these centuries, Theravada Buddhism, transmitted largely from Sri Lanka, displaced the older Hindu and Mahayana court cults. Pagan in Burma built thousands of temples in its plain before Mongol pressure ended it in the late thirteenth century. The Thai state of Sukhothai emerged in 1238, and Ayutthaya, founded in 1351, grew into the dominant power of the mainland.

The political mechanism is precise and worth having. Theravada teaches that merit accumulated through good acts determines one's condition in this life and the next. A king who builds temples, endows monasteries and feeds the monastic community is visibly the greatest maker of merit in the realm, which explains his position: he rules because his merit is greatest, and his merit is greatest because he rules. Meanwhile the monastic community provides the only widespread literacy and schooling in the country and depends on royal support to exist. It is a cheaper legitimacy machine than a bureaucracy and it does not need one.

How Angkor ended

The standard old explanation was the sack of Angkor by Ayutthaya in 1431. That happened, and it is not enough, because states recover from sacks. The current picture is a compound failure. Tree-ring records from the region show decades-long droughts in the middle of the fourteenth century and again in the early fifteenth, punctuated by unusually intense monsoons. A water system engineered for a reliable annual cycle is exactly what fails hardest when the cycle becomes unreliable: canals silt up in drought, then flood damage tears out embankments that were never designed for that much water at once, and repairing a network that size demands a labor mobilization the state can only manage when the harvests are good.

Add long wars with Ayutthaya, add the pull of the coastal ports where maritime trade was making a new kind of wealth, and the picture is of a capital that became too expensive to maintain rather than one that was destroyed. The Khmer court moved southeast toward the river trade near modern Phnom Penh. This is one of the best "multiple causation" answers available anywhere in the course: climate, warfare, economic shift and infrastructure failure, each insufficient alone.

Strongest tool, with its mechanism
Hydraulic engineering plus a royal cult, at Angkor. The mechanism is that barays and canals stored the wet-season flood and released it through the dry months, which multiplied the rice harvest and therefore the labor the state could command, while the temple complex the same labor built made the king the earthly guarantee of that order.
Best evidence of exclusion or limit
A system tuned to a reliable monsoon is fragile precisely when the monsoon stops being reliable, and the fourteenth-century droughts are the evidence. The 1431 sack was the last blow, not the cause.
Comparison hook
Against Melaka in the same region: one state taxed water it stored, the other taxed ships that passed. Against Song China: both used enormous state hydraulic works to raise the harvest, but the Song had an examined bureaucracy to maintain them and Angkor had corvee labor mobilized by a god-king, which is why one recovered from disaster and the other did not.

Religion as Governance

c. 1200 to 1450  ·  Temple, monastery, mosque

In this region religion is not the background to state building, it is the instrument. A temple is a landholder, a monastery is a school system, and a mosque in a harbor is a commercial treaty, and each did work no state in the region could otherwise afford.

The temple as an economic institution

A great South Asian temple owned land, sometimes many villages of it. It employed priests, cooks, musicians, dancers, accountants, guards and laborers. It received endowments in gold, cattle and rice, lent at interest, stored grain against famine, and redistributed food to pilgrims. When a Vijayanagara king endowed a temple, he was not only performing devotion. He was routing state revenue into an institution that would spend it locally in his name, employ people who would remember why they were employed, and give the priesthood a financial reason to bless his rule.

That is also why temple treasuries were the target of raids from the north. Plundering a temple was not merely an act of religious hostility, though it was often also that; it was the fastest way to seize a region's accumulated capital, because the temple was where the capital was.

The monastery as an administration the state did not have to build

In mainland Southeast Asia the monastic community performed functions that in Song China required a salaried bureaucracy. Monasteries taught boys to read, kept records, mediated disputes, sheltered travelers and served as the visible moral authority in every village. They cost the state donations rather than salaries, and they made the king the patron of literacy without requiring him to administer it. A student comparing state capacity across regions should notice that Angkor and Ayutthaya achieved a level of social organization with monks that China achieved with officials, and that this is one of the more elegant comparisons the unit offers.

The mosque in the harbor

In the port cities the calculation was different again. A ruler who converted to Islam and built a mosque was signaling to every Muslim merchant in the ocean that his port had a judge who would apply a law they recognized, that their contracts would be enforceable, that they could marry, worship and be buried properly, and that their partners in Gujarat or Aden could deal with his port on familiar terms. Melaka is the clearest case, and the pattern repeats along the north Javanese coast and in the Philippines. The conversion is sincere and it is also commercial infrastructure, and treating those as alternatives misunderstands how religion worked in a trading port.

One regional feature is worth carrying into any comparison question about gender. Across much of Southeast Asia, kinship was reckoned through both parents rather than only the father, daughters as well as sons inherited, and women conducted a great deal of the market trade, which is exactly what Zhou Daguan noticed at Angkor and what visitors to the archipelago's ports kept remarking on. Compared with the caste-bound households of South Asia, the Neo-Confucian order of Song China, or the manors of Europe, Southeast Asian women in this period held noticeably more economic autonomy, and the incoming world religions modified that pattern only gradually.

Use this in your answer
Strongest tool, with its mechanism
Religious institutions as state infrastructure. The mechanism is that a temple, a monastery or a harbor mosque performs functions a state would otherwise have to build and pay for: landholding and local redistribution, literacy and dispute resolution, and enforceable commercial law for foreign merchants. Each is cheaper than the bureaucracy that would be needed to replace it.
Best evidence of exclusion or limit
Institutions that strong are also independent. Temple treasuries invited plunder, and a monastic community with the moral authority to legitimize a king can withhold that legitimacy.
Comparison hook
Against Europe: the Latin Church did all three jobs at once and became a rival to kings, while in Southeast Asia the sangha depended on royal donation and stayed a support rather than a competitor. Against Dar al-Islam: the waqf endowment and the temple endowment are the same instrument, a permanent charitable foundation funding public institutions in a donor's name.

Terms to use precisely

Temple economy
The system by which a great temple held land, employed hundreds, lent, stored grain and redistributed wealth, making religious endowment a form of state spending.
Sangha
The Buddhist monastic community, which in mainland Southeast Asia supplied literacy, schooling and moral authority in exchange for royal patronage.
Devaraja
The god-king cult of the Khmer court, in which the ruler was identified with a deity and the temple complex was the visible proof of it.
Harbor master
The official appointed for each resident merchant community in a port such as Melaka to settle disputes in that community’s own language and law.
Bilateral kinship
Reckoning descent and inheritance through both parents, common in Southeast Asia, which underlies the region’s comparatively strong position for women.

Building an Answer That Scores

Each card is a complete comparison: the claim, the specific evidence, and the reason the difference existed. That last part is the one graders are looking for and the one students leave out. Take the shape and fill it with whichever two states your prompt names.

State building

Land states needed water; sea states needed a strait

Vijayanagara built dams and reservoirs in a dry granite landscape and taxed the irrigated harvest; the Khmer built barays five miles long and taxed the rice they made possible. Srivijaya and Melaka held narrow passages that shipping could not avoid and taxed other people's cargo, and Melaka had almost no farmland at all. The difference exists because a revenue source dictates a state's shape: taxing a harvest requires controlling land, water and labor, which means an army, a corvee and a temple to justify both, while taxing transit requires a harbor, an anti-piracy patrol and a reputation for fair dealing, which means diplomacy and law rather than mass mobilization.

Belief and rule

Religion did different jobs in India, on the mainland, and in the ports

In Vijayanagara, temple endowment bound priests, landholders and pilgrims to the throne and turned the temple into a landowner and lender. In Angkor and Ayutthaya, Theravada merit-making made the king the realm's greatest patron while monasteries supplied the literacy a bureaucracy would otherwise have had to provide. In Melaka, conversion to Islam gave foreign merchants a law they recognized and plugged the port into the ocean's dominant commercial network. The difference exists because each state needed something different from religion: legitimacy over a settled agrarian population, administration on the cheap, or the confidence of foreigners it could not compel.

Continuity and change

Buddhism did not decline, it relocated

By 1200 Buddhism had effectively vanished from India, its birthplace, absorbed by devotional Hinduism and stripped of patronage, with Nalanda destroyed around 1193. In the same centuries Theravada Buddhism, carried from Sri Lanka, became the state religion of Burma, Cambodia and the Thai kingdoms, and Mahayana remained dominant across East Asia. The difference exists because a religion in this period lived on patronage: in India, kings and merchants shifted their giving to temples and Bhakti devotion, while mainland Southeast Asian kings found in Theravada a legitimacy machine that fit exactly what they needed. Religions follow their funding.

Networks

The monsoon explains conversion better than conquest does

The winds reverse twice a year, so a merchant who arrived on one monsoon waited months for the other, which turned visits into residence, residence into intermarriage, and intermarriage into permanent foreign communities in every major port. That is why Islam reached the ports of Sumatra, Java and the Malay peninsula centuries before any Muslim army did, and why conversion there began at the coast and moved inland slowly or not at all. Whenever a prompt asks how a religion or a technology crossed the Indian Ocean, name the wind, name the wait, and name what the wait produced.
